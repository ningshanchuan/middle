Mount Tai. Mount Tai in Shandong is 1,545 m high. Emperors climbed it to honour Heaven, and Du Fu dreamed of standing on its summit: “一览众山小”.
Qi and Lu. Today: Shandong. Qi lay north of Mount Tai and Lu (Confucius’s home state) to the south. Even today Shandong is called 齐鲁 for short.
Juyan. Today: Ejin Banner, Inner Mongolia. An oasis at the end of the Heihe River, in the desert far to the northwest. Han soldiers guarded it, and more than 10,000 of their wooden writing slips have been dug up here.
Xiao Pass. Today: southeast of Guyuan, Ningxia. A pass guarding the way from the northwest into the Chang’an plain. In Wang Wei’s poem he meets a mounted scout here.
Mount Yanran. Today: the Khangai Mountains, Mongolia. In 89 CE the Han general Dou Xian beat the Xiongnu and carved a record of victory on a cliff — 勒石燕然. Archaeologists found that inscription in Mongolia in 2017.
Yellow Crane Tower. Today: Snake Hill, Wuchang, Wuhan. A tower on Snake Hill above the Yangtze, first built about 1,800 years ago. Legend says an immortal flew away from it on a yellow crane.
Hanyang. Today: Hanyang District, Wuhan. Hanyang lies across the Yangtze from the Yellow Crane Tower. Its Qingchuan Pavilion (晴川阁) is named after Cui Hao’s line about the sunlit river.
Parrot Isle. Today: Hanyang, Wuhan. A grassy island in the Yangtze, named after a Han-dynasty poem about a parrot. The old island was washed away; a new sandbank by Hanyang took its name.
Yueyang Tower. Today: Yueyang, Hunan. A three-storey wooden tower on the city wall above Dongting Lake. Fan Zhongyan wrote “先天下之忧而忧，后天下之乐而乐” for it — without ever climbing it himself.
Prince Teng’s Pavilion. Today: Nanchang, Jiangxi. One of the “three great towers south of the Yangtze”, with the Yellow Crane Tower and Yueyang Tower. The young poet Wang Bo made it famous.
Beigu Mountain. Today: Zhenjiang, Jiangsu. A hill on the south bank of the Yangtze at Zhenjiang, where the river widens towards the sea. Wang Wan’s boat stopped below it at the turn of the year.
West Lake. Today: Hangzhou, Zhejiang. Bai Juyi (822–824) and Su Shi (1089–1091) both governed Hangzhou, wrote poems about the lake and built dykes — the Bai and Su Causeways carry their names.
Gushan Island. Today: West Lake, Hangzhou. The biggest island in West Lake, a small hill joined to the shore by the Bai Causeway. The Tang temple is gone; today there are gardens and a museum.
Bai Causeway. Today: West Lake, Hangzhou. A 1 km dyke lined with willows, from the Broken Bridge to Gushan. It was already there in Bai Juyi’s time; later people named it after him because he loved it so much.
Su Causeway. Today: West Lake, Hangzhou. Su Shi dug the weeds and mud out of West Lake and piled them into this 2.8 km causeway with six bridges. “Spring dawn on the Su Causeway” is one of the lake’s Ten Views.
Longbiao. Today: Hongjiang, Hunan. A remote town in the hills of western Hunan. When Li Bai heard his friend Wang Changling was sent here, he “sent his worried heart to the moon”.
Five Streams. Today: western Hunan and eastern Guizhou. Five mountain streams that flow into the Yuan River. Crossing all five shows how far from home Wang Changling had to go.
Yelang. Today: the western Hunan – eastern Guizhou area. Yelang was an ancient kingdom in the southwest (the idiom 夜郎自大 comes from it); the Tang county of Yelang lay near Longbiao. Li Bai’s moon will follow his friend even “west of Yelang”.
Du Fu Thatched Cottage. Today: Chengdu, Sichuan. After years of war Du Fu built a thatched hut here and wrote about 240 poems, including the one about the autumn wind tearing off his roof (茅屋为秋风所破歌).
Mizhou. Today: Zhucheng, Shandong. Here Su Shi led the hunt of 《江城子·密州出猎》 (1075) and, on the Chaoran Terrace he rebuilt, wrote 《水调歌头》 for his brother at Mid-Autumn 1076.
Yanzhou. Today: Yan’an, Shaanxi. A Song fortress city on the Loess Plateau facing the Western Xia. Fan Zhongyan’s 《渔家傲·秋思》 shows the soldiers’ lonely autumn here.
Huiyan Peak, Hengyang. Today: Hengyang, Hunan. The first of the 72 peaks of Mount Heng. Legend says wild geese flying south go no further — so in 衡阳雁去 the geese go home, but the soldiers cannot.
Yunzhong. Today: around Togtoh, Inner Mongolia. The general Wei Shang guarded Yunzhong against the Xiongnu; the emperor sent Feng Tang to pardon him. Su Shi’s “持节云中” hopes the court will call him back the same way.
Dongpo Red Cliff, Huangzhou. Today: Huanggang, Hubei. Su Shi wrote 《念奴娇·赤壁怀古》, the two Red Cliff rhapsodies and 《记承天寺夜游》 here. This is not the battlefield of 208 (that is in today’s Chibi City, upstream) — Su Shi himself wrote “人道是” (people say…).
Zhangqiu, Jinan. Today: Zhangqiu District, Jinan, Shandong. Li Qingzhao, the greatest woman poet of the Song, grew up here among springs, streams and lotus ponds — like the lotus flowers she rowed into in 《如梦令》.
Wuling. Today: Changde, Hunan. The fisherman of 《桃花源记》 came from Wuling, by the Yuan River. Tao Yuanming chose a real place to start an imaginary journey.
Peach Blossom Spring (scenic area). Today: Taoyuan County, Changde, Hunan. The Peach Blossom Spring of the story is imaginary — nobody could find it again. This scenic area in Taoyuan County was named after it, and people have built temples and pavilions here for over a thousand years.
Nanyang. Today: Nanyang, Henan. An old city in Henan: the hermit Liu Ziji of 《桃花源记》 came from here, and Zhuge Liang farmed here before Liu Bei visited him three times (三顾茅庐).
Yongzhou. Today: Yongzhou, Hunan. A remote place in southern Hunan in the Tang. During ten years of exile Liu Zongyuan explored its hills and streams and wrote the “Eight Records of Yongzhou” (永州八记).
Little Stone Pool. Today: Lingling, Yongzhou, Hunan. A small pool on the Yu Stream (愚溪) west of old Yongzhou, with a rock bottom and fish that seem to “float in the air”. A small pool there is shown to visitors as the one in the essay.
Xiao and Xiang Rivers. Today: where the Xiao meets the Xiang, Yongzhou, Hunan. The Xiao River joins the Xiang at Yongzhou; the Xiang then flows north into Dongting Lake. In poems 潇湘 means the lands of southern Hunan — “南极潇湘”.
Baling (Yueyang). Today: Yueyang, Hunan. A city where Dongting Lake meets the Yangtze. Demoted official Teng Zijing governed it so well that “政通人和” — and rebuilt its tower.
Junshan Island. Today: Dongting Lake, Yueyang, Hunan. A small island in Dongting Lake opposite Yueyang Tower — the “distant mountain” the lake holds in its mouth. Liu Yuxi compared it to a green snail on a silver plate.
Chuzhou. Today: Chuzhou, Anhui. “环滁皆山也” — the city is ringed by hills. Ouyang Xiu, demoted here at about 40, called himself the Old Drunkard and shared his joy with the people.
Mount Langya. Today: southwest of Chuzhou, Anhui. Wooded hills just southwest of Chuzhou — “蔚然而深秀者，琅琊也”. The Niang Spring and the Old Drunkard’s Pavilion are in its valleys.
Old Drunkard’s Pavilion. Today: Mount Langya, Chuzhou, Anhui. The monk Zhixian built it and Ouyang Xiu named it after himself. With its wing-like roof corners (翼然) it is one of China’s four famous pavilions.
Luling. Today: Ji’an, Jiangxi. Ouyang Xiu’s family came from Luling, so he signed 《醉翁亭记》 “庐陵欧阳修也”. The Southern Song hero Wen Tianxiang was from here too.
Liuzhou. Today: Liuzhou, Guangxi. After Yongzhou, Liu Zongyuan was sent even further south to Liuzhou, where he dug wells, freed bond-servants and died. People still call him 柳柳州.
Humble Room. Today: He County, Anhui. Tradition says Liu Yuxi wrote 《陋室铭》 while governor of Hezhou, where the town gave him only a tiny room. A rebuilt “humble room” stands in He County today.
Zhuge Liang’s Hut, Nanyang. Today: Wolonggang, Nanyang, Henan. Zhuge Liang lived in a thatched hut until Liu Bei came three times to ask for his help. Both Nanyang (Henan) and Longzhong near Xiangyang (Hubei) claim the hut.
Ziyun Pavilion. Today: Xishan Park, Mianyang, Sichuan. Yang Xiong, a great Han writer and scholar, studied in a simple pavilion in Shu (Sichuan). Liu Yuxi pairs it with Zhuge Liang’s hut: famous people lived in humble homes too.
Mount Lu. Today: Jiujiang, Jiangxi. A misty mountain by the Yangtze and Poyang Lake. Li Bai’s waterfall “飞流直下三千尺” and Su Shi’s “不识庐山真面目” were both written about it.
Hundred-Plant Garden & Three-Flavour Study. Today: Lu Xun’s Native Place, Shaoxing, Zhejiang. The garden behind Lu Xun’s family home and, across a little river, the old private school where he studied. Both can still be visited — compare them with his essay!
Shaoxing. Today: Shaoxing, Zhejiang. A water town of canals and stone bridges south of Hangzhou. Lu Xun was born here, and many of his stories are set in places like it; Wang Xizhi’s Orchid Pavilion is nearby.
Aiwan Pavilion. Today: Mount Yuelu, Changsha, Hunan. A pavilion among maple trees on Mount Yuelu. Its name comes from Du Mu’s line “停车坐爱枫林晚” — every autumn the leaves turn red.
Pukou Railway Station. Today: Pukou, Nanjing, Jiangsu. The southern end of the Tianjin–Pukou railway, on the north bank of the Yangtze opposite Nanjing. Here Zhu Ziqing’s father climbed across the tracks to buy him oranges.
Mount Huang. Today: Huangshan, Anhui. Granite peaks famous for strange pines, odd rocks, seas of cloud and hot springs. The highest, Lotus Peak, is 1,864 m.
Stork Tower. Today: Yongji, Shanxi. A tower by the Yellow River where storks used to nest. From the top Wang Zhihuan saw “白日依山尽，黄河入海流”; the old tower was lost in war and rebuilt in 2002.
Vulture Peak. Today: near Rajgir, Bihar, India. In the novel Lingshan is Buddha’s magic mountain. It is based on a real hill in India where the Buddha preached; the real Xuanzang studied at Nalanda nearby.
Liangshan Marsh. Today: Liangshan County, Shandong. In the Song, Yellow River floods made a huge marsh around Mount Liang — a perfect hide-out for outlaws. Most of it is farmland now; Dongping Lake is what is left of the water.
Jingyang Ridge. Today: Yanggu County, Shandong. A low ridge in western Shandong where, in the novel, Wu Song kills a tiger with his bare hands after 18 bowls of wine. Today it is a park with a tiger statue.
Cangzhou. Today: Cangzhou, Hebei. In the Song, criminals were sent to this remote coastal land. It is famous for martial arts and for a 40-tonne Iron Lion cast in 953.
The Harmas (Fabre’s garden). Today: Sérignan-du-Comtat, France. A stony, weedy garden in Provence that Jean-Henri Fabre bought so he could watch insects at home. He wrote most of The Insect World (《昆虫记》) here.
Humble Administrator’s Garden. Today: Suzhou, Jiangsu. The largest classical garden in Suzhou, about 5.2 ha — half of it water. Paths, bridges and windows keep changing the view at every step (移步换景).
Lingering Garden. Today: Suzhou, Jiangsu. Famous for its long corridors, framed windows and the 6.5 m Cloud-Capped Peak, a giant Taihu rock.
Master of the Nets Garden. Today: Suzhou, Jiangsu. One of the smallest famous gardens (about 0.5 ha), arranged around one pond. A copy of one of its courtyards stands in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.
Lion Grove Garden. Today: Suzhou, Jiangsu. Its rockery is a maze of Taihu rocks shaped like lions, with tunnels to get lost in. In the 20th century it belonged to the family of the architect I. M. Pei.
Arecibo Observatory. Today: Puerto Rico, USA. A 305 m radio dish hung in a sinkhole in Puerto Rico — the world’s largest for 53 years, until FAST. It collapsed in 2020.
Guozijian (Imperial Academy), Beijing. Today: Dongcheng, Beijing. Next to Beijing’s Confucius Temple, students of the Imperial Academy learned the Analects by heart for the exams. Today it is a museum.
Chang’an. Today: Xi’an, Shaanxi. Under the Tang it was one of the biggest cities in the world, with about a million people. The Silk Road started here.
Luoyang. Thirteen dynasties made Luoyang their capital. Near the city, the Longmen Grottoes hold about 100,000 Buddhist statues carved into the cliffs.
Bianjing (Kaifeng). Today: Kaifeng, Henan. A busy trading city with night markets and shops open late — you can see it in the famous scroll “Along the River During the Qingming Festival”.
Lin’an. Today: Hangzhou, Zhejiang. After 1127 the Song court moved south to Lin’an. Its West Lake has inspired poets for more than a thousand years.
Forbidden City. 24 Ming and Qing emperors lived here. It covers about 720,000 m² — the largest and most complete ancient palace complex still standing in the world, held together by wooden joints instead of nails.
Grand Canal. The longest canal of the ancient world. Grain boats sailed north on it to feed the capital. The Yuan “straightened” it to run from Hangzhou straight to Dadu (Beijing), about 1,800 km. It became a World Heritage Site in 2014.
Huai’an. Here the canal met the Huai River, so every grain boat passed through. In 984 Qiao Weiyue built a lock with two gates nearby — one of the earliest locks of this kind in the world.
Nanwang. Today: Wenshang County, Shandong. Ming engineers dammed the Wen River and led it here, where the water split — people said “seven parts go north to the emperor, three parts go south”. It fed the locks on both slopes.
Jining. In the Ming and Qing the office that ran the whole Grand Canal was based here, so Jining was called the “Capital of the Canal”.
Linqing. Where the canal met the Wei River (卫河), Linqing became one of the busiest trading cities of the Ming. Many bricks of Beijing’s Forbidden City were fired here and shipped north on the canal.
Tongzhou. Today: Tongzhou District, Beijing. Grain boats from the south unloaded here at the northern end of the canal. Today Tongzhou is Beijing’s new sub-centre for city government offices.
Tonghui River. Guo Shoujing dug this canal from Tongzhou into the capital Dadu, so grain boats could sail right into the city. Kublai Khan named it “Tonghui”.
Li River. The Lingqu Canal joined the Li River (Pearl River system) to the Xiang River (Yangtze system). Its karst hills near Guilin are printed on the back of the 20-yuan note.
Hemudu. Today: Yuyao, Zhejiang. About 7,000 years ago people here grew rice, lived in stilt houses and already joined timbers with mortise-and-tenon joints.
Gongchen Bridge. This three-arch stone bridge marks the southern end of the Beijing–Hangzhou Grand Canal.
Zhenjiang. Canal boats crossed the Yangtze here, with Yangzhou on the far bank. The Jianbi ship lock still links the canal and the river today.
Erie Canal. It linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and New York City, about 580 km long with 83 locks — they work just like the locks on China’s Grand Canal.
Longxi. Today: Gansu. In 138 a dragon on Zhang Heng’s seismoscope in Luoyang dropped its ball, though nobody felt anything. Days later a messenger arrived: an earthquake had hit Longxi, hundreds of kilometres away.
Purple Mountain Observatory. China’s first modern observatory. Its garden keeps Ming-dynasty bronze instruments: an armillary sphere and Guo Shoujing’s simplified armillary.
Beijing Ancient Observatory. Built in 1442 on top of the city wall, it was used for almost 500 years. Huge bronze instruments still stand on its platform.
Xingtai. The Yuan astronomer and water engineer Guo Shoujing was born here. He built new instruments and helped make the Shoushi calendar, with a year of 365.2425 days.
Rome. Rome grew from a small republic into an empire around the Mediterranean. In 1582 the calendar we use today was issued here, with a year of 365.2425 days — the same as Guo Shoujing’s calendar 300 years earlier.
Central Plains. Today: mainly Henan. The plains along the middle and lower Yellow River, mostly in today’s Henan. Many early dynasties had their capitals here, so people saw it as the centre of the land.
Baghdad. Around 793 a paper mill opened in Baghdad, then the capital of the Arab empire. From the Arab world, paper later travelled on to Europe.
Philadelphia. The first paper mill in North America opened near here in 1690 — almost 1,600 years after Cai Lun. In 1776 the Declaration of Independence was signed here and printed with movable type.
Fangmatan. Today: Tianshui, Gansu. In 1986 archaeologists found a scrap of paper with a map drawn in ink in a Western Han tomb here — proof that China had paper before Cai Lun.
Liuyang. Liuyang has made fireworks for many centuries and is called the “home of fireworks” — one of gunpowder’s oldest peaceful uses.
Teotihuacan. Around the time the Western Roman Empire fell, this city in Mexico was at its height, with the huge Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon.
Cahokia. Near today’s St. Louis, Native Americans built a town of earth mounds; Monks Mound is about 30 m high. Around 1100 it was the largest town north of Mexico.
Badaling. The most-visited stretch of the Great Wall. The wall is about 7.8 m high and so wide on top that five horses could ride side by side.
Mutianyu. Watchtowers stand close together on steep ridges here, and some stretches have battlements on both sides so soldiers could fight in either direction.
Shanhai Pass. Today: Qinhuangdao, Hebei. Squeezed between the Yan Mountains and the Bohai Sea — its name means “mountain-sea pass”. At Old Dragon’s Head the wall runs right into the sea. In 1644 the Qing army entered China proper here.
Jiayu Pass. This fort guarded a narrow gap between the Qilian Mountains and the Hei Mountains on the Silk Road.
Jinshanling. Today: Luanping, Hebei. Walls and watchtowers ride up and down the mountains. This stretch was built when the general Qi Jiguang guarded the north.
Juyong Pass. The wall climbs both sides of a narrow valley here to guard the road to the capital. It is one of the most famous passes of the Great Wall.
Jiankou. A wild, unrestored stretch on knife-edge ridges. Its name means “arrow on the bowstring”, from the shape of the ridges.
Hushan. Today: Dandong, Liaoning. On the Yalu River at the border with the DPRK (North Korea). The 2009 survey put the eastern start of the Ming Great Wall here.
Yan Mountains. Many Ming walls, like Jinshanling and Mutianyu, run along its ridges; at Shanhai Pass the mountains meet the sea.
Yuanmou. Two front teeth found here in 1965 belong to Yuanmou Man, who lived about 1.7 million years ago — the earliest ancient human yet confirmed in China.
Zhoukoudian. Today: Fangshan, Beijing. Caves on Dragon Bone Hill were home to Peking Man, who used fire, and much later to Upper Cave Man, who sewed with bone needles. It is a World Heritage Site.
Banpo. Today: Xi’an, Shaanxi. Banpo villagers grew millet, lived in half-underground houses and made painted pottery, like the famous basin with a human face and fish.
Liangzhu. Today: Yuhang, Hangzhou, Zhejiang. A walled city with palaces, dams and fine jade, about 5,000 years old — proof of more than 5,000 years of Chinese civilization.
Taosi. Today: Xiangfen, Shanxi. An early capital with a palace area, big tombs and an observatory for watching the sunrise. Some scholars link it to the legendary ruler Yao.
Zhuolu. In legend, the Yan Emperor and the Yellow Emperor joined forces and defeated Chiyou here; their tribes gradually became the Huaxia people.
Mausoleum of the Yellow Emperor. Today: Huangling County, Shaanxi. The legendary Yellow Emperor is honoured as a first ancestor of the Chinese nation. Memorial ceremonies are held here every year.
Mesopotamia. Today: mainly Iraq. Between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, people built some of the world’s first cities and invented cuneiform writing and the wheel.
Great Pyramid of Giza. Built about 4,500 years ago for the pharaoh Khufu, it was the tallest building on Earth for almost 4,000 years.
Stonehenge. A ring of huge standing stones in England. Its main axis lines up with the sunrise on midsummer’s day.
Indus Valley. Today: mainly Pakistan. About 4,500 years ago cities here, such as Mohenjo-daro, had brick houses on neat street grids and covered drains.
Jianye. Today: Nanjing, Jiangsu. Sun Quan made Jianye the capital of Wu. Later the same city, renamed Jiankang, was the capital of the Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties.
Yizhou. Today: Taiwan. In 230 Sun Quan sent Wei Wen with a fleet of ten thousand soldiers across the sea to Yizhou — the earliest record of mainland people reaching Taiwan in large numbers.
Pingcheng. Today: Datong, Shanxi. The Northern Wei ruled the north from Pingcheng. In 494 Emperor Xiaowen moved the capital south to Luoyang to be closer to the Central Plains.
Daxing. Today: Xi’an, Shaanxi. Emperor Wen of Sui built Daxing as a brand-new city laid out like a chessboard. The Tang kept it and renamed it Chang’an.
Sixteen Prefectures of Yan and Yun. Today: Around Beijing, northern Hebei and northern Shanxi. These sixteen prefectures lay south of the Great Wall. Once the Liao held them, the plains of the north had no mountain barrier left to protect them.
Zhongdu. Today: Southwest Beijing. The Jin moved their capital to Zhongdu in 1153. This was the first time a dynasty made the Beijing area its main capital.
Karakorum. Today: Kharkhorin, Mongolia. The Mongol khans ruled their huge empire from Karakorum on the grassland. Kublai Khan later moved the capital to Dadu (Beijing).
Yingtian. Today: Nanjing, Jiangsu. Zhu Yuanzhang founded the Ming at Yingtian and built a city wall over 35 km long — much of it still stands. In 1421 the capital moved to Beijing.
Nerchinsk. Today: Nerchinsk, Russia. In 1689 China and Russia signed a treaty here that fixed the eastern part of their border along the Argun River and the Outer Khingan Mountains.
Xinjiang. Xinjiang is China’s largest provincial region — about one sixth of the country — with high mountains, huge basins and the Taklamakan Desert.
Mawangdui. Today: Changsha, Hunan. The tombs held silk maps, silk books and a robe that weighs only 49 grams. The finds are in the Hunan Museum.
Baoji. Many Zhou bronzes were dug up around Baoji in Shaanxi, including the He Zun, whose inscription has the oldest known “中国”.
Mancheng Han tombs. Today: Mancheng, Baoding, Hebei. The tombs were cut deep into a hillside. Two suits made of jade pieces sewn with gold wire were found inside.
Summer Palace. Today: Haidian, Beijing. A huge royal garden with Longevity Hill and Kunming Lake. Its Long Corridor is painted with more than 14,000 pictures.
Alps. The highest mountains in Europe. In 218 BCE the general Hannibal led an army with war elephants over them to attack Rome.
Arabian Peninsula. The world’s largest peninsula, mostly desert. Around 610 Muhammad began preaching Islam in Mecca here.
Ji Province. Today: Mainly today’s Hebei. Ji Province was rich farmland north of the Yellow River. Yuan Shao, the strongest warlord in the north, ruled it from the city of Ye.
Jiangdong. Today: South of the lower Yangtze (southern Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shanghai). “East of the river” — the land south of the lower Yangtze. Sun Ce and Sun Quan built their power here, which became the state of Wu.
Jing Province. Today: Mainly today’s Hubei and Hunan. Jing Province sat in the middle of the Yangtze, linking north and south, east and west — so every side in the Three Kingdoms wanted it.
Yi Province. Today: The Sichuan Basin and nearby areas. Rich, rice-growing Yi Province was ringed by mountains, so it was easy to defend. Liu Bei took it and founded Shu with Chengdu as its capital.
Yungang Grottoes. Today: West of Datong, Shanxi. More than 50,000 Buddhist statues are carved into a sandstone cliff near the old Northern Wei capital Pingcheng. The biggest Buddha is about 17 m tall.
Wuhou Shrine. Today: Chengdu, Sichuan. Zhuge Liang’s title was “Marquis Wu”, so his memorial temple is called the Wuhou Shrine. It stands next to Liu Bei’s tomb in Chengdu.
Orchid Pavilion. Today: Shaoxing, Zhejiang. In 353 Wang Xizhi and his friends wrote poems here beside a winding stream. His preface to the poems became the most famous piece of Chinese calligraphy.
Yongning Temple. Today: Han–Wei Luoyang city site, east of Luoyang. Its nine-storey wooden pagoda was perhaps about 140 m high — one of the tallest buildings in the world then. It burned down in 534; only the base remains.
Indian Ocean. The third-largest ocean. Zheng He’s fleets sailed across it to India, Arabia and East Africa, riding the monsoon winds.
Champa. Today: South-central Vietnam. Champa was a kingdom on the coast of today’s Vietnam. Zheng He’s fleet usually stopped here first after crossing the South China Sea.
Strait of Malacca. A narrow sea lane between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra — the shortest way between the Pacific and the Indian Ocean. Many big ships still pass it every day.
Samudera. Today: Northern Sumatra, Indonesia. Samudera (Samudera Pasai) was a trading kingdom at the north tip of Sumatra. The big island was later named after it.
Java. Today: Java, Indonesia. A big island of Indonesia that Zheng He’s fleet visited. Today more than 150 million people live there.
Bengal. Today: Bangladesh and West Bengal, India. In 1414 envoys from Bengal brought a giraffe from Africa to the Ming emperor — people in Beijing thought it was a qilin!
Ceylon. Today: Sri Lanka. A stone tablet Zheng He set up at Galle in 1409 is written in Chinese, Tamil and Persian. It is now in the Colombo National Museum.
Aden. Today: Aden, Yemen. Aden is an old port at the mouth of the Red Sea, built inside the crater of an extinct volcano.
Mogadishu. Today: Mogadishu, Somalia. A trading city on the east coast of Africa. Zheng He’s ships came here more than 70 years before Vasco da Gama.
Taizhou. Today: Linhai, Taizhou, Zhejiang. Qi Jiguang’s well-trained “Qi Family Army” beat the wokou pirates nine times in a row around Taizhou.
Heilongjiang (Amur) River. A great river of northeast Asia, about 4,400 km long. Today part of it is the border between China and Russia, and Heilongjiang Province is named after it.
Yaksa. Today: Albazino, Russia. Russian forces built a fort here on the Heilongjiang. Kangxi’s army besieged it twice and won; then the two sides signed the Treaty of Nerchinsk.
Outer Khingan Mountains. Today: Stanovoy Range, Russia. The Treaty of Nerchinsk set the border along these mountains: the land south of them, down to the Heilongjiang, belonged to China.
Kuye Island. Today: Sakhalin, Russia. A long, cold island north of Japan. The Treaty of Nerchinsk counted it as Chinese land, but Russia seized it in the 19th century.
Ili. Today: Yining and Huocheng, Xinjiang. From Huiyuan city in the green Ili valley, the General of Ili governed all of Xinjiang, including the lands round Lake Balkhash.
Lake Balkhash. Today: Kazakhstan. A long desert lake: its western half is fresh water and its eastern half is salty.
Volga River. Today: Russia. Europe’s longest river. In 1771 the Torghut people under Ubashi set out from here and trekked thousands of kilometres east to return to China.
Temple of Heaven. Today: Beijing. Ming and Qing emperors came here to worship Heaven and pray for good harvests. The round Hall of Prayer is built without a single nail.
Treasure Boat Shipyard. Today: Nanjing, Jiangsu. Long dry docks beside the Yangtze in Nanjing, where the giant treasure ships were built. A full-size model ship stands there today.
Ming Xiaoling Mausoleum. Today: Purple Mountain, Nanjing. The tomb of Zhu Yuanzhang. Its “Sacred Way” is lined with huge stone camels, elephants, lions and horses.
Thirteen Hongs. Today: Guangzhou, Guangdong. From 1757 Western merchants could trade only in Guangzhou, through the licensed Chinese merchants of the Thirteen Hongs, and they had to live in the row of factories by the river.
Jintian. Today: Guiping, Guangxi. In January 1851 Hong Xiuquan led a peasant uprising at Jintian village and named his movement the “Taiping Heavenly Kingdom”.
Tianjing. Today: Nanjing, Jiangsu. The Taiping army took Nanjing in 1853 and renamed it Tianjing, “Heavenly Capital”. It fell in 1864 and the movement failed.
Yuanmingyuan. Today: Haidian, Beijing. A vast Qing imperial garden, once called the “Garden of Gardens”. In 1860 British and French troops looted it and set it on fire; today its ruins remind people never to forget.
Kowloon. Today: Kowloon, Hong Kong. The peninsula across the harbour from Hong Kong Island. Britain took its southern tip in 1860; China resumed sovereignty over all of Hong Kong in 1997.
Anqing Arsenal. Today: Anqing, Anhui. One of the first modern weapons workshops of the Self-Strengthening Movement. Its workers built China’s first small steamship.
Jiangnan Arsenal. Today: Shanghai. The biggest arsenal of the Self-Strengthening Movement. It made guns, cannons and steamships, and translated many Western science books.
Fuzhou Navy Yard. Today: Mawei, Fuzhou, Fujian. A shipyard and naval school at Mawei. It built warships and trained many of China’s first modern naval officers.
Hanyang Iron Works. Today: Hanyang, Wuhan, Hubei. The first large modern iron and steel works in Asia, started by Zhang Zhidong in the “seek wealth” stage of the Self-Strengthening Movement.
Weihaiwei. Today: Weihai, Shandong. The Beiyang Fleet’s headquarters was on Liugong Island. In early 1895 the fleet was wiped out here in the Sino-Japanese War. The island is now a war museum.
Shashi. Today: Jingzhou, Hubei. A river port on the middle Yangtze. Opening it let foreign ships and goods reach deep into central China.
Peking University. Today: Beijing (the “Red Building” at Shatan; the university moved to Haidian in 1952). The Imperial University was the one reform of 1898 that survived. Twenty years later, as Peking University, it was a centre of the New Culture and May Fourth Movements.
Dongjiaominxiang. Today: Dongcheng, Beijing. After 1901 this street was a walled-off quarter for foreign embassies, guarded by foreign soldiers, where Chinese people were not allowed to live.
Dagu Forts. Today: Binhai, Tianjin. Forts guarding the mouth of the Hai River, the sea gate to Tianjin and Beijing. The Boxer Protocol forced China to tear them down.
Tokyo. Today: Tokyo, Japan. The capital of Japan. Many Chinese students studied there around 1900, and in 1905 Sun Yat-sen founded the Chinese Revolutionary Alliance (Tongmenghui) in Tokyo.
Wuchang. Today: Wuhan, Hubei. On 10 October 1911 revolutionary soldiers rose up in Wuchang and took the city in one night. Province after province followed — this was the 1911 Revolution.
Niuzhuang. Today: Yingkou, Liaoning. Niuzhuang was named in the treaty, but the river there was too shallow, so the port was actually opened at Yingkou near the sea.
Dengzhou. Today: Yantai (Penglai), Shandong. Dengzhou’s city was at Penglai, famous for the Penglai Pavilion above the sea. The treaty port itself was soon moved to nearby Yantai.
Tainan. Today: Tainan, Taiwan Province. Zheng Chenggong landed here in 1661 and drove out the Dutch from Fort Zeelandia. Tainan was Taiwan’s main city for more than 200 years.
Tamsui. Today: Tamsui, New Taipei, Taiwan Province. A river-mouth port in northern Taiwan, famous for its sunsets and the old red Fort San Domingo.
Chaozhou. Today: Chaozhou and Shantou, Guangdong. Chaozhou is an old city famous for its Guangji Bridge and its tea culture. The treaty port was set up at Shantou on the coast.
Qiongzhou. Today: Haikou, Hainan. Qiongzhou was the old name of the main city of Hainan Island. The treaty port grew into today’s Haikou.
Jiujiang. A river port on the Yangtze at the foot of Mount Lu, near Poyang Lake. It became a busy market for tea, rice and porcelain.
Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum. Today: Purple Mountain, Nanjing. Visitors climb 392 steps to reach Sun Yat-sen’s tomb on Purple Mountain. From above, the layout looks like a bell — a call to wake people up.
Yongdingmen. The southern start of Beijing’s 7.8 km Central Axis. The gate was pulled down in 1957 and rebuilt in 2004–2005 from old drawings and photos.
Altar of Agriculture. Today: Beijing Ancient Architecture Museum. Every spring the emperor came here to plough a field himself, to show that farming mattered. It faces the Temple of Heaven across the axis.
Zhengyangmen (Qianmen). At about 43 m, it was the tallest gate of old Beijing. In front stands an arrow tower with rows of little windows for archers.
Monument to the People’s Heroes. Today: Tian’anmen Square, Beijing. A 38 m granite obelisk on the Central Axis, honouring the people who gave their lives for China’s independence and liberation.
Great Hall of the People. Today: West side of Tian’anmen Square, Beijing. The National People’s Congress meets here. Its main auditorium seats about 10,000 people.
National Museum of China. Today: East side of Tian’anmen Square, Beijing. One of the biggest museums in the world, with more than a million objects — from Neolithic pottery and the Houmuwu ding to treasures of every dynasty.
Imperial Ancestral Temple. Today: Working People’s Cultural Palace, Beijing. Ming and Qing emperors worshipped their ancestors here. Seen by an emperor facing south, it lies on his left (east).
Altar of Land and Grain. Today: Zhongshan Park, Beijing. The square altar is covered with soil of five colours, standing for the east, south, west, north and centre of the land.
Jingshan. A 45 m hill just north of the Forbidden City. The pavilion on top sits exactly on the Central Axis — the best place to see the palace’s golden roofs.
Wanning Bridge. The oldest bridge on the Central Axis. Under the Yuan, grain boats on the Grand Canal passed beneath it into Jishuitan — the canal’s northern end.
Drum Tower and Bell Tower. Drums sounded the hours at night and the bell rang at dawn. The bronze bell in the Bell Tower weighs about 63 tonnes.
Shichahai (Jishuitan). Today: Xicheng, Beijing. In the Yuan this lake, then called Jishuitan, was a busy harbour full of canal boats. Today people row boats in summer and skate on it in winter.
Olympic Park. The “Bird’s Nest” stadium and the “Water Cube” stand on either side of the Central Axis. Beijing is the first city to host both Summer (2008) and Winter (2022) Olympics.
Beijing Daxing International Airport. Its terminal is shaped like a giant starfish, so passengers never walk too far to a gate. It is about 46 km south of the city centre.
Zhongguancun. Today: Haidian, Beijing. Called “China’s Silicon Valley”: thousands of tech companies sit next to famous universities and research institutes.
Huairou Science City. Today: Huairou, Beijing. A new science town in Beijing’s northern hills, home to big research facilities such as a high-energy light source.
Tsinghua University. Today: Haidian, Beijing. One of China’s top universities, famous for engineering and science, next door to Peking University in northwest Beijing.
Capital Museum. Today: Xicheng, Beijing. The museum of Beijing’s own history, with models of the old city and treasures found in the capital.
Yongding River. It used to flood so often that it was called the “river without a fixed course”; the Qing renamed it Yongding, “forever steady”. The Lugou Bridge crosses it.
Shibadong Village. Today: Huayuan County, Xiangxi, Hunan. A Miao village in the mountains of western Hunan. It found its way out of poverty by growing kiwi fruit, opening family guesthouses and selling local crafts.
FAST (China Sky Eye). Today: Pingtang County, Guizhou. A radio telescope 500 m across, built in a natural bowl among karst hills — the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world. It has found hundreds of new pulsars.
Mariana Trench. The deepest place in the ocean, in the western Pacific — deep enough to swallow Mount Everest with more than 2 km to spare.
Saihanba. Today: Weichang County, Chengde, Hebei. Three generations of foresters turned sandy wasteland here into one of the largest planted forests in the world, which helps shield Beijing from sandstorms.
Kubuqi Desert. Today: Ordos, Inner Mongolia. A desert south of the great bend of the Yellow River. People have planted grass and trees here and built huge fields of solar panels on the sand.
Xiong’an New Area. Today: Hebei. A brand-new city being built in Hebei to take some offices, universities and companies out of crowded Beijing.
Taizicheng. Today: Chongli, Zhangjiakou, Hebei. A station in the snowy hills of Chongli, where skiing events of the 2022 Winter Olympics were held. Trains from Beijing take about an hour.
Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei. The capital region around Beijing, Tianjin and Hebei, home to over 100 million people. Since 2014 the three have planned transport, industry and the environment together.
Zhengzhou. Zhengzhou grew up around a railway crossing and is now one of China’s busiest rail hubs, with high-speed lines going out in all directions.
Zhuzhou. A railway hub in Hunan that is also famous for building electric locomotives.
Lanzhou. The only provincial capital that the Yellow River flows right through the middle of. Several railways meet here on the way to Xinjiang.
Tangshan. A coal and steel city in Hebei. It was destroyed by a huge earthquake in 1976 and rebuilt as a new city.
Hefei. Hefei is known for new display screens and quantum science; many research labs and factories have grown here quickly.
Datong. Datong sits on one of China’s richest coalfields. Long coal trains leave it every day for the port of Qinhuangdao.
Datong–Qinhuangdao Railway. A railway built just for coal, from Datong in Shanxi to the port of Qinhuangdao. Its heavy trains carry hundreds of millions of tonnes a year.
Jinan. Jinan has more than 70 famous springs; the water of Baotu Spring bubbles up all year round.
Shijiazhuang. About 100 years ago Shijiazhuang was a village. Railways crossing here turned it into a big city.
Taiyuan. An old city in the coal-rich province of Shanxi. The Jinci Temple outside the city has halls and statues from the Song dynasty.
Changsha. A lively city on the Xiang River. Orange Isle in the river and the Han tombs at Mawangdui are famous.
Guiyang. A mountain city with cool summers. Many big data centres are built here because the climate is mild.
Baotou. A steel city by the Yellow River. Nearby Bayan Obo has one of the world’s largest rare-earth mines.
Golmud. A city in the Qaidam Basin, Qinghai. Trains to Lhasa leave from here and climb over passes more than 5,000 m high.
Inner Mongolia. A long region across northern China with wide grasslands — China’s largest pastoral area, famous for Sanhe horses and cattle.
Qinghai. A high province where the Yangtze, Yellow and Lancang (Mekong) rivers begin. It is named after Qinghai Lake, China’s largest lake.
Sanjiang Plain. Today: Northeast Heilongjiang. A low plain where the Heilongjiang, Songhua and Wusuli rivers meet. Once a wild marsh (“Great Northern Wilderness”), it is now a big grain base.
Songnen Plain. The flat, black-soil land along the Songhua and Nen rivers, a major area for corn, soybeans and rice.
Jianghan Plain. Where the Han River meets the Yangtze in Hubei: a flat land of lakes, rice fields and yellow rapeseed flowers in spring.
Jianghuai region. The land between the Yangtze and the Huai rivers, in Anhui and Jiangsu — rice and wheat both grow here.
Zhangjiang. Today: Pudong, Shanghai. A science park in Pudong where companies design computer chips and new medicines.
Optics Valley of China. Today: East Lake High-tech Zone, Wuhan, Hubei. Wuhan’s East Lake hi-tech zone, a centre for optical fibre and lasers. About a quarter of the world’s optical fibre is made here.
Longsheng rice terraces. Today: Longsheng County, Guilin, Guangxi. Over hundreds of years, Zhuang and Yao farmers carved these hills into steps to hold water for rice.
Hexigten Banner. Today: Chifeng, Inner Mongolia. Grasslands, forests and sand dunes in eastern Inner Mongolia, where herders raise horses, cattle and sheep.
Tanghe County. Today: Nanyang, Henan. A farming county in southern Henan, where combine harvesters bring in the wheat every June.
The Bund. Today: Huangpu, Shanghai. A riverside promenade lined with grand old buildings. Across the Huangpu River rise the skyscrapers of Lujiazui.
Pacific Ocean. The biggest and deepest ocean. In summer, warm wet winds blow from it onto eastern China and bring the rainy season.
Siberia. Today: Russia. A vast cold land in northern Asia. In winter, freezing dry winds blow from here across northern China.
Mississippi River. The great river of the United States, about 3,700 km long. Like the Yellow River it carries lots of mud and has levees to stop floods.
South China. Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan and nearby: the warmest, rainiest part of mainland China. The rainy season starts here in May.
Hukou Waterfall. Today: Between Shanxi and Shaanxi. The whole Yellow River squeezes into a narrow rock gorge here, like water poured from a teapot spout — it is the river’s biggest waterfall, thick with yellow mud.
Bayan Har Mountains. Today: Qinghai. The Yellow River begins on the northern slopes of these mountains, about 4,500 m high on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau.
Tanggula Mountains. Today: Border of Qinghai and Tibet. The Yangtze begins at Geladaindong, the highest peak of these mountains. The Qinghai–Tibet Railway crosses them at over 5,000 m.
Gangdise Mountains. Today: Tibet. A snowy range in western Tibet, north of the Himalayas. Its most famous peak is Mount Kailash (Gang Rinpoche).
Yin Mountains. Today: Central Inner Mongolia. Mountains north of the Yellow River’s great bend. They divide the grasslands from the Hetao farmlands — an old frontier of Chinese history.
Helan Mountains. Today: Between Ningxia and Inner Mongolia. They block cold winds and desert sand from the west, helping the Ningxia Plain to be a green oasis — “Jiangnan beyond the frontier”.
Liujiaxia. Today: Yongjing County, Gansu. A deep gorge on the upper Yellow River, dammed to make electricity. It was China’s first million-kilowatt hydropower station.
Songhua River. Today: Jilin and Heilongjiang. The great river of Northeast China. In winter it freezes so hard that people drive and skate on it at Harbin.
Han River. The longest tributary of the Yangtze, joining it at Wuhan. Its Danjiangkou Reservoir sends water north to Beijing.
Jialing River. A big tributary of the Yangtze in Sichuan. It meets the Yangtze at Chongqing, the “mountain city”.
Jingjiang section. Today: Yangtze River near Jingzhou, Hubei. Here the Yangtze winds so much that people say “nine bends and eighteen turns”. Its slow water drops mud, so floods are a danger.
Flaming Mountains. Today: Near Turpan, Xinjiang. Red sandstone hills that seem to burn in the summer heat; the ground can get hotter than 70 °C. They appear in “Journey to the West”.
Yellow River Delta. Today: Dongying, Shandong. The Yellow River drops so much mud where it meets the Bohai Sea that new land grows out into the sea every year. It is a big wetland for birds.
Wenchuan. Today: Wenchuan County, Aba, Sichuan. The most destructive earthquake since 1949. Rescuers came from all over China, and every province helped rebuild a stricken county with stronger schools and homes.
Beichuan. Today: Beichuan Qiang Autonomous County, Mianyang, Sichuan. The old town was wrecked by the earthquake and landslides. It is kept as a memorial, and a new Beichuan was built on safer ground nearby.
Longmen Shan. Today: Western edge of the Sichuan Basin. Where the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau pushes east against the Sichuan Basin. The rocks along this fault snapped in 2008 and caused the Wenchuan earthquake.
Three-North Shelter Forest. Today: Northwest, North and Northeast China. The “Great Green Wall”: belts of trees planted across northern China since 1978 to hold back wind and sand.
Lingdingyang. Today: Pearl River Estuary, between Hong Kong, Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Macao. The wide mouth of the Pearl River. The Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge crosses it, dipping into an undersea tunnel so big ships can pass.
Lantau Island. Today: Hong Kong. Hong Kong’s biggest island, home to Hong Kong International Airport and the landing of the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge.
Pearl River. South China’s biggest river system. It flows past Guangzhou into the sea, where its delta is one of the world’s busiest manufacturing areas.
Lunnan. Today: Luntai County, Xinjiang. An oil and gas field in the Tarim Basin. Gas from here travels about 4,000 km by pipeline to Shanghai.
Ordos Basin. Today: Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia and Inner Mongolia. A huge basin rich in coal, oil and natural gas, south of the Yellow River’s great bend.
Fenghuoshan Tunnel. Today: Qinghai, on the Qinghai–Tibet Railway. The highest railway tunnel in the world on permafrost — engineers kept the frozen ground cold so the tunnel would not sink.
Qingshuihe Bridge. Today: Hoh Xil, Qinghai, on the Qinghai–Tibet Railway. An 11.7 km bridge over frozen ground. Its open arches let Tibetan antelope cross underneath on their migrations.
Nagqu. Today: Tibet. A town at about 4,500 m on the northern Tibetan grasslands, a stop on the Qinghai–Tibet Railway.
Golden Gate Bridge. Today: San Francisco, USA. San Francisco’s famous red suspension bridge, 2.7 km long — a good yardstick for how long China’s new bridges are.
Chesapeake Bay Bridge–Tunnel. Today: Virginia, USA. Like the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge, it mixes bridges, artificial islands and undersea tunnels so ships can pass.
Lake Pontchartrain Causeway. Today: Louisiana, USA. A pair of low bridges about 38 km long across a lake near New Orleans — one of the longest bridges over water in the world.
Jiuzhaigou. Today: Aba, Sichuan. A valley of blue and green lakes held back by natural walls of travertine (calcium carbonate) — like Mammoth Hot Springs in Yellowstone.
Wulingyuan (Zhangjiajie). Today: Zhangjiajie, Hunan. Over 3,000 sandstone pillars, some more than 200 m tall, shaped by water and weather over millions of years.
Huangguoshu Waterfall. Today: Anshun, Guizhou. One of Asia’s biggest waterfalls, about 78 m high and 100 m wide, in the karst hills of Guizhou. You can walk through a cave behind the curtain of water.
Mount Danxia. Today: Shaoguan, Guangdong. Red sandstone cliffs that gave their name to the “Danxia” landform found all over China.
Wulong Three Natural Bridges. Today: Wulong, Chongqing. Three giant stone arches left behind when the roofs of underground karst caves collapsed.
Reed Flute Cave. Today: Guilin, Guangxi. A limestone cave full of stalactites and stalagmites, lit with coloured lights. It is named after the reeds outside, once used to make flutes.
Dunhuang Yardang National Geopark. Today: Northwest of Dunhuang, Gansu. Rows of clay ridges carved by strong desert winds. At night the wind howls through them, so people call it the “Ghost City”.
Yellowstone National Park. Today: Wyoming, USA. The world’s first national park (1872), famous for geysers and hot springs. At Mammoth Hot Springs, warm water builds terraces of travertine.
Mammoth Cave. Today: Kentucky, USA. The longest known cave system in the world, more than 680 km of passages in limestone.
Danjiangkou Reservoir. Today: Hubei and Henan. A huge reservoir on the Han River. Since 2014 its water flows north for about 1,400 km to Beijing and Tianjin without any pumps — downhill all the way.
Hongze Lake. Today: Jiangsu. One of China’s five largest freshwater lakes. The eastern route of the South–North Water Diversion pumps water through it on the way north.
Luoma Lake. Today: Northern Jiangsu. A lake in northern Jiangsu used as a storage step on the eastern route of the South–North Water Diversion.
Nansi Lake. Today: Southwest Shandong. Four lakes joined in a chain — Weishan, Zhaoyang, Dushan and Nanyang — next to the Grand Canal; the eastern route passes through them.
Dongping Lake. Today: Shandong. The last big lake on the eastern route. From here water goes north under the Yellow River and east to the Jiaodong Peninsula.
Tongtian River. Today: Qinghai (upper Yangtze). The name of the upper Yangtze in Qinghai. In “Journey to the West” the pilgrims cross it on a giant turtle’s back.
Yalong River. Today: Western Sichuan. A big tributary of the Jinsha (upper Yangtze) flowing south through deep gorges in western Sichuan; it has several large hydropower dams.
Jiaodong Peninsula. Today: Eastern Shandong. The peninsula of eastern Shandong with the cities of Qingdao, Yantai and Weihai. It is short of fresh water, so the eastern route sends water there.
Jiaozuo. Today: Henan. A city in northern Henan. The middle route of the South–North Water Diversion runs right through the city centre in a wide green belt.
California. Today: USA. Like northern China, dry southern California gets water carried hundreds of kilometres by canals and aqueducts.
Xiaozhai Tiankeng. Today: Fengjie, Chongqing. The deepest known sinkhole in the world, about 660 m deep — formed when the roof over an underground river fell in.
Yangshuo. Today: Guilin, Guangxi. A small town on the Li River surrounded by tall karst peaks rising out of the rice fields.
Kras (Karst) Plateau. Today: Slovenia. A limestone plateau in Slovenia. Its name “Kras” gave us the word “karst” for landscapes of caves and stone peaks.
Euphrates River. It flows about 2,800 km from the mountains of Türkiye through Syria and Iraq to the Persian Gulf. Ur, Uruk and Babylon stood beside it.
Tigris River. Faster and wilder than the Euphrates. Nineveh, the Assyrian capital, and later Baghdad were built on its banks.
Nile River. At about 6,650 km it is one of the two longest rivers on Earth. Every summer it flooded and left rich black mud on the fields — “Egypt is the gift of the Nile”.
Uruk. Today: Warka, Iraq. Around 3000 BCE perhaps 40,000 people lived inside its walls. Some of the oldest writing ever found comes from its temples.
Ur. Today: near Nasiriyah, Iraq. A rich port city of Sumer near the old coast of the Persian Gulf. Its great stepped temple, the Ziggurat of Ur, still stands.
Lagash. Today: Tell al-Hiba, Iraq. One of the many independent Sumerian city-states, each with its own king and god. It often fought its neighbour Umma over water.
Babylon. Today: near Hillah, Iraq. Hammurabi ruled from here and set up his law code. Around 600 BCE it was the biggest city in the world, entered through the blue Ishtar Gate.
Nineveh. Today: Mosul, Iraq. King Ashurbanipal kept a library of thousands of clay tablets here, including the Epic of Gilgamesh. Its ruins lie across the Tigris from Mosul.
Memphis. Today: Mit Rahina, near Cairo. It stood where the Nile valley opens into the delta, so it could watch over both Upper and Lower Egypt. The pyramids of Giza and Saqqara were its royal cemeteries.
Saqqara. The cemetery of Memphis. Here the architect Imhotep built the Step Pyramid — the oldest large stone building in the world.
Thebes. Today: Luxor, Egypt. The great temples of Karnak and Luxor stand here on the east bank of the Nile. On the west bank, pharaohs were buried in the Valley of the Kings.
Valley of the Kings. Pharaohs were buried in tombs cut deep into the rock of this desert valley. Tutankhamun’s tomb, found almost untouched in 1922, is here.
Zagros Mountains. A long chain of mountains between Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau. Snow melting here every spring fills the Tigris.
Sinai Peninsula. A rocky, dry triangle of land that joins Africa to Asia. Egyptians came here for copper and turquoise, not to farm.
Anatolia (Asia Minor). Today: Asian part of Türkiye. A big peninsula between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean — a bridge between Asia and Europe crossed by armies and traders for thousands of years.
Nubia (Kush). Today: Sudan and southern Egypt. The land up the Nile, south of Egypt, rich in gold. Its kings later built more than 200 steep pyramids at Meroë — more than Egypt has.
Kadesh. Today: near Homs, Syria. Ramesses II fought the Hittites here in one of the biggest chariot battles ever. Neither side won, and later they signed one of the oldest known peace treaties.
Alexandria. Its Library held hundreds of thousands of scrolls and its Lighthouse was one of the Seven Wonders. The Qaitbay fort now stands where the lighthouse was.
Mohenjo-daro. Today: Sindh, Pakistan. A planned city with straight streets, covered drains and a “Great Bath”. Perhaps 40,000 people lived here 4,500 years ago.
Harappa. Today: Punjab, Pakistan. The first Indus city that archaeologists dug up (1920s), so the whole civilization is also called “Harappan”. It had large granaries and brick houses.
Lothal. A small port town with a big brick basin that many archaeologists think was a dock for ships. Beads and cotton went from here as far as Mesopotamia.
Ganges River. A sacred river for Hindus, about 2,500 km long. The Buddha taught, and great Indian empires grew, in the Ganges plain.
Bodh Gaya. Buddhist tradition says Siddhartha Gautama sat under a tree here until he became the Buddha. The Mahabodhi Temple marks the spot.
Sarnath. In a deer park here the Buddha gave his first teaching. Ashoka’s lion pillar from Sarnath is now India’s national emblem.
Vaishali. An old city of the Ganges plain often visited by the Buddha. One of Ashoka’s polished stone pillars, topped by a lion, still stands here.
Pataliputra. Today: Patna, India. Ashoka ruled his huge empire from here. A Greek visitor described its wooden walls with 570 towers.
Kalinga. Today: Odisha, India (Dhauli hill). After conquering Kalinga, Ashoka was so sorry for the suffering that he turned to Buddhism and peace. His edicts are carved on a rock at Dhauli.
Sanchi. Its Great Stupa is a giant dome holding relics of the Buddha, with four carved stone gateways. It is one of the oldest stone buildings in India.
Taxila. A city where roads from Persia, Central Asia and India met, famous for its teachers. Chinese pilgrims called it 呾叉始罗.
Pasargadae. Cyrus the Great built gardens and palaces here. His simple stone tomb still stands on six giant steps.
Persepolis. Today: near Shiraz, Iran. Peoples from all over the Persian Empire came here with gifts for the king — you can still see them carved on the stairs. Alexander’s army burned it.
Susa. Today: Shush, Iran. One of the oldest cities in the world and a capital of the Persian kings. Royal messengers rode 2,500 km from Sardis to Susa in about a week.
Sardis. Today: near Salihli, Türkiye. Rich King Croesus ruled here, and some of the first coins in the world were made here. Cyrus took it in about 546 BCE.
Marathon. Athenian soldiers beat a much bigger Persian army on this plain. The story of a runner carrying the news 40 km to Athens gave us the marathon race.
Merv. Today: near Mary, Turkmenistan. A great oasis city on the Silk Road where caravans rested between Persia and Central Asia. In the 1100s it may have been the biggest city in the world.
Antioch. Today: Antakya, Türkiye. Founded by one of Alexander’s generals, it became one of the biggest cities of the Roman East and an end point of the Silk Road trade.
Ctesiphon. Today: near Baghdad, Iraq. On the Tigris, this was the capital of the Persian kings who traded with Han and Tang China. Its giant brick arch still stands.
Athens. Here citizens met on a hill called the Pnyx to vote on laws themselves. Under Pericles the city built the Parthenon on the Acropolis.
Acropolis & Parthenon. The “high city” is a rocky hill in the middle of Athens. The Parthenon on top was a marble temple for the goddess Athena.
Sparta. Today: Sparti, Greece. Boys left home at 7 to train as soldiers. Sparta had almost no city walls — its soldiers were its walls.
Peloponnese. The big southern peninsula of Greece, almost an island. Sparta led its cities in the Peloponnesian League against Athens.
Olympia. Every four years Greeks gathered here for games in honour of Zeus. The Olympic flame for today’s Games is still lit here.
Thermopylae. A narrow path between mountains and the sea. King Leonidas and his 300 Spartans held off the huge Persian army here for three days.
Salamis. In this narrow strait the Athenian navy trapped and sank the Persian fleet. Sea power made Athens the leader of Greece.
Corinth. A rich trading city on the narrow neck of land joining the Peloponnese to mainland Greece. Its people founded Syracuse.
Syracuse. Today: Siracusa, Sicily. The richest Greek city on Sicily and home of Archimedes, who designed machines to defend it against Rome.
Massalia (Marseille). Today: Marseille, France. Greek sailors founded Massalia about 2,600 years ago. In 1347 the Black Death arrived here by ship and spread into France.
Pella. Alexander the Great grew up here, taught by the philosopher Aristotle. Beautiful pebble mosaics have been found in its houses.
Chaeronea. Philip II of Macedon and his 18-year-old son Alexander won here, ending the independence of the Greek city-states. A stone lion marks the battlefield.
Tiber River. Legend says the twins Romulus and Remus were left in a basket on the Tiber and saved by a she-wolf. Rome grew where the river could be crossed.
Roman Forum. Romans came here to shop, hear speeches, go to court and watch the Senate. Now its broken columns stand among the ruins of temples.
Colosseum. About 50,000 people could watch gladiator fights here. Its arches and vaults are still copied in stadiums today.
Carthage. Today: near Tunis, Tunisia. A Phoenician trading city whose general Hannibal crossed the Alps with elephants to attack Rome. Romans later rebuilt it as a Roman city.
Appian Way. The “queen of roads” ran from Rome to Brundisium (Brindisi). Its paving stones are still there after more than 2,300 years.
Pompeii. When Vesuvius erupted, ash buried the whole town. Digging it out shows us Roman streets, shops, bakeries and paintings just as they were.
Pont du Gard. Three rows of stone arches carry an aqueduct 49 m above the river. The water channel drops only about 2.5 cm in the whole bridge.
Hadrian’s Wall. A stone wall about 118 km long across northern Britain, guarding the edge of the Roman Empire. Soldiers lived in forts along it.
Ravenna. The last Western Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was removed here in 476. Its churches glitter with Byzantine mosaics of Emperor Justinian.
Hagia Sophia. For almost 1,000 years its dome was the biggest in the world. It was a church, then a mosque after 1453; the minarets were added by the Ottomans.
Mecca. The holiest city of Islam. Every year millions of Muslims come here on the pilgrimage (hajj) to the Kaaba.
Medina. Muhammad and his followers moved here from Mecca in 622 — year 1 of the Islamic calendar. His tomb is in the Prophet’s Mosque.
Damascus. One of the oldest cities in the world that people still live in. The Umayyad caliphs built its Great Mosque in the early 700s.
Córdoba. Around the year 1000 it was one of the biggest cities in Europe, with paved streets, street lamps and huge libraries. Its Great Mosque has red-and-white arches.
Granada. The Alhambra palace, with its fountains and carved ceilings, looks down on the city. In 1492 Spain’s Catholic monarchs took Granada.
Tours (Poitiers). Charles Martel and the Franks defeated an Umayyad army from Spain here. After this, Muslim armies did not push further into France.
Aachen. Charlemagne built a palace and a domed chapel here and is buried in it. German kings were crowned in Aachen for 600 years.
Verdun. Charlemagne’s grandsons divided his empire here into three kingdoms — the beginnings of France and Germany.
Hastings. William of Normandy defeated King Harold here and became king of England. The town that grew on the battlefield is simply called “Battle”.
Bayeux. A 70-metre embroidered cloth kept here tells the story of the Norman Conquest like a comic strip.
London. William the Conqueror began the Tower of London. Later London was the heart of the English Revolution, the Industrial Revolution and a huge empire.
Paris. Notre-Dame cathedral was begun here in 1163 and its university grew around 1200. In 1789 Parisians stormed the Bastille fortress.
Chartres Cathedral. It still has most of its original stained-glass windows from the 1200s — Bible stories in coloured light for people who could not read.
Bologna. Its university is often called the oldest in Europe. Students came from all over Europe to study Roman law here.
Carcassonne. A whole hilltop town wrapped in two rings of walls with 52 towers — what a medieval fortress city looked like.
Bodiam Castle. A square castle standing in a wide moat, with round towers at the corners and a gatehouse reached by a bridge.
Dublin. The Book of Kells, painted by Irish monks around 800, is kept in the library of Trinity College here. In 1348 the Black Death crossed the sea to Dublin.
Jerusalem. A holy city for Jews, Christians and Muslims. Its Old City holds the Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Dome of the Rock.
Manzikert. Today: Malazgirt, Türkiye. The Seljuk Turks defeated and captured the Byzantine emperor here. Byzantium lost most of Anatolia and asked western Europe for help — leading to the Crusades.
Acre. Today: Akko, Israel. The Crusaders’ main port and last big stronghold in the Holy Land. When it fell in 1291, the Crusades in the East were over.
Cairo. Saladin built its Citadel on a hill in the 1170s. In the Middle Ages it was one of the biggest trading cities in the world.
Ain Jalut. Today: Harod spring, Israel. Two years after the Mongols destroyed Baghdad, the Mamluks of Egypt beat a Mongol army here — one of the first big Mongol defeats.
Talas. Today: Taraz area, Kazakhstan. The only big battle between Tang China and the Abbasid Caliphate. The traditional story says captured Chinese papermakers then spread paper-making west.
Caffa. Today: Feodosia, Crimea. A Genoese port on the Black Sea besieged by a Mongol army that had the plague. Ships escaping from here helped carry the Black Death to Europe.
Messina. Twelve Genoese galleys docked here in 1347 with sick sailors on board — the Black Death’s doorway into western Europe.
Genoa. Its merchants traded from the Black Sea to England. Christopher Columbus was born here around 1451.
Florence. Rich from wool and banking, the Medici family paid artists such as Leonardo, Michelangelo and Brunelleschi, whose dome still crowns the cathedral.
Orléans. In the Hundred Years’ War, 17-year-old Joan of Arc led a French army that broke the English siege of Orléans.
Lübeck. The leading city of the Hanseatic League, a group of trading towns around the North Sea and the Baltic. Its brick city gate, the Holstentor, still stands.
Moscow. Its fortress, the Kremlin, and Red Square are at the centre. In 1941 the German army was stopped just outside the city.
Milan. Leonardo da Vinci worked here for the Duke of Milan as painter and engineer. His Last Supper is on a wall of a convent dining hall here.
Vatican & Sistine Chapel. The pope’s own chapel. Michelangelo spent four years painting its ceiling, much of it standing on scaffolding with his head tipped back.
Mainz. Johannes Gutenberg printed his famous Bible here around 1455 with metal movable type. The Gutenberg Museum still shows how it worked.
Strasbourg. Gutenberg first experimented with printing here before returning to Mainz. Today the European Parliament meets in Strasbourg.
Nuremberg. A rich trading city full of printers and craftsmen. The artist Albrecht Dürer lived here; after World War II, Nazi leaders were tried here.
Wittenberg. Martin Luther taught at the university here. In 1517 he posted his 95 Theses — the story says on the door of the Castle Church.
Worms. At a great meeting (the Diet of Worms) the emperor ordered Luther to take back his books. Luther said no, and was declared an outlaw.
Wartburg Castle. Friends hid Luther in this hilltop castle. In about ten weeks he translated the New Testament from Greek into German.
Augsburg. Here in 1555 the German princes agreed that each ruler could choose whether his land would be Catholic or Lutheran.
Trent. Today: Trento, Italy. Catholic bishops met here on and off for 18 years to answer the Protestants and reform their own Church.
Bruges (Flanders). Rich cloth merchants lived in this canal city in Flanders. Painters here, like Jan van Eyck, mastered oil paint.
Westminster. William Caxton set up England’s first printing press next to Westminster Abbey. The Houses of Parliament stand here too.
Kraków. The old capital of Poland, with one of Europe’s biggest medieval market squares. Young Copernicus studied at its university.
Toledo. In the Middle Ages, scholars here translated Arabic books into Latin. The old city sits on a hill wrapped by the Tagus River.
Seville. Magellan’s fleet left from here in 1519, and the only ship to return, the Victoria, came back here in 1522. American silver poured in through its port.
Lisbon. Vasco da Gama set out from Lisbon’s Belém harbour. The pepper and spices his ships brought back made Lisbon one of Europe’s richest ports.
Lagos (Portugal). Prince Henry the Navigator’s ships sailed down the African coast from this harbour. Sadly it also had Europe’s first market for enslaved Africans (1444).
Cape of Good Hope. Dias first called it the “Cape of Storms”. The king of Portugal renamed it “Good Hope” — hope of a sea road to India.
Mossel Bay. After rounding Africa’s tip, Dias landed here before his tired crew made him turn back. A full-size copy of his caravel is in the town museum.
Palos de la Frontera. Columbus left from this small river port with the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa María. Copies of the three ships float at nearby La Rábida.
Canary Islands. Columbus took on water and food at La Gomera, then let the trade winds blow him west. About 36 days later he sighted land.
San Salvador (Bahamas). Columbus landed on an island of the Taíno people that he named San Salvador. He thought he had reached islands near Asia.
Tordesillas. Spain and Portugal drew a line down the Atlantic here and split the newly reached lands between them — without asking anyone who lived there.
Strait of Magellan. A twisting, stormy channel between South America and Tierra del Fuego. Magellan’s ships needed 38 days to get through to the Pacific.
Guam. After about 98 days on the open ocean, Magellan’s starving crew reached this island of the Chamorro people. Later Manila galleons stopped here.
Mactan. The local chief Lapulapu refused to obey Magellan, and Magellan was killed in the fight. Filipinos remember Lapulapu as a hero.
Spice Islands (Moluccas). Today: Maluku, Indonesia. Cloves and nutmeg grew only on a few small islands here, so they were worth more than gold in Europe. Magellan’s fleet set out to reach them by sailing west.
Manila. Chinese junks brought silk and porcelain here, and Spanish galleons brought silver from Mexico. Its walled old town, Intramuros, still stands.
Acapulco. Once a year a galleon arrived here from Manila loaded with Chinese goods, then sailed back full of American silver.
Red Sea. A long, narrow sea between Africa and Arabia. Zheng He’s ships reached its shores; today the Suez Canal links it to the Mediterranean.
Frombork. Nicolaus Copernicus worked at the cathedral of this small Baltic town and watched the sky from its walls. He is buried in the cathedral.
Padua. Galileo taught mathematics at the University of Padua for 18 years. Here he built telescopes and found four moons circling Jupiter.
Cambridge. Isaac Newton studied and taught at Trinity College here. His laws of motion and gravity explained why the planets move as they do.
Lexington and Concord. British soldiers marched out of Boston to seize weapons, and local militia fought them at Lexington and Concord. The war for independence had begun.
Saratoga. A whole British army surrendered here. The victory convinced France to join the war on the American side — the turning point of the Revolution.
Bastille. Today: Place de la Bastille, Paris. Parisians stormed this old royal fortress-prison to seize gunpowder. 14 July is now France’s national day. The fortress was pulled down; a column stands in the square.
Versailles. Louis XIV built this giant palace. The French Revolution began with a meeting of the Estates-General here (1789), and the treaty ending World War I was signed in its Hall of Mirrors.
Saint-Domingue (Haiti). Today: Haiti. France’s richest sugar colony, worked by enslaved Africans. Led by Toussaint Louverture, they won their freedom and founded Haiti in 1804.
Manchester. Steam-powered cotton mills made Manchester the first great industrial city. In 1830 the first inter-city railway linked it with Liverpool.
Liverpool. Raw cotton from America arrived at Liverpool’s docks and went by train to the Manchester mills. Earlier the port was deeply involved in the slave trade.
Liège. Belgium was the first country on the European mainland to industrialise, using the coal and iron around Liège.
Lowell. Water-powered textile mills along the Merrimack River employed thousands of young women, the “mill girls”.
Chengde Mountain Resort. Emperor Qianlong received the British envoy Lord Macartney here and turned down his requests for more trade.
Humen. In 1839 Lin Zexu destroyed over a million kilograms of opium on the beach here. Its forts guarding the Pearl River were attacked in the Opium War.
Sarajevo. The heir to Austria-Hungary was shot near the Latin Bridge here. Within weeks the alliances pulled Europe into World War I.
Ypres. Today: Ieper, Belgium. Five big battles were fought around this small Belgian town, and poison gas was first used on a large scale here. Every evening buglers play the Last Post at the Menin Gate.
Neuve Chapelle. Many soldiers from India fought in this early trench battle. A memorial here remembers the Indian soldiers who died on the Western Front.
The Somme. On the first day alone, about 57,000 British soldiers were killed or wounded. Tanks were used in battle for the first time here.
Cambrai. Nearly 400 British tanks attacked together here, breaking through the German trenches for a short time.
Amiens. Allied tanks, planes and infantry worked together and broke the German lines. A German general called it “the black day of the German Army”.
Warsaw. German bombs destroyed most of the city in World War II; Poles rebuilt the Old Town brick by brick. In 1955 the Soviet bloc signed the Warsaw Pact here.
Dunkirk. Today: Dunkerque, France. Trapped by the German army, about 338,000 British and French soldiers were carried across the Channel by warships and hundreds of small boats.
Stalingrad. Today: Volgograd, Russia. The Red Army fought street by street on the Volga and trapped a whole German army. It was the turning point of the war in Europe.
Pearl Harbor. Japan’s surprise air attack sank or damaged many US warships, and the next day the USA entered the war. The USS Arizona Memorial floats above the sunken ship.
Washington, D.C.. Twenty-six countries, including China, the USA, Britain and the USSR, signed the Declaration by United Nations here, promising to fight the Axis together.
Midway. US planes sank four Japanese aircraft carriers here in one day — the turning point of the war in the Pacific.
Kunming. American and Chinese pilots flew supplies here over the Himalayas (“the Hump”) from India, and the Flying Tigers fought from its airfields.
El Alamein. In the desert west of Alexandria, British and Commonwealth troops beat Rommel’s German and Italian army and saved the Suez Canal.
Auschwitz-Birkenau. Today: Oświęcim, Poland. The largest Nazi camp, where about 1.1 million people, most of them Jews, were murdered. It is now a memorial so that the world remembers.
Normandy. In 1066 Duke William sailed from here to conquer England. On D-Day about 156,000 Allied troops landed on its beaches to free France.
Berlin. The Soviet Red Army took Hitler’s capital in 1945. Then the city was split, the West kept alive by the 1948 airlift, and divided by a wall from 1961 to 1989.
Hiroshima. The first atomic bomb used in war destroyed the city. The ruined dome of one building was kept as a Peace Memorial.
Nagasaki. Three days after Hiroshima, a second atomic bomb hit Nagasaki. Its Peace Park calls for a world without nuclear weapons.
Tokyo Bay. On the deck of the battleship USS Missouri, Japan signed the Instrument of Surrender. China’s representative signed for the Allies too.
Yalta. Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin met in the Livadia Palace here to plan the end of the war and the founding of the United Nations.
Tempelhof Airport. Today: Tempelhofer Feld park, Berlin. When the USSR blocked the roads into West Berlin, Western planes landed here every few minutes with food and coal. Today the old runways are a huge park.
Berlin Wall. East Germany built the wall overnight to stop its people escaping to the West. In 1989 crowds climbed on it at the Brandenburg Gate and began to knock it down.
Checkpoint Charlie. The best-known crossing point between East and West Berlin. In 1961 American and Soviet tanks stood just metres apart here for 16 hours.
Frankfurt. Today: Frankfurt am Main, Germany. Many Berlin Airlift flights took off from here. Today the European Central Bank, which looks after the euro, has its tower in Frankfurt.
Panmunjom. The armistice that stopped the fighting in the Korean War was signed here. Soldiers of North and South still stand face to face in its blue huts.
San Cristóbal, Cuba. A US spy plane photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites near this town. For 13 days the world feared a nuclear war.
Saigon. Today: Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. North Vietnam took Saigon in 1975, ending the Vietnam War and reuniting the country. The city was renamed Ho Chi Minh City.
San Francisco. Delegates of 50 countries met here and signed the Charter of the United Nations. China was the first to sign.
Luxembourg. Six countries put their coal and steel under one shared authority, which met here — the first step towards today’s European Union.
Maastricht. The treaty signed here created the European Union and planned a shared currency, the euro.
Doha. At a WTO meeting here, members approved China joining the World Trade Organization. China became a member on 11 December 2001.
Astana. In a speech here in 2013, China proposed building a new “Silk Road Economic Belt” across Asia to Europe — the “Belt” of the Belt and Road.
Jakarta. A month after Astana, China proposed a “21st-Century Maritime Silk Road” in Jakarta — the “Road” of the Belt and Road.
Suez Canal. A 193-km canal joining the Mediterranean and the Red Sea, so ships between Europe and Asia need not sail around Africa. In 2021 one stuck ship held up about 400 others.
Panama Canal. Locks lift ships 26 m up over the hills of Panama and down again, linking the Pacific and the Atlantic. Many ships from Asia to Savannah pass through it.
Port of Savannah. Giant ships from Asia unload containers here, and trucks and trains carry the goods on to Atlanta and across the South-East.
Port of Los Angeles. Together with its neighbour Long Beach, it handles a big share of the containers coming into the USA, many of them from China.
Atlanta. A transport hub of the American South-East with one of the world’s busiest airports. Goods from Savannah reach it by truck in about four hours.
Mombasa. Goods for much of East Africa arrive here. A new Chinese-built railway, opened in 2017, links the port to Nairobi.
Longyangxia. A dam on the upper Yellow River next to one of the biggest solar parks in the world — millions of panels on the Qinghai plateau.
Grand Canyon. About 446 km long and up to 1.8 km deep. Its walls show rock layers almost 2 billion years old, like pages in a history book.
Colorado River. It flows about 2,300 km from the Rocky Mountains to Mexico. Over about 6 million years it cut the Grand Canyon, grinding the rock with sand and stones.
Isthmus of Panama. A narrow strip of land, only about 50 km wide at its narrowest, joining North and South America. The Panama Canal cuts through it.
Andes. The longest mountain range on land — about 7,000 km down the whole west side of South America. Its highest peak, Aconcagua, is 6,961 m.
Amazon River. The river with the most water on Earth, flowing about 6,400 km east to the Atlantic. Its basin holds the world’s largest rainforest.
Great Rift Valley. A giant crack in the Earth’s crust running thousands of kilometres through East Africa. The continent is very slowly pulling apart here.
Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon. The deepest canyon on Earth (over 5,000 m in places), where the Yarlung Zangbo River bends around Namcha Barwa peak in Xizang.
Qinghai Lake. China’s largest lake, a salty lake about 3,200 m up on the plateau. Every summer migrating birds nest on its islands.
Mount Fuji. Japan’s highest mountain (3,776 m), a volcano with an almost perfect cone. It last erupted in 1707.
Monument Valley. Red flat-topped mesas and buttes on the Arizona–Utah border, in the land of the Navajo Nation. Hard rock on top protected them while the land around wore away.
Blue Ridge Mountains. Part of the old, worn-down Appalachians. In north Georgia they hold the state’s highest point, Brasstown Bald (1,458 m).
Stone Mountain. A huge dome of granite near Atlanta, rising about 250 m above Georgia’s Piedmont hills.
Niagara Falls. Three waterfalls on the border of the USA and Canada, between Lake Erie and Lake Ontario. Every second about 2,800 tonnes of water pour over them.
Geirangerfjord. A long, narrow arm of the sea in Norway between cliffs, carved by glaciers in the Ice Age. Waterfalls tumble down its walls.
Lena Delta. Where Siberia’s Lena River splits into hundreds of channels before reaching the Arctic Ocean. It is frozen for most of the year.
Rocky Mountains. The “backbone” of western North America, running about 4,800 km from Canada to New Mexico. Many peaks rise above 4,000 m.
Great Plains. A huge flat grassland east of the Rocky Mountains. Today it is one of the world’s biggest wheat and corn regions.
North European Plain. A wide, flat lowland stretching from France across Germany and Poland toward Russia. Fertile farms and big cities fill it.
Great Lakes. Five linked lakes on the US–Canada border — Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie and Ontario. Together they hold about a fifth of the world’s fresh surface water.
Lake Baikal. The deepest lake on Earth (1,642 m) and the oldest, about 25 million years old, in Siberia, Russia. It holds more fresh water than all five Great Lakes together.
Lake Victoria. Africa’s largest lake, shared by Uganda, Kenya and Tanzania. It is a main source of the White Nile.
Caspian Sea. Despite its name it is a lake — the largest on Earth, with salty water. It lies on the border between Europe and Asia.
Mississippi River. With the Missouri it forms one of the longest river systems in the world, draining the middle of the USA into the Gulf of Mexico.
Chattahoochee River. Georgia’s best-known river. It starts in the Blue Ridge Mountains, flows past Atlanta and gives the city most of its drinking water.
Tallulah Gorge. A gorge about 300 m deep in north-east Georgia, cut by the Tallulah River. A suspension bridge swings over it.
Yosemite Valley. A U-shaped valley in California’s Sierra Nevada, carved by Ice Age glaciers, with giant granite cliffs such as El Capitan and Half Dome.
Deccan Plateau. A large plateau covering most of southern India, built of ancient lava flows. Its black soil is good for growing cotton.
Greenland. The world’s largest island (about 2.2 million km²), mostly buried under an ice sheet up to 3 km thick. Only about 57,000 people live there, on the coast.
Madagascar. The fourth-largest island in the world, off south-east Africa. Most of its animals, like lemurs, live nowhere else on Earth.
Hawaii. A chain of volcanic islands in the middle of the Pacific, the 50th US state. On the Big Island, Kīlauea is one of the most active volcanoes on Earth.
Korean Peninsula. A mountainous peninsula between the Yellow Sea and the Sea of Japan, home to North Korea and South Korea.
Iberian Peninsula. Europe’s south-western corner, shared by Spain and Portugal. The Pyrenees cut it off from France.
Strait of Hormuz. The narrow sea passage (about 40 km wide at its narrowest) out of the Persian Gulf. About a fifth of the world’s oil passes through it on tankers.
Gulf of Mexico. A big gulf of the Atlantic Ocean ringed by the USA, Mexico and Cuba. Its warm water feeds hurricanes and the Gulf Stream.
Gulf of Guinea. The great bend of Africa’s west coast, where the equator meets the prime meridian (0°, 0°) in the open sea.
Bay of Bengal. The world’s largest bay, east of India. The Ganges and Brahmaputra pour into it, and strong cyclones often form here.
Hudson Bay. A huge, shallow sea in northern Canada that freezes over every winter. Polar bears wait on its shores for the ice to come back.
Strait of Gibraltar. Only about 14 km wide, it separates Europe (Spain) from Africa (Morocco) and joins the Atlantic to the Mediterranean.
Bering Strait. About 82 km wide, between Russia (Asia) and Alaska (North America). In the Ice Age it was dry land that people and animals walked across.
Mediterranean Sea. A nearly closed sea between Europe, Africa and Asia. Its name means “sea in the middle of the land”; Egypt, Greece and Rome all grew up on its shores.
Caribbean Sea. A warm sea of the Atlantic dotted with islands such as Cuba, Hispaniola and Jamaica. Hurricanes cross it from June to November.
Nile Delta. A green fan of farmland where the Nile reaches the Mediterranean. About 39 million people live here, with desert all around.
Ganges–Brahmaputra Delta. The largest delta on Earth, mostly in Bangladesh. Its Sundarbans mangrove forest is home to Bengal tigers.
Ural Mountains. Old, low mountains running about 2,500 km north to south across Russia. They are the usual boundary between Europe and Asia.
Caucasus Mountains. A high range between the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea, often used as part of the Europe–Asia boundary. Mount Elbrus (5,642 m) is its highest peak.
Maldives. About 1,200 tiny coral islands in the Indian Ocean. Most land is less than 1.5 m above the sea, so rising seas are a real danger.
Mount Kilimanjaro. Africa’s highest mountain (5,895 m), a volcano in Tanzania. It has glaciers on top even though it is close to the equator.
Denali. North America’s highest mountain (6,190 m), in Alaska. Its name means “the high one” in the local Koyukon language.
Uluru (Ayers Rock). A giant sandstone rock, 348 m high, in the dry Red Centre of Australia. It is sacred to the Anangu people and glows red at sunset.
Sahara. The largest hot desert in the world, almost as big as the whole United States. Only a small part is sand dunes; most is rock and gravel.
Sahel. A dry belt of grassland and thorny bushes along the southern edge of the Sahara. Drought and overuse can turn it into desert, so countries are planting a “Great Green Wall” of trees.
Lake Chad. A shallow lake on the edge of the Sahel that has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s, because of drought and water taken for farming.
Congo River. Africa’s river with the most water — and the deepest river in the world (over 220 m). It crosses the equator twice as it arcs through the rainforest.
Niger River. West Africa’s great river. It flows in a big curve through Mali — past Timbuktu at the edge of the Sahara — and ends in the oil-rich Niger Delta in Nigeria.
Atlas Mountains. A mountain wall in north-west Africa (Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia) that blocks moist sea air, so the Sahara begins just south of it.
Kalahari Desert. A dry sandy region in southern Africa, mostly in Botswana. The San people have lived here as hunters and gatherers for thousands of years.
Jordan River. A short river that flows south into the Dead Sea, the lowest place on land (about 430 m below sea level). Several countries share its scarce water.
Arabian Sea. The part of the Indian Ocean between Arabia and India. Monsoon winds over it bring India its summer rain.
Mekong River. It starts on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau in China, where it is called the Lancang, and flows through six countries. Its delta in Vietnam is one of Asia’s great rice bowls.
Sea of Japan. The sea between the Korean Peninsula, Russia and the islands of Japan.
Gobi Desert. A cold desert across southern Mongolia and northern China, with freezing winters and hot summers. Many dinosaur fossils have been found here.
Lagos (Nigeria). Nigeria’s biggest city and port, on the Gulf of Guinea — one of the fastest-growing cities in Africa, with over 15 million people.
Riyadh. Capital of Saudi Arabia, an oasis city in the middle of the Arabian desert. Summer days often pass 45 °C.
Jeddah. Saudi Arabia’s main port on the Red Sea. Millions of pilgrims pass through it on their way to Mecca.
Osaka. Japan’s second big city region, a port and trading city for over 1,000 years, squeezed onto a narrow coastal plain.
New Delhi. Capital of India. Greater Delhi has over 30 million people and is one of the world’s largest city areas.
Atacama Desert. One of the driest places on Earth, in northern Chile, in the rain shadow of the Andes. Some weather stations there have never recorded rain.
Sierra Madre. Two long ranges, the Sierra Madre Occidental (west) and Oriental (east), that frame Mexico’s high central plateau.
Mexico City. Capital of Mexico, built on the old Aztec capital Tenochtitlan, 2,240 m up in a mountain valley. About 22 million people live in its metro area.
Lake Maracaibo. A large lake in Venezuela, joined to the Caribbean, sitting on top of one of the world’s biggest oil fields.
Canadian Shield. Very old rock scraped bare by Ice Age glaciers, wrapped around Hudson Bay. It has thin soil and countless lakes, but is rich in minerals.
St. Lawrence River. It carries water from the Great Lakes about 1,200 km to the Atlantic. Ships use it as a water highway into the middle of North America.
Havana. Capital of Cuba and the biggest city in the Caribbean, famous for its colourful old town and seaside road, the Malecón.
Caracas. Capital of Venezuela, in a cool mountain valley about 900 m up, just inland from the Caribbean coast.
São Paulo. Brazil’s biggest city and the largest city in the Southern Hemisphere, with about 22 million people in its metro area.
Rio de Janeiro. Brazil’s famous harbour city between green mountains and beaches, with the Christ the Redeemer statue on Corcovado hill.
Machu Picchu. An Inca city built in the 1400s on a ridge about 2,430 m high in the Andes of Peru, with farming terraces cut into the steep slopes.
Chichén Itzá. A great Maya city on Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula. Its step pyramid, El Castillo, has 365 steps — one for each day of the year.
Lake Titicaca. A lake about 3,800 m up in the Andes on the Peru–Bolivia border, often called the highest navigable lake in the world.
Iguazu Falls. About 275 waterfalls on the border between Brazil and Argentina. The biggest drop is called the “Devil’s Throat”.
Banff National Park. Canada’s first national park, in the Rocky Mountains of Alberta, with turquoise glacier lakes such as Moraine Lake.
Torres del Paine. Granite towers in Patagonia, in the far south of Chile, near the cold southern tip of the Andes.
Rhine River. It flows about 1,230 km from the Alps to the North Sea through Switzerland, Germany and the Netherlands — Europe’s busiest shipping river.
Danube River. It flows east about 2,850 km through 10 countries — more than any other river — and four capitals, including Vienna and Budapest, to the Black Sea.
English Channel. The strip of sea between Britain and France, only about 33 km wide at the Strait of Dover. Trains cross under it in the Channel Tunnel.
Pyrenees. A mountain wall about 430 km long between France and Spain. The tiny country of Andorra sits high in its valleys.
Scandinavian Peninsula. Europe’s largest peninsula, home to Norway and Sweden: deep fjords on the Atlantic side, gentler land on the Baltic side.
Chernobyl. In 1986 a reactor at the nuclear power plant here exploded, spreading radioactive dust over much of Europe. A 30-km zone around it is still closed.
Black Forest. Wooded mountains in south-west Germany. In the 1980s acid rain killed many of its trees, which led to cleaner power plants and cars.
North Sea. A shallow, stormy part of the Atlantic between Britain, Norway, Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands, with oil and gas under its floor.
Black Sea. An inland sea between Europe and Asia, joined to the Mediterranean through the narrow Bosporus at Istanbul. The Danube flows into it.
Baltic Sea. A cold, not very salty sea in northern Europe, between Scandinavia and the North European Plain. Parts of it freeze in winter.
Po Valley. The flat, fertile plain of the Po River in northern Italy, between the Alps and the Apennines — Italy’s farming and factory heartland.
Ruhr. Germany’s old coal and steel region along the Rhine, with cities such as Essen, Dortmund and Duisburg. Many old mines are now parks and museums.
Great Barrier Reef. The largest coral reef system on Earth, stretching about 2,300 km off Queensland, Australia. It can be seen from space.
Coral Sea. The warm sea off north-east Australia, named for its many coral reefs, including the Great Barrier Reef.
Great Victoria Desert. Australia’s largest desert, a land of red sand dunes and spinifex grass in the south of the continent.
Great Dividing Range. Mountains running about 3,500 km down Australia’s east coast. They catch moist Pacific air, so the coast is rainy and the inland is dry.
Sydney. Australia’s biggest city, on a beautiful harbour, famous for its Opera House and Harbour Bridge.
Melbourne. Australia’s second-largest city, on Port Phillip Bay in the south-east. It was the country’s capital from 1901 to 1927.
Brisbane. The capital of Queensland on Australia’s sunny east coast, a gateway to the Great Barrier Reef.
Brussels. Capital of Belgium and home of the main offices of the European Union.
Madrid. Capital of Spain, on a high plateau in the middle of the Iberian Peninsula.
Matterhorn. A sharp pyramid-shaped peak (4,478 m) in the Alps on the Swiss–Italian border.
Kinderdijk. Nineteen windmills built in the 1700s to pump water off the low fields of the Netherlands, about a quarter of which lies below sea level.
Aoraki / Mount Cook. New Zealand’s highest mountain (3,724 m), in the Southern Alps of the South Island.
Milford Sound. Despite its name it is a fjord, a deep sea inlet carved by glaciers, in New Zealand’s Fiordland, with cliffs rising straight out of the water.
River Thames. London’s river. The city grew up here because ships could sail in from the North Sea; Tower Bridge has crossed it since 1894.
Quito. Capital of Ecuador, almost exactly on the equator but 2,850 m up in the Andes, so it is about 14 °C all year — “eternal spring”.
La Paz. Bolivia’s seat of government, about 3,600 m up in the Andes — the highest capital city in the world. Cable cars are part of its public transport.
Manaus. A city of about 2 million people in the middle of the Amazon rainforest, near where the dark Rio Negro meets the Amazon.
Mumbai. India’s biggest port and money capital, on the Arabian Sea. Most of its yearly rain falls in the summer monsoon.
Alice Springs. A desert town in the very middle of Australia, almost on the Tropic of Capricorn. It gets less than 300 mm of rain a year.
Atlanta. The capital of Georgia, at about 33.7°N, 84.4°W. It has hot, humid summers and mild winters — a humid subtropical climate.
Chicago. The third-largest US city, on Lake Michigan. Far from any ocean, it has hot summers and freezing winters — a continental climate.
Utqiaġvik (Barrow). The northernmost town in the USA, on the Arctic Ocean in Alaska. In winter the Sun does not rise for about two months.
Nuuk. The capital of Greenland and its biggest town, with about 20,000 people on the ice-free south-west coast.
Buenos Aires. Capital of Argentina, on the wide Río de la Plata. Its seasons are flipped: Christmas comes in summer.
Royal Observatory, Greenwich. Longitude 0° — the prime meridian — runs through this observatory in London. World time (UTC) is counted from here.
International Date Line. A line in the Pacific, mostly along 180° longitude, where the calendar date changes. Cross it going west and you skip ahead one day.
Kiritimati (Christmas Island). A coral atoll in Kiribati that uses UTC+14 — one of the first places on Earth to start each new day.
Dubai. A city of skyscrapers in the United Arab Emirates, built in the desert with money from oil and trade. Its Burj Khalifa (828 m) is the tallest building in the world.
Reykjavík. Capital of Iceland, the northernmost capital of a country, at about 64°N. It uses UTC all year round.
Accra. Capital of Ghana, on the Gulf of Guinea. It lies very close to the prime meridian, so it uses UTC±0.
Los Angeles. The second-largest US city, on the Pacific coast of California. It is 3 hours behind Atlanta.
Honolulu. The capital of Hawaii, on the island of Oahu in the middle of the Pacific. It uses UTC−10, 5 hours behind Atlanta in winter.
Anchorage. Alaska’s biggest city. In June the Sun shines for over 19 hours a day; in December for less than 6.
Auckland. New Zealand’s biggest city, built on dozens of old volcanoes. It is one of the first big cities to see each new day.
Bangkok. Capital of Thailand, on the delta of the Chao Phraya River, famous for its temples and canals.
Denver. The “Mile-High City” at the foot of the Rocky Mountains in Colorado, exactly one mile (1,609 m) above sea level.
New York City. The biggest city in the USA, with about 19 million people in its metro area. When the New Year’s ball drops in Times Square, it is already the next morning in Beijing.
Tromsø. A city in northern Norway at about 69.7°N, north of the Arctic Circle. In summer the Sun never sets (midnight sun); in winter it does not rise for weeks (polar night).
Arctic Circle (Rovaniemi). The Arctic Circle (about 66.5°N) crosses this town in Finnish Lapland; a line on the ground lets you step across it. North of it the Sun stays up all night at least once a year.
Tropic of Cancer marker, Ruisui. A marker on the Tropic of Cancer (about 23.4°N) in Ruisui, Hualien County, Taiwan Province, China. Around June 21 the noon Sun is straight overhead here.
Tropic of Capricorn sign, Namibia. A road sign in the Namib Desert, Namibia, marking the Tropic of Capricorn (about 23.4°S). Around December 21 the noon Sun is straight overhead here.
Equator monument, Kikorongo. A monument in Kasese District, Uganda, where the road crosses the equator (0° latitude).
Mitad del Mundo. “The middle of the world”: an equator monument just north of Quito, Ecuador. One foot in each hemisphere!
Fairbanks. A town in central Alaska at about 65°N, just south of the Arctic Circle. Around the summer solstice the Sun barely sets.
Lima. Capital of Peru, on the Pacific coast. It is a desert city: it almost never rains, but sea fog is common.
Nairobi. Capital of Kenya, just south of the equator but about 1,700 m high, so it is mild all year. Wild animals roam a national park at the city’s edge.
Cape Town. A port city near the southern tip of Africa, below flat-topped Table Mountain.
Seoul. Capital of South Korea, on the Han River. About half of all South Koreans live in its metro area.
Dhaka. Capital of Bangladesh, on the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta — one of the most crowded cities on Earth.
Heihe. A city on the Heilongjiang River in north-east China, facing Russia. It is the northern end of the “Hu Line” that divides China’s crowded east from its emptier west.
Tengchong. A city in western Yunnan, famous for its volcanoes and hot springs. It is the southern end of the “Hu Line”.
Ellis Island. From 1892 to 1954 about 12 million immigrants entered the USA through the station on this island in New York Harbor.
Site where the 1954 Constitution was drafted. China’s first Constitution was drafted in this house beside West Lake. Today it is a museum about the history of the Constitution.
Xinhua Gate (Zhongnanhai). The main gate of Zhongnanhai on Chang’an Avenue in Beijing, where the State Council has its offices.
Supreme People’s Court (building). The highest court of the People’s Republic of China, in Beijing. It is the country’s highest trial organ and supervises the courts below it.
Supreme People’s Procuratorate (building). The country’s highest legal supervision organ, in Beijing. Procurators bring criminal cases to court and check that laws are carried out correctly.
Heilongjiang Provincial People’s Government. The government of Heilongjiang, China’s northernmost province, in its capital Harbin. Local governments carry out laws and run services in their area.
People’s Procuratorate of Tianqiao District, Jinan. A district-level procuratorate in Jinan, capital of Shandong. Procuratorates exist at every level, from the county up to the Supreme People’s Procuratorate.
Huade County. A county in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. Its supervisory commission, like those at every level, checks that public officials obey the law.
CPPCC National Committee (offices). Offices of the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference in Beijing. Its members meet every March, at the same time as the NPC.
Shilin Yi Autonomous County. Today: Shilin Yi Autonomous County, Kunming, Yunnan. A county where the Yi people practise regional ethnic autonomy. It is famous for the Stone Forest, a “forest” of limestone pillars.
Xipu Village, Shouning. A village in Shouning County in the hills of north-east Fujian. Its villagers’ committee is elected by the villagers to run village affairs.
Changning County. A county in Baoshan, western Yunnan. Its museum keeps a 1954 certificate for a deputy elected to the Yunnan Provincial People’s Congress.
Ditan Park (Temple of Earth). A Ming–Qing altar in Beijing where emperors made offerings to the Earth. Every Spring Festival it holds one of the city’s best-known temple fairs.
Lei Feng Memorial. A museum in Lei Feng’s home town in Wangcheng District, Changsha, Hunan, about the young soldier (1940–1962) remembered for helping others.
Guangling County. A county in Datong, Shanxi. Shanxi was long known for coal; today wind turbines also turn on its hills.
Beijing South Railway Station. A huge high-speed rail station opened in 2008; the Beijing–Shanghai and Beijing–Tianjin lines start here.
Guangzhou Baiyun International Airport. One of China’s busiest airports, serving Guangzhou and the Pearl River Delta.
Mombasa–Nairobi Railway. About 480 km from the port of Mombasa to Kenya’s capital Nairobi, built by a Chinese company. It cut the trip from over 10 hours to about 5.
China–Laos Railway. 1,035 km from Kunming in China to Vientiane, capital of Laos, through 167 tunnels. It gives land-locked Laos a fast link to ports and markets.
Jakarta–Bandung High-Speed Railway. Southeast Asia’s first high-speed line: 142 km in Indonesia at up to 350 km/h, built jointly by Indonesian and Chinese companies.
Boao. A seaside town in Hainan where the Boao Forum for Asia meets every year to discuss Asia’s development.
Khorgos. A “dry port” on the China–Kazakhstan border. The two countries’ railway tracks are different widths, so containers are lifted from one train to another here.
Almaty. Kazakhstan’s largest city, below the snowy Tian Shan mountains. In 2013 the “Silk Road Economic Belt” was first proposed in Kazakhstan.
Tehran. Capital of Iran, at the foot of the Alborz Mountains, on the land bridge between Central Asia and the Mediterranean.
Istanbul. The city where Asia meets Europe, on both sides of the Bosporus strait. Trains now cross under the strait by tunnel.
Duisburg. One of the world’s largest inland ports, where the Ruhr meets the Rhine in Germany. Many China–Europe freight trains end here.
Rotterdam. Europe’s largest seaport, in the Netherlands, where the Rhine reaches the North Sea.
Kuala Lumpur. Capital of Malaysia, near the Strait of Malacca, with the 452-m Petronas Twin Towers.
Colombo. Sri Lanka’s main port, in the middle of the Indian Ocean shipping lanes, with a container terminal built with Chinese investment.
Piraeus. The port of Athens, Greece’s biggest. A Chinese shipping company has run its container terminals since 2009, and it became one of the Mediterranean’s busiest.
Kia Georgia plant. A car factory in West Point, Georgia, opened in 2009. Robots and assembly lines — capital goods — help thousands of workers build hundreds of thousands of cars a year.
Greene County, Georgia. A rural county in Georgia’s Piedmont. In the 1930s and 1940s many farmers here still ploughed cotton fields with mules.
Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport. For most years since 1998 the world’s busiest airport by passengers. Planes and cargo from here link Georgia with the whole world.
Nanjing South Railway Station. One of Asia’s largest railway stations, on the Beijing–Shanghai high-speed line. Stations, rails and trains are capital that helps an economy grow.
Port of Savannah. One of the busiest container ports in the USA. Its Garden City Terminal is the largest single container terminal in North America.
Port of Brunswick. Georgia’s second deep-water port, one of the busiest US ports for shipping cars and trucks on “roll-on/roll-off” ships.
Yiwu. A city in Zhejiang with one of the world’s largest wholesale markets for small goods — about 75,000 booths selling toys, socks, decorations and more to buyers from around the world.
Dalton, Georgia. The “Carpet Capital of the World” in north-west Georgia: factories here make a large share of the carpet sold in the USA.
Port of Los Angeles. The busiest container port in the USA. Many ships from China and the rest of Asia unload here.
Canton Fair Complex. The China Import and Export Fair (Canton Fair) is held here in Guangzhou every spring and autumn. For decades it was China’s main window for selling goods abroad.
Amsterdam. Capital of the Netherlands, a city of canals. In the 1600s and 1700s Dutch ships brought Chinese porcelain, tea and silk here.
Ngorongoro Crater. A huge collapsed volcano in Tanzania, about 20 km across, full of wildlife. Maasai herders graze their cattle in the area.
New York Stock Exchange. The world’s largest stock exchange, on Wall Street in New York. Every trading day buyers and sellers set the prices of thousands of companies’ shares.
Maple Grove, Minnesota. A suburb of Minneapolis, Minnesota. On Black Friday, sale prices and limited stock make shoppers line up before dawn.
City of Manchester Stadium. A stadium in Manchester, England, with about 53,000 seats — a fixed supply, whatever the demand for tickets.
Tsukiji fish market. Tokyo’s famous fish market, where early-morning tuna auctions set prices. The wholesale market moved to nearby Toyosu in 2018.
Chicago Board of Trade Building. A 1930 skyscraper in Chicago where traders buy and sell contracts for corn, wheat and soybeans. A statue of Ceres, goddess of grain, stands on top.
Crozet, Virginia. A small town in Virginia where, in June 2008, gas passed $4 a gallon for the first time — world oil demand had grown faster than supply.
Grand Bazaar, Istanbul. One of the largest and oldest covered markets in the world, with over 4,000 shops under its domed roofs.
Eccles Building (Federal Reserve). Headquarters of the Federal Reserve, the central bank of the United States, in Washington, D.C.
Shanghai Stock Exchange. One of the largest stock markets in the world, in Pudong, Shanghai.
La Viñuela reservoir. A reservoir near Málaga in southern Spain that almost dried up in a long drought — so farms, towns and factories had to choose who got the water.
Mountain Pass mine. A rare-earth mine in the Mojave Desert, California — the only big one in the USA. Rare earths go into phone speakers, electric-car motors and wind turbines.
Great Green Wall (Sahel). A project started in 2007 by African countries to plant a belt of trees and grassland about 8,000 km long across the Sahel, from Senegal to Djibouti, to hold back the desert.
Tian’anmen. On 1 October 1949 Chairman Mao Zedong stood on this gate tower and proclaimed the founding of the People’s Republic of China. The square in front can hold about a million people.
Yalu River. In October 1950 the Chinese People’s Volunteers crossed the Yalu at Dandong to resist US aggression and aid Korea.
Tibet (Xizang). Tibet lies on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, the “roof of the world”, on average over 4,000 m high. Its capital is Lhasa.
Lhasa. Lhasa is about 3,650 m above sea level and has so many sunny days that it is called the “City of Sunlight”. The Potala Palace towers over the city.
Anshan Steel. Today: Anshan, Liaoning. In 1953 three big new works at Anshan started producing — the beginning of New China’s steel industry.
Changchun. In 1956 the First Automobile Works in Changchun built New China’s first trucks, the “Jiefang” (Liberation).
Shenyang. Shenyang is a big industrial city. Its aircraft works built New China’s first jet fighter in 1956, and the Japanese invasion began here on 18 September 1931.
Wuhan. Wuhan grew from three towns — Wuchang, Hankou and Hanyang. It is called the “thoroughfare of nine provinces” because rivers and railways meet here.
Daqing Oilfield. Today: Daqing, Heilongjiang. Workers led by “Iron Man” Wang Jinxi drilled for oil here in freezing cold. By 1963 China could supply almost all its own oil.
Lop Nur. Lop Nur was once a big salt lake in Xinjiang; today it is a dry, salty basin. Scientists tested China’s first atomic bomb in this remote desert.
Jiuquan. Legend says a Han general poured the emperor’s wine into a spring here to share it with his soldiers — “wine spring”. The Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center lies in the desert to the northeast.
Xiaogang Village. Today: Fengyang County, Anhui. In 1978, 18 farming families secretly signed a contract with red fingerprints to farm their own plots. Harvests jumped, and the idea spread across China.
Shenzhen. In 1980 Shenzhen was a small border town next to Hong Kong. As a Special Economic Zone it grew into a high-tech city of over 17 million people.
Zhuhai. Zhuhai sits next to Macao. The Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge starts here.
Shantou. A port in eastern Guangdong. Many overseas Chinese families have their roots here.
Xiamen. Xiamen is an island city in Fujian facing Taiwan across the strait. Gulangyu Island, with no cars, is famous for its pianos.
Hainan Island. China’s second-largest island, warm all year round. It became a province and the biggest Special Economic Zone in 1988.
Pudong. “Pudong” means “east of the Huangpu River”. In 1990 it was mostly farmland; today the towers of Lujiazui stand here.
Hong Kong. Britain took Hong Kong after the Opium Wars. China resumed sovereignty in 1997 under “one country, two systems”.
Macao. Portugal occupied Macao for a long time. It returned in 1999; its Historic Centre is a World Heritage Site.
Lankao. Lankao, by the old Yellow River in Henan, suffered from sandstorms, floods and salty soil. County leader Jiao Yulu led people to plant paulownia trees to hold the sand.
Red Flag Canal. Today: Linzhou (Lin County), Henan. People of Lin County cut a 1,500 km canal network along the cliffs of the Taihang Mountains, mostly with hammers and chisels, to bring water from the Zhang River.
Taihang Mountains. The Taihang range runs about 400 km north–south, between the Loess Plateau and the North China Plain.
Dalian. Dalian is at the tip of the Liaodong Peninsula, a big port facing the Yellow Sea and the Bohai.
Qinhuangdao. Named after the First Emperor of Qin, who is said to have visited. Shanhaiguan, where the Great Wall meets the sea, is part of the city.
Yantai. A port on the north coast of the Shandong Peninsula, famous for its apples.
Qingdao. A big port on the Yellow Sea in Shandong, known for its red-roofed old town and beaches.
Lianyungang. A port in northern Jiangsu; trains from here cross Asia to Europe. Its Huaguo Mountain is linked with the Monkey King story.
Nantong. Nantong lies on the north bank of the Yangtze near its mouth, just across from Shanghai.
Ningbo. Ningbo–Zhoushan Port handles more cargo by weight than any other port in the world.
Wenzhou. Wenzhou in southern Zhejiang is famous for its small family businesses that sell goods all over the world.
Fuzhou. Fuzhou, the capital of Fujian, sits on the Min River near the sea.
Guangzhou. Guangzhou has traded by sea since the Qin and Han. In the Tang and Song it was a starting point of the Maritime Silk Road.
Zhanjiang. A port on the Leizhou Peninsula, at the southern tip of mainland Guangdong.
Beihai. Beihai in Guangxi faces the Beibu Gulf. In the Han dynasty nearby Hepu was a Maritime Silk Road port.
Tianjin. Tianjin is Beijing’s gateway to the sea, on the Bohai. Since 2008 high-speed trains link the two cities in about 30 minutes.
Pearl River Delta. Where the Pearl River reaches the sea, cities like Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Hong Kong and Macao form one of the world’s biggest city clusters — the Greater Bay Area.
Xianyang. Today: northeast of today’s Xianyang, Shaanxi (near Xi’an). From Xianyang the First Emperor ruled a united China after 221 BCE. The city was burned down when the Qin fell.
Lintao. Today: Min County, Gansu. The Qin wall ran about 5,000 km from Lintao in the west to Liaodong in the east.
Liaodong. Today: eastern Liaoning. Liaodong means “east of the Liao River”. The Liaodong Peninsula reaches into the sea between the Bohai and the Yellow Sea.
Dazexiang. Today: southeast of Suzhou, Anhui. Stuck in heavy rain and facing death for being late, 900 conscripts rose up here — the first big peasant uprising in Chinese history.
Julu. Today: southwest of Pingxiang, Hebei. Xiang Yu sank his boats and smashed his cooking pots so his army could not retreat, then crushed the main Qin army here.
Hexi Corridor. A 1,000-km strip of oases between the Qilian Mountains and the desert, west of the Yellow River — the main road of the Silk Road.
Wuwei (Liangzhou). The eastern gateway of the Hexi Corridor. The famous bronze “Galloping Horse Treading on a Flying Swallow” was found in a Han tomb here.
Zhangye. An oasis city at the foot of the Qilian Mountains, famous for its rainbow-coloured Danxia hills.
Dunhuang. At the western end of the Hexi Corridor the Silk Road split here into north and south roads. Nearby are the Mogao Caves.
Yang Pass. Today: southwest of Dunhuang, Gansu. With the Jade Gate (Yumen Pass), Yang Pass marked the edge of Han lands. Wang Wei’s poem says: “West of Yang Pass you will find no old friends.”
Western Regions. Today: west of the Yumen and Yang passes — mainly today’s Xinjiang and beyond. In Han times “Western Regions” meant the oasis kingdoms around the Taklamakan Desert and lands beyond. Zhang Qian’s journeys opened the way there.
Wulei (Protectorate of the Western Regions). Today: east of Luntai, Xinjiang. Setting up the Protectorate here in 60 BCE marked the Western Regions (today’s Xinjiang) officially becoming part of China’s territory.
Loulan. Today: northwest of Lop Nur, Xinjiang. Loulan was a busy oasis town by Lop Nur lake. When the water dried up the city was abandoned and buried by sand.
Qiuci (Kucha). Today: Kuqa, Xinjiang. Qiuci (said “Qiū-cí”) was famous for its music and dance, and for the Buddhist cave paintings at Kizil.
Shule (Kashgar). Today: Kashgar (Kashi), Xinjiang. The last big oasis before the Pamir mountains — caravans rested here before the hard climb west.
Yutian (Khotan). Today: Hotan, Xinjiang. Famous for its white jade, carried east to the Central Plains for thousands of years.
Gaochang. Today: southeast of Turpan, Xinjiang. Xuanzang stayed here on his way to India and the king begged him to remain. Its mud-brick ruins still stand in the hot Turpan Basin.
Pamirs (Congling). A knot of huge mountains where the Silk Road climbed over 4,000 m. China’s westernmost point is here.
Dayuan (Fergana). Today: Fergana Valley, Central Asia. Dayuan was famous for its strong, fast horses, which Emperor Wu of Han wanted very much.
Da Yuezhi. Today: Amu Darya valley, Central Asia. Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian to ask the Yuezhi to fight the Xiongnu together. They said no, but the journey opened the Silk Road.
Anxi (Parthia). Today: Iran and Iraq. The Parthians stood between Han China and Rome and earned a lot as middlemen in the silk trade.
Daqin (Roman Empire). Today: Rome, Italy. Silk reached Rome along the Silk Road and Roman nobles loved it. In 97 CE Gan Ying set out for Daqin but turned back at the Persian Gulf.
Xuwen. From Xuwen, at the southern tip of the Leizhou Peninsula, Han ships sailed to Southeast Asia and southern India.
Hepu. Glass beads, agate and amber from faraway lands have been found in the Han tombs at Hepu in Guangxi.
Persian Gulf. Gan Ying, sent by Ban Chao to find Rome, reached the Persian Gulf, the farthest west a Han envoy is known to have gone.
Qinling Mountains. Along the Qinling–Huaihe line the January average is about 0 °C and yearly rain about 800 mm. North of it people grow wheat; south of it, rice. Giant pandas live in these mountains.
Huai River. The Huai flows east between the Yellow River and the Yangtze. With the Qinling Mountains it marks the line between North and South China.
Greater Khingan Range. A long forested range in the far northeast — one of China’s biggest timber areas. West of it lies the Inner Mongolia Plateau, east of it the Northeast Plain.
Inner Mongolia Plateau. Wide, flat and about 1,000 m high, with huge grasslands such as Hulunbuir and Xilin Gol — “the sky is blue, the land is vast”.
Northeast Plain. Its fertile black soil grows soybeans, corn and rice. Winters are long and very cold.
North China Plain. Flat as a table, it is China’s main wheat-growing area. Spring droughts are a problem, so water is brought from the south.
Loess Plateau. Wind piled up yellow dust here over millions of years. Rain cuts deep gullies, and the Yellow River gets its colour from this soil. People traditionally live in cave houses (yaodong).
Middle–Lower Yangtze Plain. Low, flat and full of rivers and lakes, this plain grows rice and raises fish — it has been called the “land of fish and rice” for centuries.
Sichuan Basin. Ringed by mountains, the basin has purple soil, mild winters and lots of cloud. The Chengdu Plain inside it is one of China’s richest farm areas.
Chengdu Plain. Thanks to Dujiangyan, the Chengdu Plain has had no floods or droughts for 2,000 years and became the “Land of Abundance”.
Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau. Rugged land of limestone (karst) hills, caves and stone forests. Flat little basins called “bazi” are where people farm.
Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. The highest plateau on Earth, on average over 4,000 m. The Yangtze, Yellow and Lancang (Mekong) rivers all start here.
Tarim Basin. Between the Tian Shan and the Kunlun Mountains. Oases ring the edge, and the West–East Gas Pipeline starts here.
Tarim River. Fed by melting snow from the mountains, it flows along the edge of the Taklamakan and never reaches the sea. Desert poplar forests grow along its banks.
Taklamakan Desert. A sea of moving sand dunes inside the Tarim Basin. Silk Road caravans went around it by the northern and southern roads.
Tian Shan. Xinjiang is “three ranges holding two basins”: the Altai, the Tian Shan and the Kunlun. Snowmelt from the Tian Shan waters the oases.
Junggar Basin. North of the Tian Shan; it holds the Gurbantünggüt Desert and the Karamay oilfield.
Qaidam Basin. About 3,000 m high on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, rich in salt, oil and minerals. Its dry, red land looks like Mars.
Qarhan Salt Lake. The salt crust is so thick that a road and railway run across it.
Turpan. Turpan is one of the hottest places in China. Underground karez canals bring snowmelt from the mountains, so farmers can grow sweet grapes and melons.
Aydingkol Lake. A salty lake in the Turpan Basin, lying about 154 m below sea level.
Hetao Plain. Inside the great northern bend of the Yellow River. Canals from the river turn this dry land into fields — “the Yellow River brings disaster, but enriches Hetao”.
Ningxia Plain. Yellow River water irrigates rice paddies and orchards in the middle of a dry land, so it looks like the green south.
Jiangnan. Jiangnan means “south of the river”. Its water towns have canals instead of streets and stone bridges everywhere.
Yangtze River Delta. Flat land built from Yangtze silt, criss-crossed by rivers and canals — one of the most crowded and richest areas of China.
Zhouzhuang. A canal town near Shanghai where people travel by boat under old stone bridges.
Wuzhen. Houses stand on stilts over the canals. Today it also hosts the World Internet Conference.
Yarlung Zangbo River. Its valley is warm enough for highland barley. Downstream it cuts the Yarlung Zangbo Grand Canyon, the deepest canyon on Earth.
Sanjiangyuan. Today: southern Qinghai. The sources of the Yangtze, the Yellow River and the Lancang (Mekong) are all here, among glaciers, wetlands and wild yaks and antelopes.
Hengduan Mountains. These ranges run north–south and “cut across” east–west travel. Three great rivers flow side by side in deep valleys here.
Himalayas. An arc of snowy peaks on China’s southwest border, including Mount Qomolangma (Everest).
Mount Qomolangma (Everest). On the China–Nepal border. Its height was measured again in 2020 by Chinese and Nepali surveyors: 8,848.86 m.
Kunlun Mountains. A huge range on the northern edge of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau — called the “ancestor of mountains” in Chinese legends.
Qilian Mountains. Snowmelt from the Qilian Mountains feeds the oases of the Hexi Corridor just to the north.
Wushan Mountains. The Yangtze cuts through the Wushan Mountains to form the Wu Gorge, one of the Three Gorges.
Changbai Mountains. A sleeping volcano. Its crater holds Tianchi (Heaven Lake), the source of the Songhua, Tumen and Yalu rivers.
Stone Forest. Today: Shilin County, Yunnan. Rain slowly dissolved the limestone and left a “forest” of stone pillars, some over 30 m tall.
Yushan (Jade Mountain). The highest peak of Taiwan’s Central Mountain Range; in winter its top is often white with snow.
Xining. A plateau city about 2,260 m high. Trains to Lhasa leave from here.
Namtso. One of the highest large lakes in the world; its name means “heavenly lake” in Tibetan.
Harbin. Winters reach −30 °C, and every year Harbin builds a city of ice sculptures for its Ice and Snow Festival.
Beijing. China’s capital and its political and cultural centre. The Forbidden City, the palace of the Ming and Qing emperors, is in the middle of the city.
Shanghai. Shanghai sits at the mouth of the Yangtze. Its port moves more containers than any other in the world.
Nanjing. Nanjing has been a capital for many dynasties. In December 1937 Japanese troops murdered about 300,000 Chinese people here (the Nanjing Massacre). It was liberated on 23 April 1949.
Xi’an. Thirteen dynasties had their capital here. In the Xi’an Incident of December 1936, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng forced Chiang Kai-shek to stop the civil war and fight Japan.
Hangzhou. Hangzhou is at the southern end of the Grand Canal and is famous for West Lake.
Chongqing. A “mountain city” where the Jialing River meets the Yangtze. In 1945 Mao Zedong came here for peace talks with the Nationalists.
South Lake, Jiaxing. Today: Jiaxing, Zhejiang. The First Congress began in Shanghai but had to move. The delegates finished it on a tourist boat on South Lake — now called the “Red Boat”.
Whampoa (Huangpu) Military Academy. Today: Changzhou Island, Guangzhou. Sun Yat-sen founded this army school to train officers for the Northern Expedition against the warlords.
Nanchang. The Nanchang Uprising fired the first shot of armed resistance against the Nationalists. 1 August is now the founding day of the People’s Liberation Army.
Jinggangshan. Today: on the Jiangxi–Hunan border. Mao Zedong led his troops into these mountains. In 1928 Zhu De’s army joined them here, and the Red Army began the road of “surrounding the cities from the countryside”.
Liutiaohu, Shenyang. Japanese soldiers blew up a section of railway here and blamed Chinese troops, then attacked Shenyang. Soon all of Northeast China was occupied.
Ruijin. Today: Ruijin, Jiangxi. Called the “Red Capital”. In October 1934 the Red Army set out from Ruijin and Yudu on the Long March.
Yudu. Today: Yudu, Jiangxi. Over 80,000 Red Army soldiers crossed the Yudu River here on pontoon bridges at night to begin the Long March.
Xiang River. Today: upper reaches in northern Guangxi. The Xiang flows north to Dongting Lake. Near its source the Lingqu Canal links it to the Li River. In 1934 the Red Army broke through the enemy’s fourth blockade line here at a terrible cost.
Zunyi. Today: Zunyi, Guizhou. The Zunyi Conference made Mao Zedong’s ideas the leading line of the Red Army — a turning point that saved the Party and the Red Army.
Chishui River. Mao Zedong led the Red Army back and forth across this river four times to confuse and escape the much larger enemy forces.
Jinsha River. “River of golden sand” is the name of the upper Yangtze from Yushu to Yibin, flowing through deep gorges.
Jiaopingdu. Today: Luquan County, Yunnan (Jinsha River). With only a few small boats, the Red Army ferried its whole main force across the Jinsha here in nine days and nights.
Dadu River (Anshunchang). Today: Shimian County, Sichuan. Seventeen brave soldiers crossed the wild Dadu River by boat at Anshunchang under enemy fire.
Luding Bridge. Today: Luding, Sichuan. An iron-chain bridge built in 1706. The enemy had removed the planks; 22 soldiers climbed across the bare chains under fire and captured it.
Jiajin Mountain. Over 4,000 m high with thin air and snow. Many soldiers in thin clothes froze on the way up.
Maogong. Today: Xiaojin County, Sichuan. After crossing Jiajin Mountain, the Central Red Army met the Fourth Front Army here.
Songpan grasslands. Today: Zoigê (Ruo’ergai) wetlands, Sichuan. Huge marshes where one wrong step could pull a person into the mud. The soldiers ate wild plants and boiled their leather belts.
Lazikou Pass. Today: Têwo (Diebu) County, Gansu. A narrow gap between cliffs, only about 30 m wide — the “gateway to Gansu”. The Red Army took it in one night.
Wuqi. Today: Wuqi County, Shaanxi. After about 12,500 km (25,000 li) the Central Red Army arrived in northern Shaanxi here.
Huining. Today: Huining, Gansu. The meeting of the First, Second and Fourth Front Armies at Huining and Jiangtaibao marked the victory of the Long March.
Lugou (Marco Polo) Bridge. Today: southwest of Beijing. A stone bridge from 1192 with nearly 500 carved lions. Japanese troops attacked here in 1937, and all of China rose to resist.
Pingxingguan. Today: Lingqiu County, Shanxi. The Eighth Route Army ambushed a Japanese column in a narrow valley — the first big Chinese victory of the war.
Taierzhuang. Today: Zaozhuang, Shandong. Li Zongren’s Nationalist troops fought house by house and won a major victory over the Japanese army.
Yan’an. Today: Yan’an, Shaanxi. From Yan’an the Party led the War of Resistance. The Seventh National Congress was held here in 1945. Its pagoda on the hill is a symbol of the revolution.
Dabie Mountains. On the borders of Hubei, Henan and Anhui. Marching 1,000 li into these mountains turned the PLA from defence to attack.
Jinzhou. Today: Jinzhou, Liaoning. Taking Jinzhou closed the “door” between Northeast China and North China, so the Nationalist armies there were trapped.
Xuzhou. A crossroads city in northern Jiangsu. Millions of farmers pushed carts of food to the front in the Huaihai Campaign.
Xibaipo. Today: Pingshan County, Hebei. From this small village the Party leaders directed the three great campaigns — Liaoshen, Huaihai and Pingjin.
Bohai Sea. Almost surrounded by Liaoning, Hebei, Tianjin and Shandong. It is shallow — only about 18 m deep on average — and parts freeze in winter.
Bohai Kingdom. Today: Ning’an, Heilongjiang (capital Shangjing Longquanfu). The Tang made its ruler “King of Bohai”. Its capital was laid out like a small Chang’an.
Yellow Sea. Silt from rivers once made its water yellowish — hence the name. It is about 44 m deep on average.
East China Sea. The Yangtze flows into the East China Sea. China’s biggest fishing ground, Zhoushan, and the Diaoyu Islands are here.
South China Sea. A warm tropical sea dotted with coral islands — the South China Sea Islands.
South China Sea Islands. The Dongsha, Xisha, Zhongsha and Nansha islands — mostly small coral islands, reefs and banks.
Bohai Strait. The “door” of the Bohai. The Miaodao Islands are stepping stones across it.
Miaodao Islands. Today: Changdao, Yantai, Shandong. About 30 islands strung between Shandong and Liaodong.
Chongming Island. Built up from Yangtze silt over about 1,300 years — the world’s largest alluvial island in a river mouth.
Zhoushan Islands. Over 1,300 islands off Zhejiang. Warm and cold currents meet here, so there are lots of fish.
Yangshan Port. Today: Shanghai (on islands of Zhejiang). Shanghai built its deepest port on small islands 30 km out to sea, linked by the 32-km Donghai Bridge.
Diaoyu Islands. Diaoyu Dao and its islands, such as Chiwei Yu, lie in the East China Sea about 170 km northeast of Taiwan.
Taiwan Strait. About 130 km wide at its narrowest, between Pingtan Island in Fujian and Taiwan. Big ships sail through it every day.
Taiwan. Taiwan has been part of China since ancient times. Two-thirds of the island is mountains; the Tropic of Cancer crosses its middle.
Qiongzhou Strait. Only 20–40 km wide. Trains ride across on ferries to Haikou.
Sanya. Sanya is about 18° N — but China’s southernmost point, Zengmu Ansha, is much farther south.
Beibu Gulf. A rich fishing ground. Weizhou Island in the gulf was made by volcanoes.
Dongsha Islands. A ring-shaped coral atoll southeast of Guangdong.
Xisha Islands. Coral islands with white sand and clear water, rich in sea turtles and fish.
Zhongsha Islands. Most of the Zhongsha group is hidden under the sea; only rocks of Huangyan Island stick out.
Nansha Islands. Hundreds of islands, reefs and banks spread over a huge area of sea.
Zengmu Ansha. An underwater coral bank in the Nansha Islands, about 17 m below the sea surface.
Penghu Islands. About 90 small islands in the Taiwan Strait, dry and windy, with cliffs of dark basalt.
Mohe. In Mohe’s “Arctic Village” winters fall below −40 °C, and in summer you can sometimes see the northern lights.
Haikou. Haikou faces the Qiongzhou Strait; ferries carry trains and cars over from Guangdong.
Alishan. Famous for sunrise over a sea of clouds, giant old cypress trees and a little forest railway that climbs from Chiayi.
Sun Moon Lake. One half is round like the sun and the other curved like the moon, so it is called Sun Moon Lake. The Thao people live by its shore.
Chiayi. A marker tower shows exactly where the Tropic of Cancer crosses. The Alishan railway starts here.
Hualien. Steep mountains drop straight into the Pacific here; Taroko Gorge is nearby.
Taipei. Taipei lies in a basin in northern Taiwan. Its 508-m Taipei 101 tower was once the tallest building in the world.
Keelung. Winter monsoon winds bring rain on more than 200 days a year.
Hsinchu. Many of the world’s computer chips are made in Hsinchu.
Kaohsiung. A big harbour city in the south of Taiwan, where factories made goods for export.
Taroko Gorge. The Liwu River cut this narrow gorge through marble cliffs hundreds of metres high.
Pingtan Island. From Pingtan in Fujian it is only about 130 km across the strait to Taiwan.
Meizhou Island. Today: Putian, Fujian. People on both sides of the strait honour Mazu, goddess of the sea, who came from this island.
Victoria Harbour. One of the world’s great deep harbours. Over the years much of its edge was filled in to make land.
Hong Kong International Airport. Two small islands were flattened and joined with reclaimed land to make room for the airport.
Kai Tak. Its runway stuck out into Kowloon Bay, and planes flew very low over apartment roofs to land.
Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge. Bridges, two artificial islands and an undersea tunnel link Hong Kong, Zhuhai and Macao across the Pearl River Estuary.
Kwai Tsing Container Terminals. Giant cranes load ships with containers on land reclaimed from the sea.
Taipa, Coloane and Cotai. Macao filled in the sea between Taipa and Coloane to create Cotai, roughly doubling its land since 1912.
Ruins of St. Paul’s. Only the stone front of the church survived the fire. It is the symbol of Macao and part of its World Heritage Historic Centre.
A-Ma Temple. A temple to the sea goddess Mazu. Portuguese sailors heard the name “Ma-Gau” here — that is where the name “Macao” comes from.
Mohe-yan Desert. Today: Gobi between Guazhou (Gansu) and Hami (Xinjiang). Xuanzang spilled his water bag here and went four nights and five days without water before his horse found a spring.
Samarkand. Today: Samarkand, Uzbekistan. The Sogdians of Samarkand were the great traders of the Silk Road; many lived in Tang Chang’an.
Nalanda. Today: Bihar, India. Thousands of monks studied at Nalanda. Xuanzang became one of its most famous teachers.
Mogao Caves. Today: southeast of Dunhuang, Gansu. Over 700 caves with about 45,000 m² of wall paintings. In 1900 a hidden library cave with tens of thousands of old scrolls was found.
Yumen Pass (Jade Gate). Today: northwest of Dunhuang, Gansu. Jade from Khotan came into China through this pass, which gave it its name. Its square mud-brick fort still stands.
Yangzhou (Jiangdu). In the Sui it was called Jiangdu; in the Tang Yangzhou was one of the richest trading cities. The monk Jianzhen set out from here for Japan.
Nara (Heijō-kyō). Today: Nara, Japan. Japan copied Chang’an’s chessboard streets for its capital. Jianzhen arrived in 754 and built the Tōshōdai-ji temple here.
Liujiagang. Today: Liuhe, Taicang, Jiangsu. Zheng He’s great fleet of over 200 ships and 27,000 men gathered here before sailing.
Changle. Today: Fuzhou, Fujian. The fleet waited at Changle for the northeast winter wind to blow it south.
Palembang. Today: Palembang, Indonesia. A port on Sumatra. Zheng He’s fleet captured the pirate chief who robbed ships here.
Malacca. Today: Melaka, Malaysia. On the narrow Strait of Malacca between the Pacific and Indian oceans; Zheng He’s ships stored goods here.
Calicut. Today: Kozhikode, India. A rich spice port on India’s southwest coast — the first voyage’s farthest point.
Hormuz. Today: Strait of Hormuz, Iran. Merchants from Arabia, Persia and India met here. Both Marco Polo and Zheng He came to Hormuz.
Malindi. Today: Malindi, Kenya. The giraffe from Malindi reached Beijing in 1415; people thought it was the magical qilin.
East coast of Africa. Zheng He’s ships reached Mogadishu and Malindi on Africa’s east coast — decades before European ships sailed around Africa.
Venice. Today: Venice, Italy. A city built on islands, with canals for streets. Marco Polo left here in 1271 and came back 24 years later.
Tabriz. Today: Tabriz, Iran. Its huge covered bazaar is one of the oldest in the world.
Constantinople. Today: Istanbul, Türkiye. Silk from China reached Europe through this city between Europe and Asia.
Shangdu (Xanadu). Today: Zhenglan Banner, Inner Mongolia. Marco Polo met Kublai Khan at his summer palace here; its ruins are a World Heritage Site.
Dadu. Today: Beijing. Kublai Khan built Dadu on a neat grid plan. Parts of its earth wall survive as a park in northern Beijing.
Quanzhou. Marco Polo called it one of the greatest ports in the world. Arab, Persian and Indian merchants lived here, and a Song ship was dug up in its bay in 1974.
Jingdezhen. Named after the Song emperor’s Jingde reign. Its blue-and-white porcelain was shipped all over the world.
Qingjing Mosque. Today: Quanzhou, Fujian. One of China’s oldest mosques, built by Arab merchants who lived in Quanzhou.
Shangjing (Liao capital). Today: Bairin Left Banner, Inner Mongolia. Yelü Abaoji of the Khitan founded the Liao and built this capital on the grasslands.
Xingqing (Western Xia capital). Today: Yinchuan, Ningxia. Li Yuanhao declared himself emperor here in 1038. The great royal tombs of the Western Xia stand west of the city.
Huining (Jin capital). Today: Acheng, Harbin, Heilongjiang. Wanyan Aguda of the Jurchen founded the Jin here; within 12 years the Jin destroyed both the Liao and the Northern Song.
Chenqiao. Today: Fengqiu County, Henan. Soldiers put a yellow imperial robe on general Zhao Kuangyin here and made him emperor of the new Song dynasty.
Chanzhou (Chanyuan). Today: Puyang, Henan. After fighting here, the Song and Liao made peace; the Song paid silver and silk each year, and there was peace for over 100 years.
Yancheng. Today: Luohe, Henan. Yue Fei’s army beat the Jin’s armoured cavalry here. People said: “Easier to shake a mountain than the Yue army.”
Dasan Pass. Today: south of Baoji, Shaanxi. A mountain pass in the Qinling. After 1141 the Song–Jin border ran from the Huai River in the east to Dasan Pass in the west.
Lake Tai (Taihu). In the Southern Song people said “When Suzhou and Huzhou have a good harvest, the whole country has enough to eat.” Both cities lie by Lake Tai.
Suzhou. Suzhou is famous for its classical gardens, canals and silk.
Yingxian Wooden Pagoda. Today: Ying County, Shanxi. About 67 m tall and made entirely of wood without nails, it has stood for over 950 years and survived earthquakes.
Longquan kilns. Today: Longquan, Zhejiang. Its green celadon, like jade, was exported as far as Africa.
Gaocheng Observatory, Dengfeng. Today: Dengfeng, Henan. A brick tower used to measure the sun’s shadow. Guo Shoujing’s calendar fixed the year at 365.2425 days.
Luoyang Bridge, Quanzhou. Today: Quanzhou, Fujian. Builders grew oysters on the stones to glue the foundations together — a clever trick from 900 years ago.
Erlitou. Today: Yanshi, Luoyang, Henan. Archaeologists found palace foundations, bronze workshops and a dragon made of over 2,000 pieces of turquoise.
Yin Ruins (Yinxu). Today: Anyang, Henan. Over 150,000 oracle bones with the earliest Chinese writing were dug up here, and the 832-kg Houmuwu Ding.
Ningxiang. Today: Ningxiang, Hunan. Farmers digging a hillside found the Shang bronze “Four-Ram Square Zun”, far south of the Shang heartland.
Wei River. The Zhou people farmed the Wei valley before they defeated the Shang. Later Xianyang and Chang’an were built beside it.
Haojing. Today: southwest of Xi’an, Shaanxi. King Wu set out from Haojing to defeat the Shang at Muye (about 1046 BCE).
Muye. Today: Xinxiang, Henan. Many of the Shang soldiers turned against King Zhou; the Shang fell and the Western Zhou began.
Mengjin ford. Today: Mengjin, Henan. King Wu gathered his allies at this Yellow River ford before marching on the Shang.
Zhaoge. Today: Qi County, Henan. King Zhou of Shang lived here. After Muye the Zhou army marched into Zhaoge (said “Zhāo-gē”).
Qufu. Today: Qufu, Shandong. Confucius was born and taught here. The Temple of Confucius, the Kong family mansion and cemetery are World Heritage Sites.
Linzi. Today: Zibo, Shandong. One of the biggest cities in the world 2,300 years ago — so crowded that “raising your sleeves makes shade”.
Shangqiu. Today: Shangqiu, Henan. The Zhou gave the Shang royal family the state of Song here so they could keep honouring their ancestors.
Sanxingdui. Today: Guanghan, Sichuan. Huge bronze masks with bulging eyes and a 4-m bronze “sacred tree” show a rich civilisation in the southwest at the same time as the Shang.
Dujiangyan. Today: Dujiangyan, Sichuan. The Fish Mouth splits the Min River in two, the Feisha Weir spills floods, and the Bottle-Neck Channel lets water onto the Chengdu Plain — still working after 2,200 years.
Min River (Sichuan). Rushing out of the mountains onto the Chengdu Plain, the Min often flooded until Dujiangyan was built. It joins the Yangtze at Yibin.
Mount Qingcheng. A green, misty mountain with old Taoist temples, next to Dujiangyan.
Kuiqiu. Today: near Lankao and Minquan, Henan. Here Duke Huan of Qi called the lords together and became the first hegemon (overlord).
Chengpu. Today: southwest of Juancheng, Shandong. Jin pulled back 90 li (“retreat three days’ march”) to keep a promise, then defeated Chu here.
Changping. Today: Gaoping, Shanxi. Qin crushed the army of Zhao — the biggest battle of the Warring States. After it no state could stop Qin.
Xinzheng. Today: Xinzheng, Henan. Han (韩), the smallest of the seven Warring States, was the first that Qin conquered (230 BCE).
Handan. Today: Handan, Hebei. King Wuling of Zhao made his soldiers wear nomad trousers and ride horses here. Many Chinese idioms come from Handan stories.
Daliang. Today: Kaifeng, Henan. When Wei attacked Zhao, Sun Bin of Qi marched on Daliang instead, forcing Wei to rush home — “besiege Wei to rescue Zhao”.
Ying (Chu capital). Today: Jingzhou, Hubei. Chu was the big southern state on the Yangtze. The poet Qu Yuan served its king.
Great Wall of Qi. Today: central Shandong. Over 600 km of wall across the hills of Shandong — one of the oldest long walls in China.
Great Wall. The First Emperor joined the walls of Qin, Zhao and Yan into a wall from Lintao to Liaodong. The pin marks Badaling near Beijing, one of the best-known sections.
Lingqu Canal. Today: Xing’an, Guangxi. Linking the north-flowing Xiang River with the south-flowing Li River, it joined the Yangtze and Pearl river systems so Qin ships could reach Lingnan.
Guiling. Today: Changyuan, Henan (one view). The Wei army hurried home to save Daliang and ran into Sun Bin’s ambush here.
Guandu. Today: northeast of Zhongmou, Henan. Cao Cao, with far fewer men, burned Yuan Shao’s grain at Wuchao and won — and soon ruled all of North China.
Wuchao. Today: between Fengqiu and Yanjin, Henan. Cao Cao led 5,000 men on a night raid and set the grain on fire; Yuan Shao’s huge army collapsed.
Ye. Today: Linzhang, Hebei. After Guandu, Cao Cao took Ye and built the Bronze Sparrow Terrace here.
Liyang ford. Today: Xun County, Henan. An old ford on the Yellow River; Yuan Shao’s army crossed here to attack Cao Cao.
Xu (Xuchang). Today: Xuchang, Henan. Cao Cao brought the last Han emperor here and ruled in his name. Guandu guarded the road to Xu.
Chibi (Red Cliffs). Today: northwest of Chibi City, Hubei (one view). Sun Quan and Liu Bei’s allies set fire to Cao Cao’s chained ships on the Yangtze. This battle led to the Three Kingdoms.
Jiangling. Today: Jingzhou, Hubei. An old walled city on the Yangtze — Li Bai’s poem says he sailed “a thousand li to Jiangling in one day”.
Wulin. Today: Honghu, Hubei. Cao Cao’s camp across the river from Chibi; the burning ships drifted into it.
Dongting Lake. It soaks up Yangtze floods like a sponge. Hunan (“south of the lake”) and Hubei (“north of the lake”) are named after it.
Fei River (Shouyang). Today: Shou County, Anhui. The Eastern Jin beat the much bigger Former Qin army here. Fleeing soldiers took every bush on Mount Bagong for an enemy.
Jiankang. Today: Nanjing, Jiangsu. Many people fled south to Jiankang during the wars in the north, and the south began to develop.
Yinqueshan. Today: Linyi, Shandong. Bamboo slips of both Sunzi’s and Sun Bin’s Art of War were found here, proving they were two different books.
Terracotta Army. Today: Lintong, Xi’an, Shaanxi. About 8,000 life-size clay soldiers, each with a different face, stand in battle order 1.5 km east of the tomb. Farmers found them in 1974.
Daming Palace. Today: Xi’an, Shaanxi. About four times the size of Beijing’s Forbidden City. Foreign envoys climbed to the Hanyuan Hall to meet the emperor.
Giant Wild Goose Pagoda. Today: Xi’an, Shaanxi. Xuanzang translated Buddhist books here for years. The pagoda still stands in Xi’an, about 64 m tall.
Zhaozhou Bridge. Today: Zhao County, Hebei. The world’s oldest surviving open-spandrel stone arch bridge: small arches on top of the big one let floodwater through and make it lighter.
Longmen Grottoes. Today: south of Luoyang, Henan. The 17-m Vairocana Buddha at Fengxian Temple was finished in 675 under Empress Wu Zetian.
Zhuojun. Today: Beijing. Emperor Yang of Sui sent troops and grain north to Zhuojun along the Yongji Canal. Later An Lushan started his rebellion from Fanyang.
Yuhang. Today: Hangzhou, Zhejiang. Rice and silk from the rich south were loaded onto canal boats here and sent north.
Jokhang Temple. Today: Lhasa, Tibet. In front of the temple stands the Tang–Tubo Alliance Tablet of 823, which speaks of the two as “uncle and nephew”, one family.
Lake Erhai (Nanzhao). Today: Dali, Yunnan. The Tang gave the Nanzhao ruler the title “King of Yunnan”. Its capital stood by this lake.
Beiting Protectorate. Today: Jimsar County, Xinjiang. With the Anxi Protectorate it governed the lands north and south of the Tian Shan.
Silla (Gyeongju). Today: Gyeongju, Republic of Korea. Silla students studied in Chang’an; some even passed the Tang civil-service exam.
Leshan Giant Buddha. Today: Leshan, Sichuan. 71 m tall, carved into a cliff where three rivers meet — it took 90 years. Its ears are about 7 m long.
Ürümqi. The city farthest from any ocean in the world — about 2,500 km.
Hohhot. Its name means “Blue City” in Mongolian.
Nanning. A green city near the border with Vietnam; it hosts the China–ASEAN Expo every year.
Yinchuan. Yinchuan lies on the Ningxia Plain, watered by the Yellow River.
Chengdu. The world’s first paper money, jiaozi, was used here in the Northern Song. Today it is famous for giant pandas.
Wusuli River. The sun rises here about four hours earlier than at the Pamirs in the far west.
Geladaindong. Glaciers on this peak of the Tanggula Mountains melt into the Tuotuo River, the source of the Yangtze.
Tuotuo River. A wide, braided stream on the high plateau. The Qinghai–Tibet Railway crosses it.
Yushu. Today: Yushu, Qinghai. A Tibetan town about 3,700 m high in the Sanjiangyuan area.
First Bend of the Yangtze (Shigu). Today: Shigu, Lijiang, Yunnan. Flowing south beside other great rivers, the Jinsha makes a sharp V-turn here and heads for the east.
Tiger Leaping Gorge. Today: Lijiang, Yunnan. Legend says a tiger jumped across the river here on a rock. The cliffs rise about 3,900 m above the water.
Yibin. Here the Min River joins the Jinsha, and from here down the river is called the Yangtze (Chang Jiang).
Baiheliang inscriptions. Today: Fuling, Chongqing. People carved fish on this rock to mark very low water. Now it lies under the reservoir and visitors see it through windows.
Zhang Fei Temple. Today: Yunyang, Chongqing. To save it from the rising water, the whole temple was taken apart and rebuilt 32 km upstream.
Baidicheng. Today: Fengjie, Chongqing. Li Bai wrote: “At dawn I left Baidi among the coloured clouds; a thousand li to Jiangling in one day.”
Qutang Gorge. Its entrance, Kuimen, is so famous that it is printed on the back of the 10-yuan note.
Wu Gorge. Long and winding between the twelve peaks of Wushan; the Goddess Peak looks like a girl looking down at the river.
Shennong Stream. Today: Badong, Hubei. Boatmen used to pull small boats called “pea-pod boats” up its rapids by rope.
Xiling Gorge. Once full of dangerous rapids and rocks; now the dam has made the water calm and deep.
Three Gorges Dam. Today: Sandouping, Yichang, Hubei. The dam is 2,335 m long and 181 m high. It controls floods, makes clean electricity and lets big ships sail to Chongqing.
Gezhouba Dam. Today: Yichang, Hubei. Built 38 km below the Three Gorges Dam; it evens out the water released by the big dam.
Yichang. The river leaves the gorges and spreads out onto the plain here — the “gateway to the Three Gorges”.
Hukou. Today: Hukou, Jiangxi. Its name means “lake mouth”: Poyang Lake flows into the Yangtze here.
Poyang Lake. In summer it is a huge lake; in winter much of it dries into grassland where white cranes spend the winter.
Baimamiao, Taizhou. Today: Taizhou, Jiangsu. The East China Military Region Navy was set up in this small town by the Yangtze — the birthday of the PLA Navy.
Geneva. Today: Geneva, Switzerland. Premier Zhou Enlai led New China’s delegation to its first big international conference here.
Bandung. Today: Bandung, Indonesia. Zhou Enlai proposed “seeking common ground while setting aside differences”, and the conference was a success.
Singapore. A city-state at the tip of the Malay Peninsula, on the busy Strait of Malacca.
UN Headquarters, New York. Today: New York, USA. On 25 October 1971 the UN General Assembly voted to restore the PRC’s seat.
Golden Bauhinia Square. Today: Wan Chai, Hong Kong. A golden bauhinia flower statue by Victoria Harbour. A flag-raising ceremony is held here every day.
Jinghong (Xishuangbanna). Today: Xishuangbanna, Yunnan. Every April the Dai people celebrate New Year with the Water-Splashing Festival.
Guilin. Today: Guilin, Guangxi. People say “Guilin’s scenery is the best under heaven” — green limestone peaks rising beside the Li River.
Potala Palace. Today: Lhasa, Tibet. Tradition says Songtsen Gampo first built a palace here for Princess Wencheng. The red-and-white palace climbs the whole hill.
Qinghai–Tibet Railway. Today: Xining–Lhasa (pin: Tanggula Pass, 5,072 m). The highest railway in the world. Engineers kept the frozen ground cold with special pipes so the tracks would not sink.
Wuhan Yangtze River Bridge. Cars run on the upper deck and trains below, joining the Beijing–Hankou and Guangzhou–Hankou railways into one line.
Nanjing Yangtze River Bridge. After Soviet experts left, Chinese engineers finished this road-and-rail bridge using China-made steel.
Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center. Today: Ejin Banner, Inner Mongolia (in the desert). China’s first satellite went up from here in 1970, playing the song “The East Is Red”, and Yang Liwei flew to space from here in 2003.
Xichang Satellite Launch Center. Today: Xichang, Sichuan. In the mountains of southern Sichuan, close to the equator for a launch site in China, with many clear days.
Wenchang Space Launch Site. Today: Wenchang, Hainan Island. On the coast of Hainan, so big rockets can be shipped in by sea, and the low latitude saves fuel.
Yangtze River. From the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau to the East China Sea at Shanghai. It is a “golden waterway” full of ships.
Yellow River. It picks up yellow loess on the plateau and carries it to the Bohai. Early Chinese civilisation grew up along its middle and lower reaches.
Georgia. Native peoples lived here for 13,000 years before the colony was named for King George II in 1732. Today Georgia is the largest state east of the Mississippi River by land area, with 159 counties.
Savannah. Oglethorpe laid Savannah out in 1733 as neighborhoods around shady squares — 22 of those squares are still there. Sherman’s March to the Sea ended here in 1864, and today it has one of the busiest container ports in the country.
South Carolina. Today: South Carolina (and North Carolina). Georgia was founded as a buffer to protect rich English Carolina, where enslaved Africans — most of the people — grew rice and indigo. In December 1860 South Carolina became the first state to leave the Union.
Savannah River. This 300-mile river forms most of the border between Georgia and South Carolina. Augusta grew at its Fall Line rapids and Savannah near its mouth — today giant container ships sail up it to the port.
Spanish Florida. Today: Florida (US state). Spain claimed “La Florida” in the 1500s and made St. Augustine its capital; Georgia was founded as a buffer against it. The United States got Florida from Spain by a treaty in 1819, and it became a state in 1845.
Charles Town. Today: Charleston, South Carolina. Rice grown by enslaved Africans made it the richest city in the colonial South. In April 1861 the first shots of the Civil War were fired at Fort Sumter, in its harbor.
St. Augustine. Founded 42 years before Jamestown, it is the oldest city in the US that Europeans started and people have lived in ever since. Its fort, the Castillo de San Marcos, is built of coquina, a stone made of tiny shells that swallowed cannonballs instead of cracking.
Fort Frederica. Oglethorpe built this fort town on St. Simons Island to guard Georgia against the Spanish. After the soldiers left in 1749 the town faded away; today you can walk its old streets past the brick foundations of the houses.
Ebenezer. Today: Town site in Effingham County, Georgia. Protestant refugees from Salzburg built this town up the Savannah River. They raised silkworms and spoke out against slavery; the town is gone, but their brick Jerusalem Lutheran Church (1767) still holds services.
Tybee Island. Georgia’s northernmost barrier island, at the mouth of the Savannah River; a lighthouse has stood here since 1736. In 1862 Union cannons on Tybee, about a mile away, broke a hole in Fort Pulaski’s brick walls in about 30 hours.
Yamacraw Bluff. Today: Riverfront of downtown Savannah. A 40-foot bluff above the Savannah River where Tomochichi’s Yamacraw people lived. Here they welcomed Oglethorpe and about 114 settlers in February 1733, and Savannah was laid out on top.
Altamaha River. Formed where the Ocmulgee and Oconee rivers meet, it is one of the biggest rivers on the East Coast and runs free with no dams. It was colonial Georgia’s southern frontier; Highland Scots built Darien near its mouth in 1736.
St. Marys River. It flows out of the Okefenokee Swamp and loops around to the Atlantic, forming the border between Georgia and Florida. It became Georgia’s southern border in 1763.
Fall Line. A line of rapids and small waterfalls where the hard rocks of the Piedmont meet the soft sands of the Coastal Plain. Boats could go no farther upstream, so Augusta, Macon and Columbus grew up here, using the falling water to run their mills.
Darien. Highland Scots founded Darien in 1736 to guard the frontier, and in 1739 they signed a petition against bringing slavery into Georgia. After the Civil War, Tunis Campbell organized freedpeople here and was elected a state senator.
Augusta. Founded in 1736 as a fur-trading post at the Savannah River’s Fall Line, it is where treaties of 1763 and 1773 took huge areas of Creek and Cherokee land. Later its canal powered cotton mills, and today golfers know it for the Masters tournament.
Johnson Square. Savannah’s first square, laid out by Oglethorpe in 1733. Under the tall stone monument in the middle lies Nathanael Greene, a general of the American Revolution.
Congregation Mickve Israel. Jewish settlers reached Savannah just five months after the colony began, and Oglethorpe let them stay. Their congregation is one of the oldest in America, and its Gothic-style synagogue on Monterey Square still keeps a Torah scroll they brought in 1733.
Salzburg. Today: Salzburg, Austria. A city in the Alps, then ruled by an archbishop. In 1731 he drove out about 20,000 Protestants for their faith; some of them sailed to Georgia in 1734 and founded Ebenezer.
St. Simons Island. One of Georgia’s “Golden Isles,” the barrier islands of the coast. Oglethorpe built Fort Frederica here, and in 1742 his soldiers stopped a Spanish invasion at Bloody Marsh; today a white lighthouse from 1872 greets visitors.
Bloody Marsh. Oglethorpe’s soldiers and Highland Scots ambushed Spanish troops marching beside this marsh on St. Simons Island. Spain soon sailed away and never seriously attacked Georgia again — though, despite the name, only a few soldiers died here.
Fort King George. Built in 1721 near the mouth of the Altamaha, it was the first British fort on what became Georgia’s coast — 12 years before the colony began. Today a rebuilt wooden blockhouse stands near where the Highland Scots settled Darien in 1736.
Wormsloe Historic Site. Noble Jones, one of the first settlers, built a small fortified house here of tabby, a concrete made with oyster shells; its ruins are the oldest standing building in Savannah. Visitors arrive along a 1.5-mile avenue of about 400 live oaks hung with Spanish moss.
Bethesda orphan house. The preacher George Whitefield founded this orphan house near Savannah in 1740 and raised money for it with his famous outdoor sermons. It is one of the oldest children’s homes in America that is still running.
French Louisiana. Today: Louisiana and the Mississippi valley. In 1682 France claimed the whole Mississippi valley and named it for King Louis XIV. The United States bought the huge land west of the river, with New Orleans, in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803.
Virginia. Jamestown (1607) made Virginia the first lasting English colony, and its House of Burgesses (1619) was the first elected assembly in English America. Four of the first five presidents came from Virginia, and the Revolution’s last big battle was fought at Yorktown.
Etowah River. This river runs from the north Georgia mountains past the Etowah Mounds and joins the Oostanaula at Rome to form the Coosa. Mississippian people built their great mound town right on its bank, and a V-shaped stone fish trap they made can still be seen in the river.
Ocmulgee Mounds. People have lived here for more than 12,000 years — a Clovis spear point was found here — and around AD 900 Mississippian farmers built a town with big earth mounds. You can climb the 55-foot Great Temple Mound and step inside the Earth Lodge, whose clay floor is about 1,000 years old.
Macon. Macon grew where the Ocmulgee River leaves the hills at the Fall Line, and steamboats carried its cotton downriver. At the edge of town stand the Ocmulgee Mounds, about 900 years older than the city.
Stallings Island. A small island in the Savannah River just above Augusta. People camped here about 4,500 years ago and left a huge heap of river mussel shells — along with some of the oldest pottery in North America.
Sapelo Island. About 4,000 years ago Native people here piled oyster and clam shells into great rings, some 300 feet across. Today the island can be reached only by ferry, and a small community descended from people once enslaved on its plantations still lives there.
Kolomoki Mounds. One of the oldest and largest Woodland-period towns in the Southeast, in southwest Georgia. Its Mound A rises 57 feet — the biggest Woodland platform mound in eastern North America — and you can climb the steps to the top.
Rock Eagle effigy mound. A giant bird with spread wings, made of thousands of white quartz rocks piled on the ground — 102 feet from head to tail. Nobody knows exactly who built it or why; the best view is from the stone tower beside it.
Etowah Mounds. Today: Etowah Indian Mounds Historic Site, near Cartersville. A 54-acre Mississippian town with six mounds, a plaza and a defensive ditch; it was probably the town of Itaba that de Soto saw in 1540. Mound A, 63 feet tall and built basket by basket, is the tallest in Georgia, and a pair of painted marble statues was found here.
Cartersville. A small city in northwest Georgia near the Etowah River; just outside town are the Etowah Indian Mounds. Downtown you can still see the world’s first painted wall sign for Coca-Cola, from 1894.
Coosa. Today: Under Carters Lake, Murray County. Coosa was the main town of a chiefdom whose power reached into Tennessee and Alabama. When de Soto arrived on July 16, 1540, he took its chief hostage; the town site now lies under the waters of Carters Lake.
Tennessee. Georgia’s neighbor to the north. It was the last state to leave the Union in 1861 and the first to rejoin in 1866; in the 1930s the Tennessee Valley Authority’s dams brought electric power to its farms.
Alabama. Georgia’s neighbor to the west — the Chattahoochee River forms much of the border. Much of it was Creek and Cherokee land until the forced removals of the 1830s; later Montgomery, Selma and Birmingham became famous places of the civil rights movement.
Carters Lake. A deep reservoir in the north Georgia mountains, made when Carters Dam was finished on the Coosawattee River in 1977. The site of Coosa, the great Mississippian town de Soto visited, now lies under its water.
Ichisi. Today: Near Macon, Georgia. A chiefdom on the Ocmulgee River, probably centered at the Lamar mounds near Macon. De Soto’s army arrived in late March 1540 — the first Europeans its people had ever seen.
Lamar mound site. Two mounds in the river swamp near Macon, part of Ocmulgee Mounds National Historical Park. One has a rare spiral ramp winding to its top; this was probably the center of Ichisi when de Soto came in 1540.
Ocute. Today: Oconee River valley, central Georgia. A powerful chiefdom on the Oconee River. In April 1540 de Soto marched here from Ichisi, and its chief gave the Spaniards food and hundreds of carriers for their journey.
Oconee River. It flows from the hills near Athens south past Milledgeville and joins the Ocmulgee to form the Altamaha. After the treaties of 1783 and 1790 it was the border between Georgia’s settlers and the Creek Nation.
Ocmulgee River. It rises east of Atlanta, crosses the Fall Line at Macon and joins the Oconee to form the Altamaha. Creek towns lined its banks, and in the 1800s steamboats carried cotton down it toward the coast.
Santa Catalina de Guale. The main Spanish mission among the Guale people. Guale people burned it in a revolt in 1597; it was rebuilt in 1605 and abandoned in 1680 after attacks, and archaeologists found its church in 1981.
St. Catherines Island. A barrier island with wide salt marshes, where the Spanish mission of Santa Catalina de Guale once stood. Today it is a private nature reserve where scientists study the island’s past and protect rare animals.
Coweta. Today: Near Phenix City, Alabama, across from Columbus. A leading Lower Creek town on the Chattahoochee River, with a square ground where councils met and the Green Corn festival was held. Coweta was a “red” town that led in war, while nearby Cusseta was a “white” town for peace.
Cusseta. Today: Near Columbus, Georgia. A leading Lower Creek town on the Chattahoochee near today’s Columbus — a “white” town, where peace talks were held. Its people were forced west in the 1830s, but the Georgia town of Cusseta keeps its name.
Columbus. Founded in 1828 at the last falls of the Chattahoochee River, where water power ran big textile mills. Today it is Georgia’s second-largest city, next to Fort Benning, and its river has one of the longest city whitewater rafting runs in the world.
Cherokee towns. Cherokee towns stood along rivers in the mountain valleys of north Georgia and nearby states. Each had a council house big enough for the whole town, and every Cherokee belonged to one of seven clans, passed down through the mother.
Apalachee. Today: Tallahassee area, Florida. The land of the Apalachee people, skilled corn farmers in the Florida panhandle. De Soto spent the winter of 1539–40 at their main town, Anhaica, in today’s Tallahassee, then marched north into Georgia on March 3, 1540.
Capachequi. Today: Near Albany, Georgia. A chiefdom in southwest Georgia, near today’s Albany and the Flint River. De Soto’s army passed through in March 1540, the first Europeans to reach the area.
Toa. Today: Flint River near Montezuma, Georgia. A large town on the Flint River that de Soto’s army reached on March 23, 1540, on its way north toward Ichisi.
Ulibahali. Today: Rome, Georgia. A town of the Coosa chiefdom where two rivers meet to form the Coosa River. De Soto’s army passed through in August 1540; today the city of Rome stands on the spot.
Rome. A city in the Valley and Ridge region where the Etowah and Oostanaula rivers meet to form the Coosa. Like ancient Rome it sits on seven hills — that is how it got its name in 1834.
barrier islands. A chain of sandy islands along Georgia’s coast, from Tybee in the north to Cumberland in the south; Spain built missions on them in the 1500s and 1600s. They take the pounding of waves and storms and shelter the salt marshes behind them, and their shapes keep changing as the sand moves.
Flint River. It begins underground beneath Atlanta’s airport and flows about 340 miles south past Albany to join the Chattahoochee at Lake Seminole. The Creek lived along it; after an 1821 treaty it marked the edge of Creek land until the rest was taken in 1825–27.
Arizona. A desert state in the Southwest, home of the Grand Canyon and of the Navajo Nation, the largest Native American reservation in the US. Its southern strip was bought from Mexico in the Gadsden Purchase of 1853.
Eatonton. A small town in Putnam County, central Georgia, near the Rock Eagle effigy mound. The writer Alice Walker, author of The Color Purple, grew up nearby.
Swift Creek site. A Woodland-period village site beside Swift Creek near Macon, dug by archaeologists in the 1930s. It gave its name to Swift Creek pottery, made by pressing carved wooden paddles into wet clay.
Track Rock. Soapstone boulders in the north Georgia mountains covered with hundreds of carvings — circles, crosses and shapes like animal tracks and footprints. Native people carved them long ago, and the Cherokee told stories about them.
Oklahoma. Much of it was set aside as “Indian Territory,” where the US government forced the Cherokee, Creek and other Southeastern nations to move in the 1830s. The Cherokee Nation rebuilt there and has its capital at Tahlequah today.
Ogeechee River. A dark “blackwater” river that flows about 290 miles from the Piedmont to the sea just south of Savannah. The 1763 Treaty of Augusta took the land between it and the Savannah River from the Creek, and in 1864 Sherman’s army stormed Fort McAllister at its mouth.
Tondee’s Tavern. Peter Tondee’s tavern in Savannah, where the Liberty Boys met in 1774–75 to protest British laws; Georgia’s first Provincial Congress gathered here in July 1775. The building is gone, but a historical marker shows the spot.
Midway. The heart of St. John’s Parish. Its rice planters, who enslaved many people, were also Georgia’s most eager Patriots and sent Lyman Hall to Congress in 1775; Midway Church (1792) and its old cemetery still stand.
Sunbury. Today: Historic site in Liberty County. Once a busy port that rivaled Savannah. In November 1778 the British demanded the surrender of its Fort Morris, and Col. John McIntosh answered “Come and take it!”; after the war the town faded away, and only its old cemetery is left.
St. John’s Parish. Today: Liberty County, Georgia. This coastal parish was Georgia’s hotbed of revolution: in 1775, before the rest of the colony joined, it sent its own delegate, Lyman Hall, to the Continental Congress. In 1777 it was renamed Liberty County to honor that spirit.
Fort Morris. An earthen fort at Sunbury guarding the Medway River. When the British demanded its surrender in November 1778, Col. John McIntosh replied “Come and take it!” — they backed off, though the fort fell in January 1779; its earth walls can still be seen.
Brier Creek. British troops surprised a Patriot army camped by this creek in Screven County and routed it; hundreds of Patriots were killed, captured or drowned escaping through the swamp. The victory let Britain bring back royal government in Georgia.
Louisville. Named for King Louis XVI of France, who had helped America win independence. In 1796 the legislature publicly burned the papers of the corrupt Yazoo land sale here — legend says the fire was lit with a magnifying glass and sunlight.
Kettle Creek. About 400 Patriot militia under Andrew Pickens, John Dooly and Elijah Clarke beat about 700 Loyalists under Colonel Boyd here in Wilkes County. Austin Dabney, a Black soldier who fought for the Patriots, was badly wounded here; Georgia later honored him with land for his service.
Wilkes County. A backcountry county made in 1777 from the 1773 “New Purchase,” land the Creek and Cherokee were pressed to give up to pay off traders’ debts. The Battle of Kettle Creek was fought here, and its county seat, Washington, was the first town in the US named for George Washington.
Nancy Hart’s cabin. Today: Cabin site in Elbert County, Georgia. Legend says that in 1779 Nancy Hart, a tall frontier woman, captured a group of Loyalist soldiers who barged into her cabin, holding them at gunpoint until help came. A replica cabin, built with stones from her real chimney, stands at the site today.
Broad River. A river of the northeast Georgia Piedmont that flows into the Savannah River. During the Revolution its backcountry was home to tough frontier families like Nancy Hart’s, who fought off Loyalist raiders.
Elbert County. A county in northeast Georgia between the Broad and Savannah rivers, where Nancy Hart lived during the Revolution. It is famous for granite: its county seat, Elberton, calls itself the “Granite Capital of the World.”
Hart County. Created in 1853 and named for Nancy Hart — the only Georgia county named for a woman. Much of it borders Lake Hartwell, a big reservoir on the Savannah River.
Hartwell. The county seat of Hart County, also named for Nancy Hart. Huge Lake Hartwell, made by damming the Savannah River in the early 1960s, lies right next to the town.
Gwinnett County. Created in 1818 on land the Creek were forced to give up, and named for Button Gwinnett, a signer of the Declaration of Independence. Once farmland, today it is one of the most diverse counties in the Southeast, with about a million people.
Hall County. Made in 1818 on land the Cherokee gave up in 1817–1819, and named for Lyman Hall, a signer of the Declaration of Independence. Its county seat, Gainesville, calls itself the “Poultry Capital of the World,” and Lake Lanier covers a big part of the county.
Walton County. Created in 1818 on land taken from the Creek, and named for George Walton, one of Georgia’s three signers of the Declaration of Independence. Its county seat is Monroe, east of Atlanta.
Kentucky. Kentucky was part of Virginia until it became a state in 1792. In the Civil War it was a slave state that stayed in the Union — Lincoln, who was born there, is said to have joked that he hoped to have God on his side, but he must have Kentucky.
Athens. Home of the University of Georgia, where classes began in 1801; the founders named the town after ancient Athens, a famous center of learning. Today it is also known for music — bands like R.E.M. got their start here.
University of Georgia. Chartered on January 27, 1785, it was the first state-chartered public university in the US; Abraham Baldwin wrote its charter. In January 1961 Charlayne Hunter and Hamilton Holmes became its first Black students, after a federal judge ordered the university to admit them.
Milledgeville. Laid out in 1803 and named for Governor John Milledge, it was Georgia’s capital for 60 years. Here the secession convention voted to leave the Union in January 1861, and in November 1864 Union officers held a mock session in the statehouse to “repeal” secession.
Atlanta. It began in 1837 as “Terminus,” the end of a railroad line, then became Marthasville and, in 1845, Atlanta. Burned in 1864, it rose again as the state capital, Dr. King’s hometown and host of the 1996 Olympics — and its airport is one of the busiest in the world.
Appalachian Mountains. A 1,500-mile chain of very old, worn-down mountains that ends in north Georgia, where the Appalachian Trail starts at Springer Mountain. The Proclamation of 1763 banned colonists from settling west of them — one of the complaints that led to the Revolution.
Little River. A river in east Georgia that flows into the Savannah River above Augusta. The 1773 “New Purchase” took about 2 million acres of Creek and Cherokee land north of it, which became Wilkes County.
Boston. It became the center of protest against British rule: the Boston Massacre (1770) and the Boston Tea Party (1773) happened here, and Britain closed its harbor as punishment in 1774. Today the 2.5-mile Freedom Trail leads past 16 historic sites.
Yorktown. About 17,000 American and French soldiers trapped the British army here, and on October 19, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered about 8,000 men — the last major battle of the Revolution. Today the battlefield is part of Colonial National Historical Park, together with Jamestown.
Mississippi River. North America’s great river flows about 2,340 miles from Minnesota to the Gulf of Mexico. It was the US western border in 1783; Cherokee families on the Trail of Tears crossed it in icy winter, and capturing Vicksburg on its bank gave the Union the whole river in 1863.
Massachusetts. Named after the Massachusett people, it was where the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth (1620) and the Puritans founded Boston (1630). The Revolution’s first battles were fought at Lexington and Concord in 1775, and Shays’ Rebellion (1786–87) pushed leaders to write a stronger Constitution.
Clarke County. Named for Elijah Clarke, the Wilkes County militia leader of the Revolution. It is Georgia’s smallest county by area, and since 1991 it and the city of Athens have shared one government, Athens-Clarke County.
Haitian Monument. It honors more than 500 free Black soldiers from Saint-Domingue (today’s Haiti) who fought beside the French and Americans in the 1779 Siege of Savannah. Legend says one 12-year-old drummer boy was Henri Christophe, who later became king of Haiti.
Old Governor’s Mansion. Finished in 1839 in grand Greek Revival style, it was home to Georgia’s governors while Milledgeville was the capital, and General Sherman made it his headquarters in November 1864. Today it is a museum that also tells the stories of the enslaved people who worked there.
Creek (Muscogee) Nation. Today: Central and western Georgia, eastern Alabama. In the 1780s the powerful Creek Nation was frontier Georgia’s strong neighbor — one reason Georgia wanted a strong national government. Treaty by treaty its land was taken: by 1827 no Creek land was left in Georgia, and in 1836 most Creek people were forced west to Indian Territory.
Cherokee Nation. Today: Northwest Georgia (the Nation is now based in Tahlequah, Oklahoma). The Cherokee Nation had its own written laws, a newspaper and a capital at New Echota. Georgia raffled off its land in the 1832 lotteries, and in 1838 soldiers forced the Cherokee out on the Trail of Tears — the Nation rebuilt itself in Oklahoma, where it is based today.
New York. The Dutch town of New Amsterdam became English New York in 1664, and in 1789 the first Congress under the Constitution met here. The Erie Canal made it the nation’s busiest port, millions of immigrants arrived here, and in 1882 Edison’s Pearl Street station began lighting its streets with electricity.
Franklin County. Land the Cherokee were made to give up in 1783 became Franklin County, handed out free to white settlers as “headrights.” It was named after Benjamin Franklin.
Fort Wilkinson. Today: Near Milledgeville, Georgia. At this frontier fort on the Oconee River, Creek leaders signed the 1802 Treaty of Fort Wilkinson, giving up the land between the Oconee and Ocmulgee rivers. Nothing of the fort stands today — only a historical marker.
Washington, D.C.. The nation’s capital, home of Congress, the President and the Supreme Court — the original Declaration of Independence and Constitution are kept at the National Archives here. On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people filled the National Mall for the March on Washington.
Fort Jackson. Today: Near Wetumpka, Alabama. After the Creek War, Andrew Jackson forced the 1814 Treaty of Fort Jackson here, taking about 23 million acres — even from Creek towns that had fought on his side. Today the site is Fort Toulouse–Fort Jackson Park, with rebuilt forts and a riverside arboretum.
Creek Agency. Today: On the Flint River near Roberta, Georgia. US agent Benjamin Hawkins ran a big farm here on the Flint River, trying to get Creek families to farm the American way. In the 1818 Treaty of the Creek Agency, the Creek gave up more land near the heads of the Ocmulgee and Apalachee rivers.
Apalachee River. A Piedmont river that winds southeast through northeast Georgia and flows into the Oconee River at Lake Oconee. In 1818 the Creek gave up land near its headwaters.
Habersham County. Cherokee mountain land given up in 1817–1819 became Habersham County in northeast Georgia. Today its hills are known for apple orchards — the town of Cornelia has a giant red apple monument.
Rabun County. Cherokee mountain land given up in 1817–1819 became Rabun County, in Georgia’s far northeast corner. Its mountains, lakes and the deep Tallulah Gorge on its southern edge make it a favorite place for hikers.
Indian Springs. Creek people prized the mineral spring here for healing. In 1825 William McIntosh signed away the last Creek lands in Georgia at Indian Springs, even though Creek law forbade it — and he was killed for it a few months later.
New Echota. Today: Near Calhoun, Georgia. The Cherokee built their capital here, with a council house, a supreme court and a print shop that printed the Cherokee Phoenix in English and Cherokee from 1828. The 1835 Treaty of New Echota, signed by a small group without the Nation’s consent, led to the 1838 roundup — today you can walk among rebuilt buildings and the old Vann’s Tavern.
Dahlonega. In 1828–29 gold was found on Cherokee land here, and thousands of “Twenty-Niners” rushed in — America’s first big gold rush. The old courthouse on the town square is now a gold museum, and Dahlonega gold covers the dome of the State Capitol.
Yazoo lands. Today: Most of today’s Alabama and Mississippi. Georgia once claimed land all the way west to the Mississippi River. In 1795 bribed lawmakers sold 35 million acres of it cheaply to land companies; furious voters threw them out, and the next legislature burned the law in public.
Mississippi. Most of Mississippi was once part of Georgia’s Yazoo lands, and after Indian Removal cotton planters took over its rich soil. It seceded in January 1861; a century later, in the 1964 Freedom Summer, volunteers went door to door here helping Black citizens register to vote.
Dahlonega Mint. Today: Site of Price Memorial Hall, University of North Georgia, Dahlonega. From 1838 to 1861 this branch of the US Mint turned gold from the old Cherokee Nation into coins stamped with a small “D.” After the building burned in 1878, Price Memorial Hall was built on its foundations — its steeple shines with Dahlonega gold.
Indian Territory. Today: Oklahoma. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 sent the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations west to this land beyond Arkansas. At the end of the Trail of Tears the Cherokee rebuilt their nation here, with Tahlequah as capital; in 1907 it became part of the state of Oklahoma.
Fort Wool. Today: New Echota, near Calhoun, Georgia. In May 1838 US soldiers built this stockade at the old Cherokee capital, and rounded-up Cherokee families were held inside before being marched to camps in Tennessee. Nothing is left of it; a sign at New Echota tells its story.
Fort Cass. Today: Charleston, Tennessee. Thousands of Cherokee were held in crowded camps around Fort Cass in the hot summer of 1838, and many died of disease. Chief John Ross won the right for the Cherokee to lead their own removal, and most groups set out from here in the fall.
Charleston, Tennessee. This small town on the Hiwassee River grew up around Fort Cass, the center of the 1838 removal camps. Thousands of Cherokee waited here through a hot, sickly summer before the long walk west.
Nashville. In October 1838 about a dozen Cherokee groups of roughly 1,000 people each passed Nashville on the northern route of the Trail of Tears. Today Tennessee’s capital is “Music City,” the home of country music.
Hopkinsville. Cherokee leaders White Path and Fly Smith died near this Kentucky town in November 1838. Their graves lie in the Trail of Tears Commemorative Park, one of the few places where people who died on the march are known to be buried.
Golconda. Late in 1838, Trail of Tears groups crossed the Ohio River into Illinois by ferry at this little river town. The ferry owner charged the Cherokee a dollar a person — about eight times the usual fare.
Illinois. In the winter of 1838–39 thousands of Cherokee waited for weeks in freezing southern Illinois while ice blocked the Mississippi, and many died. Later the state became an industrial giant, with Chicago as its booming railroad and factory city.
Ohio River. The Ohio flows about 980 miles from Pittsburgh to the Mississippi. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 banned slavery north of it, so it became a border between slave and free states — and Trail of Tears groups crossed it by ferry at Golconda in 1838.
Springfield, Missouri. In January and February 1839, Trail of Tears groups passed here as they pushed west across the Ozarks in midwinter. Today it is Missouri’s third-largest city.
Missouri. Missouri joined the Union as a slave state in the Missouri Compromise of 1820, and its towns were the starting points of the Oregon and Santa Fe trails. In the winter of 1839 Trail of Tears groups crossed it, and in the Civil War it was a border slave state that stayed in the Union.
Ozarks. The Ozarks are an old, worn-down highland of forested hills, caves and springs in Missouri and Arkansas. Trail of Tears groups struggled across them in the middle of winter 1839, short of blankets, food and warm clothes.
Tahlequah. After the Trail of Tears the Cherokee made Tahlequah their new capital in 1839, and the last groups arrived in March. The brick Cherokee National Capitol was finished in 1869, and the town’s street signs are written in both English and Cherokee.
North Carolina. North Carolina at first refused to ratify the Constitution, saying yes only in 1789 once a bill of rights was on the way. Cherokee who escaped removal in its mountains became the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, and in 1960 college students founded SNCC in Raleigh.
Gordon County. A northwest Georgia county where New Echota, the Cherokee capital, once stood. Its county seat is Calhoun, and New Echota Historic Site lies just outside town.
Chief Vann House. James Vann, a wealthy Cherokee leader, built this brick mansion in 1804 — one of the finest houses on the frontier. The Vann family also held about 100 enslaved African Americans, a hard truth the historic site tells today; in 1835 Georgia forced Joseph Vann out of the house.
Chatsworth. A small mountain-edge town in Murray County, close to the Chief Vann House. Just east of town, Fort Mountain State Park has a mysterious ancient stone wall about 855 feet long.
Valley and Ridge. Northwest Georgia’s region of long, parallel ridges with fertile valleys between them — like the folds of a wrinkled rug. Dalton’s carpet mills and the city of Rome are here.
Appalachian Plateau. Georgia’s smallest region, in the far northwest corner: the high, flat tops of Lookout and Sand mountains. Creeks have cut deep canyons such as Cloudland Canyon into it, and coal was once mined here.
Piedmont. “Piedmont” means “foot of the mountains”: rolling hills of red clay between the Blue Ridge and the Fall Line. Most Georgians live and work here — in Atlanta, Athens and their suburbs — and the region is known for granite and poultry farms.
Coastal Plain. Georgia’s largest region, about three-fifths of the state: flat, sandy land that was once under the ocean. It grows peanuts, pecans and cotton, and reaches the sea at the ports of Savannah and Brunswick.
Brasstown Bald. Georgia’s highest point, 4,784 feet (1,458 m) up in the Blue Ridge Mountains. On a clear day you can see four states — Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina and South Carolina — from the tower on top.
Springer Mountain. The Appalachian Trail begins on top of this 3,780-foot Blue Ridge mountain. A bronze plaque on a rock marks the start of the roughly 2,200-mile walk to Maine.
Cloudland Canyon. Creeks have cut canyons hundreds of feet deep into the edge of Lookout Mountain here. Hikers climb down about 600 steps to reach two waterfalls at the bottom.
Providence Canyon. “Georgia’s Little Grand Canyon” is not ancient at all: its gullies, up to about 150 feet deep, formed in the 1800s when poor farming let rain wash the soil away. The walls are striped pink, orange, red and purple.
Okefenokee Swamp. North America’s largest blackwater swamp covers about 438,000 acres; its name is often said to mean “trembling earth,” because floating peat islands shake when you step on them. Alligators, cranes and black bears live here, and it feeds the Suwannee and St. Marys rivers.
Golden Isles. St. Simons, Jekyll, Sea Island and Little St. Simons are nicknamed the “Golden Isles” — some say for the marsh grass that glows gold at sunset. The port city of Brunswick is their gateway on the mainland.
Cumberland Island. Georgia’s largest barrier island, about 17 miles long, is a National Seashore you can reach only by ferry. Wild horses roam its dunes, beaches and live-oak forests.
Dalton. Dalton calls itself the “Carpet Capital of the World” — its mills make most of the carpet sold in the United States. In May 1864 Sherman’s army of about 100,000 faced Johnston’s 60,000 near here, and the Atlanta Campaign began.
Albany. In 1961–62 the Albany Movement protested segregation across this whole Coastal Plain city: more than 1,000 people were jailed, including Dr. King, and its freedom songs spread across the nation. The Albany Civil Rights Institute tells the story today.
Valdosta. A south Georgia city on the Coastal Plain, close to the Florida line. It is nicknamed the “Azalea City” for the flowers that cover it every spring.
Brunswick. This coastal port city, the seat of Glynn County, ships huge numbers of cars overseas. In World War II its J. A. Jones shipyard built 99 Liberty ships in about two years, as farm workers poured in for jobs.
Appalachian Trail. A footpath of about 2,200 miles from Springer Mountain in Georgia to Mount Katahdin in Maine. Hikers who walk the whole trail usually take five to seven months, following white paint blazes on trees and rocks.
Ellijay. A Blue Ridge mountain town known as “Georgia’s Apple Capital.” Every October the Georgia Apple Festival fills its streets, and families pick apples in the nearby orchards.
Taylor Ridge. A long ridge running southwest to northeast through the Valley and Ridge region. Its shape is typical of the area: rock layers were folded like a rug, then worn down, leaving the hardest layers standing as ridges.
Coosa River. The Coosa begins in downtown Rome, where the Etowah and Oostanaula rivers meet, and flows west into Alabama. Riverboats once carried cotton on it.
Lookout Mountain. A long, flat-topped mountain of the Appalachian Plateau that runs from Georgia into Alabama and Tennessee; Cloudland Canyon is cut into its side. In 1863 Union soldiers won the “Battle Above the Clouds” on its northern end.
Sand Mountain. The other big piece of the Appalachian Plateau in Georgia’s far northwest corner: a long, flat-topped mountain that runs southwest into Alabama. Its name comes from its sandy soil.
Gainesville. The “Poultry Capital of the World”: Gainesville led Georgia’s switch from cotton to raising broiler chickens, and a statue of a chicken stands in its Poultry Park. Lake Lanier lies right next to town.
Inner Coastal Plain. Georgia’s farm belt, the inland part of the Coastal Plain, where rich soil and long summers grow peanuts, cotton, pecans, peaches, blueberries and sweet Vidalia onions.
Vidalia. This small Coastal Plain town gave its name to Vidalia onions, so sweet that some people bite into them like apples. By law, only onions grown in about 20 counties around Vidalia may carry the name.
Port of Savannah. One of America’s busiest container ports: in fiscal year 2022 it handled a record 5.76 million TEU (twenty-foot containers). Its river channel was deepened to 47 feet (2015–2022) so giant ships can reach the docks.
Tallulah River. This mountain river carved Tallulah Gorge, about 2 miles long and nearly 1,000 feet deep. In 1970 the tightrope walker Karl Wallenda crossed the gorge on a wire — doing two headstands along the way!
Sitton Gulch Creek. This creek and the streams that feed it, such as Daniel Creek, carved Cloudland Canyon’s deep gorges into the edge of Lookout Mountain. It shows how running water, given enough time, can cut through solid rock.
Elberton. Elberton calls itself the “Granite Capital of the World”: dozens of quarries and workshops around town cut Piedmont granite into monuments and gravestones. Even its courthouse is trimmed with local granite.
Suwannee River. The Suwannee begins in the Okefenokee Swamp and winds about 240 miles south through Florida to the Gulf of Mexico. Tannins from swamp plants stain its water the color of tea, and it is the river in Florida’s state song, “Old Folks at Home.”
Jekyll Island. In 1886 some of America’s richest families, like the Rockefellers and Morgans, made this barrier island their private winter club. Today it is a state park, with Driftwood Beach, where storm-killed trees lie bleached on the sand.
Sea Island. One of the Golden Isles, Sea Island is a small barrier island famous for its beach resort, opened in 1928. In 2004 the leaders of the world’s biggest economies met here for the G8 summit.
Lake Lanier. Buford Dam on the Chattahoochee created this huge reservoir in the 1950s, and it stores much of metro Atlanta’s drinking water. Its winding shoreline is about 690 miles long, and millions of people boat and swim here every year.
Dublin. A Coastal Plain city on the Oconee River, only about 220 feet above sea level. Named after Dublin, Ireland, it throws a big St. Patrick’s Day festival every March.
Texas. Texas broke away from Mexico in 1836 and was its own republic until the US annexed it in 1845 — which led straight to war with Mexico. Cowboys later drove Texas cattle north to the railroads, and with 254 counties it is the only state with more counties than Georgia’s 159.
Buford Dam. This dam across the Chattahoochee River holds back Lake Lanier. It helps control floods, makes electricity and stores water for metro Atlanta.
Augusta Canal. This canal brings Savannah River water from above the Fall Line into Augusta, where it once turned the wheels of big cotton mills. In the Civil War it powered the Confederate Powder Works; today you can ride it in a tour boat.
Fort Hawkins. Today: Macon, Georgia. Built in 1806 on a hill above the Ocmulgee River, where the river leaves the hills at the Fall Line, this fort was a trading post with the Creek — and the city of Macon grew up beside it. A blockhouse rebuilt in 1938 stands on the site today.
Russell–Brasstown Scenic Byway. A 41-mile scenic loop road through the Chattahoochee National Forest in the Blue Ridge Mountains. It passes the road up to Brasstown Bald, Georgia’s highest point, and is especially colorful in fall.
James H. Floyd State Park. A quiet state park in the Valley and Ridge region, with two fishing lakes and a view of long Taylor Ridge. A side trail links it to the long-distance Pinhoti Trail.
Amicalola Falls. At 729 feet, Georgia’s tallest waterfall tumbles down a Blue Ridge mountainside; its Cherokee name means “tumbling waters.” A staircase of about 600 steps climbs beside it, and a trail from here leads to Springer Mountain.
Fulton County. Georgia’s most populous county, home of Atlanta, the state capital. Under the old county-unit system its 556,326 people got just 6 unit votes — the same as three tiny rural counties — until the courts struck the system down in 1963.
Chatham County. Home of Savannah and its busy port, on Georgia’s northern coast. It was one of Georgia’s first eight counties, created in 1777.
Bibb County. Home of Macon, near the middle of the state where the Ocmulgee River crosses the Fall Line. In 1868 Henry McNeal Turner represented this area in the legislature, one of Georgia’s first Black lawmakers.
Richmond County. Home of Augusta on the Savannah River, Georgia’s second-oldest city. Since 1996 Augusta and Richmond County have shared one combined government.
Ware County. Georgia’s largest county by area, home of Waycross and much of the Okefenokee Swamp. Alligators probably outnumber people in its wild southern half!
Waycross. Named for the many railroads and roads that cross here, Waycross is a railroad town and the seat of Ware County. It is a gateway to the Okefenokee Swamp, with Okefenokee Swamp Park just south of town.
Muscogee County. Home of Columbus on the Chattahoochee River, across from Alabama; the county is named for the Muscogee (Creek) people who lived here. In 1971 Columbus and Muscogee County became the first in Georgia to join into one combined government.
Glynn County. Home of Brunswick and most of the Golden Isles on Georgia’s coast. Its old 1907 courthouse sits in a park shaded by huge live oaks draped with Spanish moss.
Georgia State Capitol. Home of the General Assembly and the governor’s office, its dome is covered in real gold leaf from Dahlonega. In 1956 lawmakers here added the Confederate battle flag to the state flag; it was finally replaced in 2001 and 2003.
Nathan Deal Judicial Center. Opened in 2019 next to the Capitol, this is the home of the Supreme Court of Georgia, whose nine justices hear the state’s most important appeals. It is named for Nathan Deal, governor from 2011 to 2019.
Georgia Governor’s Mansion. The governor’s official home, a Greek Revival mansion with 30 columns finished in 1967 in Atlanta’s Buckhead neighborhood. Visitors can tour its rooms full of early-1800s furniture.
Pike County Courthouse. Built in 1895 in Zebulon, this courthouse is a good example of a Georgia county seat. Every one of Georgia’s 159 counties has a courthouse, where trials are held and land and marriage records are kept.
Zebulon. The small county seat of Pike County; both the town and the county are named after the explorer Zebulon Pike, who gave his name to Pikes Peak. Its 1895 courthouse stands in the middle of town.
Thomas County Courthouse. The elected county commissioners of Thomas County meet here to decide on roads, budgets and other county business. The courthouse itself dates from 1858.
Thomasville. The boll weevil was first found in Georgia here in 1915, and within a few years it was wrecking cotton across the state. Today Thomasville is known for its Big Oak, a live oak more than 300 years old, and its yearly Rose Show.
Atlanta City Hall. The mayor and city council run Atlanta from this 1930 tower. On his first day in office in 1962, Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. ordered the “White” and “Colored” signs taken down inside it.
Chattanooga. Railroads from four directions met here, so it was called the “gateway to Georgia”. After the Union won the “Battle Above the Clouds” on Lookout Mountain in November 1863, Sherman’s march on Atlanta began here in May 1864.
Maine. Maine was part of Massachusetts until 1820, when it joined as a free state to balance slave state Missouri (the Missouri Compromise). It is the most northeastern state, famous for forests, lobsters and rocky coasts.
Louisiana Purchase. Today: central United States, from the Mississippi to the Rockies. In 1803 the United States bought this huge land from Napoleon’s France for $15 million — about 3 cents an acre — and roughly doubled in size. Parts of 15 of today’s states were carved from it, though Native nations still lived on the land and had never sold it.
California. Gold was found at Sutter’s Mill in 1848, and within a few years about 300,000 people rushed in — Chinese miners called it “Gold Mountain” (金山). It joined as a free state in 1850 and today has more people than any other state.
Kansas. After 1854 settlers could vote on slavery, so people for and against it rushed in and fought — about 55 people were killed in “Bleeding Kansas”. It joined the Union as a free state in January 1861; later Topeka’s school case, Brown v. Board, ended legal school segregation.
Nebraska. Wagon trains to Oregon and California crossed Nebraska along the Platte River, passing Chimney Rock. On the treeless plains, settlers built houses out of blocks of sod — grassy earth.
Harpers Ferry. Today: Harpers Ferry, West Virginia. In October 1859 John Brown and 21 men seized the US arsenal here, hoping to arm enslaved people for an uprising. Marines captured him in the small brick fire-engine house now called “John Brown’s Fort”, which still stands where the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers meet.
Fort Pulaski. Georgia troops seized this brick fort near Savannah in January 1861. In April 1862 new Union rifled cannons on Tybee Island punched a hole through its thick walls in about 30 hours — brick forts were suddenly out of date. You can still see the cannonball scars.
Chickamauga. The biggest battle ever fought in Georgia: a Confederate victory with about 34,600 men killed, wounded or missing. Union General George Thomas held firm and became the “Rock of Chickamauga”; in 1890 the battlefield became the first national military park.
Chickamauga Creek. In September 1863 Confederate General Bragg crossed the bridges over West Chickamauga Creek to attack Rosecrans’ Union army. The quiet creek still winds through the battlefield park.
Gettysburg. The largest battle ever fought in North America, with about 51,000 casualties in three days — a turning point of the Civil War. On the second day Union soldiers held the rocky hill Little Round Top, and in November 1863 Lincoln gave his short Gettysburg Address here.
Resaca. The first big battle of the Atlanta Campaign. Instead of charging Johnston’s trenches, Sherman kept sending troops around their side, so the Confederates had to keep falling back toward Atlanta.
Kennesaw Mountain. Confederates dug in on this mountain, and on June 27, 1864 Sherman tried his one big head-on attack of the campaign — it failed, with about 3,000 Union losses. Today you can hike to the top and see the earthworks and the Atlanta skyline.
Big Shanty. Today: Kennesaw, Georgia. On April 12, 1862, Union spies led by James Andrews stole the locomotive “General” here while the crew ate breakfast, starting the Great Locomotive Chase. The real “General” is on display in the town’s Southern Museum today.
Peachtree Creek. Two days after taking command, Confederate General John Bell Hood attacked Sherman’s army as it crossed this creek just north of Atlanta, but the attack failed. Today the battlefield lies under the Buckhead and Midtown neighborhoods.
Jonesboro. Sherman’s troops won here and cut the Macon and Western Railroad, the last rail line feeding Atlanta. With no more supplies coming in, Hood left the city, and Union soldiers marched into Atlanta on September 2, 1864.
Andersonville. The largest Confederate prison camp: about 45,000 Union soldiers were packed inside a log stockade with little food, no shelter and a dirty creek for water, and nearly 13,000 died. Rows of their white headstones fill the national cemetery next to it.
Sumter County. A farming county in southwest Georgia; its county seat is Americus. The Confederates built Andersonville prison here, far from the fighting and close to a railroad.
National Prisoner of War Museum. This museum at Andersonville tells the stories of all American prisoners of war, from the Revolution to today. Its brick towers are meant to look like the walls and watchtowers of a prison camp.
Fort McAllister. This fort of earth and sand on the Ogeechee River had beaten off Union warships, but on December 13, 1864 Hazen’s soldiers stormed it in about 15 minutes. That opened a link to the Union fleet, and Savannah fell days later.
Irwinville. At dawn on May 10, 1865, Union cavalry surprised the camp of Confederate President Jefferson Davis, who had fled south after Richmond fell. The spot is now a small state historic site in the pine woods.
Richmond. Richmond, Virginia, was the Confederate capital, only about 100 miles from Washington, so both armies fought for years to protect or take it. When it fell in April 1865, fires burned much of the city and Jefferson Davis fled south.
Fort Sumter. Confederate guns around Charleston Harbor fired on this US fort on an island for 34 hours until it surrendered — the Civil War had begun. Amazingly, no one was killed in the bombardment; today boats take visitors out to the fort.
Ringgold. The stolen “General” ran out of fuel just north of Ringgold after a chase of about 87 miles, and the raiders fled into the woods and were caught. The town’s 1849 stone depot still shows patches where Civil War cannonballs hit it.
Marietta. A city northwest of Atlanta, just below Kennesaw Mountain. In 1915 a mob kidnapped Leo Frank, a Jewish factory manager, from prison and lynched him near here; in World War II about 28,000 workers built B-29 bombers at the Bell plant.
Irwin County. A small farming county in south Georgia whose county seat is Ocilla. Jefferson Davis, the Confederate president, was captured at his camp near Irwinville here in May 1865.
Little Kennesaw Mountain. The smaller peak just southwest of Kennesaw Mountain. In June 1864 Confederate cannons sat on its top, and soldiers dug trenches that you can still follow on the hiking trail.
Atlanta University. Today: Clark Atlanta University (merged 1988). After the Civil War, Atlanta University, Clark, Morehouse and Spelman colleges grew up side by side to educate freed people and their children. W. E. B. Du Bois taught here and wrote The Souls of Black Folk; today the campuses form the largest group of Black colleges in the US.
McIntosh County. A coastal county of marshes and rivers whose county seat is Darien. During Reconstruction, Tunis Campbell organized freed people here, and Black voters elected Black officials.
New Jersey. Between New York and Philadelphia, New Jersey saw so many battles that it is called the “Crossroads of the Revolution” — Washington crossed the icy Delaware to surprise Trenton in 1776. Later Thomas Edison invented the phonograph and a practical light bulb at Menlo Park here.
Camilla. In September 1868 a few hundred mostly Black Republicans marched into town for a political rally. Armed white men opened fire and then hunted the marchers in the countryside; about a dozen Black people were killed — violence meant to stop Black people from voting.
Thomson. Small-town home of Tom Watson, the fiery Populist who fought for poor farmers in the 1890s and won free mail delivery to rural homes (Rural Free Delivery). His home, Hickory Hill, still stands in Thomson.
Piedmont Park. In 1895 Atlanta held a giant fair here to show off the “New South”, and Booker T. Washington gave his famous “Atlanta Compromise” speech. Today it is Atlanta’s biggest park, with a lake and a view of the Midtown skyline.
Auburn Avenue. When segregation kept Black Atlantans out of white businesses, they built their own here — banks, insurance companies, newspapers and churches — and “Sweet Auburn” was called “the richest Black street in the world”. Martin Luther King Jr. was born on this street in 1929.
Hazlehurst. A small town in south Georgia farm country. In the 1930s the photographer Dorothea Lange took pictures of sharecropper families near here, showing that sharecropping and poverty lasted long after Reconstruction.
Warm Springs. Spring water comes out of the ground here at about 88°F (31°C). Franklin D. Roosevelt first came in 1924 hoping the warm pools would help his legs after polio, built his Little White House here, and died here on April 12, 1945; nearby FDR State Park was built by the CCC.
Little White House. Today: Roosevelt’s Little White House State Historic Site. President Franklin D. Roosevelt built this small white cottage in 1932 and came here to rest. On April 12, 1945, he collapsed here while an artist was painting his portrait — the “Unfinished Portrait” still hangs in the museum.
Camp Stewart. Today: Fort Stewart, Hinesville, Georgia. In 1940, as World War II spread, the Army opened this huge camp near Hinesville to train soldiers to use anti-aircraft guns. Whole villages were moved out to make room; today Fort Stewart is the largest Army base east of the Mississippi.
Robins Field. Today: Robins Air Force Base. From 1942 workers here repaired thousands of warplanes, and a whole new city, Warner Robins, grew up around the gate. Today the base is one of Georgia’s biggest employers, and its Museum of Aviation shows more than 85 aircraft.
Fort Benning. Founded near Columbus in 1918, it became the world’s largest infantry school in World War II, and paratroopers learned to jump from its tall towers. It was renamed Fort Moore in 2023, then Fort Benning again in 2025.
Bell Bomber plant. Today: Lockheed Martin plant, Marietta. In World War II about 28,000 Georgians — many of them women — built B-29 Superfortress bombers in one of the biggest factory buildings in the South. After the war Lockheed took over the plant, and it still builds military airplanes today.
Techwood Homes. Today: replaced by Centennial Place (1990s). During the Great Depression the government cleared a run-down neighborhood near Georgia Tech and built the first federal public housing in the US, with brick apartments for low-income families. It was for white families only; it was torn down before the 1996 Olympics.
Camp Gordon. Today: DeKalb–Peachtree Airport, Chamblee. In 1917 the Army built this camp in Chamblee, northeast of Atlanta, and trained the 82nd Division — nicknamed “All-American” because its men came from every state. Sergeant Alvin York, a famous war hero, trained here; the land is now an airport.
Camp Hancock. Today: site in Augusta, Georgia. Pennsylvania’s 28th “Keystone” Division trained at this camp on the edge of Augusta, which also had one of the Army’s first airfields. In 1918 the deadly flu struck and about 3,000 soldiers fell sick, so the camp was quarantined.
Camp Wheeler. Just east of Macon, this camp trained the 31st “Dixie” Division of soldiers from Georgia, Alabama and Florida for World War I. It reopened in 1940, and tens of thousands more trained here for World War II.
Souther Field. Today: Jimmy Carter Regional Airport, Americus. Almost 2,000 military pilots learned to fly at this field northeast of Americus in World War I. In 1923 a young Charles Lindbergh bought his first airplane here and made his first solo flight — four years before he flew alone across the Atlantic.
Americus. In 1963 police arrested young civil rights protesters here, and four leaders were charged with “insurrection”, a crime that could bring the death penalty, before a federal court freed them. Americus is also where Habitat for Humanity, which builds homes around the world, began in 1976.
New York. The Dutch colony of New Netherland became New York when England took it in 1664. The state stretches from New York City to Niagara Falls, and its capital is Albany — not the same Albany as the one in Georgia.
Georgia Tech. Georgia’s big engineering university opened in 1885 to help build a “New South” of factories and railroads. The brick Tech Tower, with “TECH” spelled down its sides in lights, is the campus landmark.
University Homes. Today: demolished (2009). Built near Atlanta University with New Deal money and opened in 1937, it was among the first public housing in the US for Black families. Because of segregation, white families lived at Techwood Homes and Black families here.
Greene County. A county of farms and pine forests east of Atlanta; its county seat is Greensboro. In 1941 Jack Delano photographed life here, from young CCC workers building fences to farm women washing clothes by hand.
Putnam County. A farming county in middle Georgia; its county seat is Eatonton, where the writer Alice Walker was born. In the 1930s Black CCC workers here served in separate, segregated camps.
Ebenezer Baptist Church. Martin Luther King Jr. was baptized here as a boy and later preached beside his father, “Daddy King”. His funeral was held here in April 1968; today the old church is part of the King National Historical Park, and you can hear recordings of his sermons inside.
Raleigh. In April 1960, after the lunch-counter sit-ins, Ella Baker brought student protesters to Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, where they founded SNCC (the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee). SNCC soon moved its office to Atlanta.
Birmingham. In 1963 police in Birmingham, Alabama, turned fire hoses and dogs on marching children, and King wrote his “Letter from Birmingham Jail”; the pictures shocked the nation. That September a bomb at the 16th Street Baptist Church killed four Black girls.
Plains. A tiny farm town of about 600 people near Americus. Jimmy Carter was born here in 1924, grew peanuts, went off to be governor and president, came home again, and died here on December 29, 2024, at age 100.
Memphis. King came to this Mississippi River city in Tennessee to support Black sanitation workers on strike, who carried signs saying “I AM A MAN”. He was shot on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel on April 4, 1968; the motel is now the National Civil Rights Museum.
Pickrick restaurant. Today: site now part of Georgia Tech. On July 3, 1964, the day after the Civil Rights Act became law, owner Lester Maddox chased away three Black students with a pistol. He closed the restaurant rather than serve Black customers — and two years later was elected governor.
Echols County. A tiny county of pine forest on the Florida line, with only a few thousand people. Under Georgia’s old county unit system, it and two other small counties together had as many votes as Fulton County (Atlanta), which had hundreds of times more people.
Glascock County. One of Georgia’s smallest counties, with about 3,000 people; its county seat is Gibson. In the county unit system, its votes counted far more per person than votes in big cities like Atlanta.
Quitman County. A small county on the Chattahoochee River and the Alabama line, with only about 2,000 people; its county seat is Georgetown. Under the county unit system its few voters had as much say as tens of thousands in Atlanta.
Oslo. The capital of Norway, where the Nobel Peace Prize is given every December. In 1964 Martin Luther King Jr. received it here; at 35 he was then the youngest person ever to win it, and he gave all the prize money to the civil rights movement.
Lincoln Memorial. On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people filled the space in front of this marble memorial to Abraham Lincoln for the March on Washington, and King gave his “I Have a Dream” speech from its steps. The spot where he stood is carved into the step.
The Arch (UGA). This iron arch, shaped like the one on Georgia’s state seal, is the front gate of the University of Georgia in Athens. In January 1961 Charlayne Hunter and Hamilton Holmes walked through it as UGA’s first Black students, facing an angry crowd.
White House. Every president since John Adams in 1800 has lived and worked in this white house at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington, D.C. In 1977 Jimmy Carter became the first president from Georgia to move in.
National Center for Civil and Human Rights. This Atlanta museum next to Centennial Olympic Park tells the story of the civil rights movement and of human rights around the world. At its lunch-counter exhibit you sit on a stool, put on headphones and hear what sit-in protesters faced.
Albany Civil Rights Institute. This museum in Albany, Georgia, tells the story of the Albany Movement of 1961–62. On some Saturdays the Freedom Singers still perform the songs that once filled the church next door during mass meetings.
Mount Zion Baptist Church. Today: part of the Albany Civil Rights Institute. In 1961–62 the Albany Movement held its packed mass meetings in this small church, where people sang freedom songs and Martin Luther King Jr. spoke. The old church is now part of the Albany Civil Rights Institute.
Selma. On “Bloody Sunday”, March 7, 1965, state troopers beat peaceful marchers, led by John Lewis, on the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Two weeks later thousands marched 54 miles from Selma to Montgomery, and Congress soon passed the Voting Rights Act.
Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park. Along a few blocks of Auburn Avenue you can visit the house where King was born at 501 Auburn Avenue, Ebenezer Baptist Church, and the King Center. Dr. King and Coretta Scott King rest in a white tomb in the middle of a reflecting pool.
Forsyth County. In 1912 white mobs forced about 1,000 Black residents out of this county north of Atlanta, and for decades it stayed almost all white. In January 1987 a small march for “brotherhood” was attacked; a week later about 20,000 people marched here.
Centennial Olympic Park. Atlanta turned run-down blocks downtown into this park for the 1996 Olympics. On July 27, 1996, a bomb went off at a concert here, killing two people; today kids splash in its fountain shaped like the five Olympic rings.
Olympic stadium / Turner Field. Today: Center Parc Stadium, Georgia State University. Built as the main stadium of the 1996 Olympics, it became the Braves’ Turner Field and is now Georgia State’s football stadium. Next door stood Atlanta Stadium (1965), which brought the Braves and Falcons to town; Hank Aaron hit his record 715th home run there in 1974.
Carter Center. Jimmy and Rosalynn Carter started the Carter Center to watch over fair elections and fight diseases around the world. Its work has nearly wiped out Guinea worm disease — from about 3.5 million cases a year in 1986 to only a handful today; the presidential library and museum share the same hilltop.
Georgia Aquarium. One of the largest aquariums in the world, it opened in 2005 next to Centennial Olympic Park. Visitors walk through a glass tunnel while whale sharks — the biggest fish on Earth — glide overhead.
World of Coca-Cola. Coca-Cola was invented by an Atlanta pharmacist in 1886, and the company still has its headquarters in the city. At this museum beside Centennial Olympic Park you can see the vault that holds the secret formula and taste drinks from around the world.
Fayetteville. A small city south of Atlanta that became a movie-making center after Georgia began giving film companies tax credits of up to 30% in 2008. Huge sound stages at Trilith Studios have hosted Marvel superhero movies — people joke about “Y’allywood”.
Trilith Studios. One of the biggest film studios in North America, with dozens of sound stages in Fayetteville. Marvel movies such as Black Panther and Avengers: Endgame were filmed here, helping film spending in Georgia reach about $4.4 billion in 2022.
Tyler Perry Studios. Filmmaker Tyler Perry turned about 330 acres of the old Fort McPherson army base into a studio campus, opened in 2019 — one of the first major studios in the US owned by an African American. It has sound stages named after Black actors and even a full-size copy of the White House.
Fort McPherson. Today: Tyler Perry Studios, Atlanta. An Army post in southwest Atlanta for more than 120 years; in World War I it held German prisoners of war. After it closed in 2011, much of the land became Tyler Perry Studios.
West Point. A small mill town on the Chattahoochee River at the Alabama line. In 2009 Kia opened its first US car factory here, bringing thousands of jobs; the plant can build hundreds of thousands of cars a year.
Ellabell. A quiet rural community west of Savannah that suddenly became home to one of Georgia’s biggest factories: Hyundai’s electric-vehicle Metaplant, which began full production in October 2024.
Bryan County. A fast-growing coastal county next to Savannah; its county seat is Pembroke. Georgia gave Hyundai about $2.1 billion in incentives to build its Metaplant here.
Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America. This giant electric-vehicle factory near the Port of Savannah began full production in October 2024 and held its grand opening in March 2025. Robots do much of the work, and about 8,500 jobs are planned by 2031.
Camp David. The president’s private mountain retreat in the woods of Maryland. In September 1978 Jimmy Carter kept the leaders of Israel and Egypt here for 13 days until they agreed to the Camp David Accords, which led to peace between the two countries.
Oakland Cemetery. This old garden cemetery near downtown is the resting place of Gone with the Wind author Margaret Mitchell, golfer Bobby Jones and Maynard Jackson, Atlanta’s first Black mayor. In the past it was segregated, with separate sections for Black and white burials.
Jimmy Carter National Historical Park. In Plains you can visit Carter’s boyhood farm, his old high school (now the visitor center) and the little train depot that was his campaign headquarters in 1976. On the farm you can still see the peanut fields where young Jimmy worked.
New England. Rocky soil and long winters made farming hard, so New Englanders fished, cut timber and built ships; most towns were founded by Puritans who wanted to worship in their own way. From 1872, 120 Chinese boys came here to study.
Middle colonies. Today: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware. They were called the “breadbasket colonies” because their rich soil grew so much wheat; barrels of flour sailed out of busy ports like Philadelphia and New York. People of many nations and religions lived side by side here.
Southern colonies. Warm weather and a long growing season made planters rich from tobacco, rice and indigo. That wealth came from the forced labor of enslaved Africans — about 4 in every 10 people here were enslaved by 1770.
New Hampshire. Its giant white pines were so valuable that the king’s men marked the best ones as masts for the Royal Navy. On 21 June 1788 it became the 9th state to approve the Constitution — exactly the number needed for it to take effect.
Maryland. Lord Baltimore founded it in 1634 as a refuge for Catholics, and its Toleration Act of 1649 protected Christians of different churches. In the Civil War it was a slave state that stayed in the Union, and Lee’s first invasion of the North was stopped here at Antietam.
Rhode Island. Roger Williams founded it in 1636 as a place where the government could not force anyone’s religion. It was so independent that it skipped the Constitutional Convention and ratified last, by just 34 votes to 32, in 1790.
Connecticut. Thomas Hooker’s settlers wrote the Fundamental Orders of 1639, often called the first written constitution in America. At the Convention, its delegates Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth proposed the Great Compromise that created today’s Senate and House.
Delaware. Swedes, then the Dutch, then the English ruled it. On 7 December 1787 it became the first state to ratify the Constitution — every vote was yes — so it calls itself “The First State”; in the Civil War it was a slave state that stayed in the Union.
Pennsylvania. William Penn, a Quaker, founded it in 1681 as a “holy experiment” where people of every faith could worship freely. Both the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution were written in its city of Philadelphia, and in 1863 Lee’s second invasion of the North failed at Gettysburg.
Jamestown. In May 1607, 104 English men and boys built James Fort here — the first permanent English settlement in America. In the “starving time” winter of 1609–10 most of the settlers died; archaeologists found the real fort’s remains in 1994.
Hudson River. Henry Hudson sailed up it in 1609 looking for a sea route to Asia, and the Dutch then built fur-trading posts along it. When the Erie Canal opened in 1825, boats could travel from the Hudson all the way to Lake Erie.
Delaware River. On Christmas night 1776, George Washington’s army crossed this icy river in boats and surprised the enemy at Trenton the next morning. Every Christmas Day, people in costume re-enact the crossing at Washington Crossing.
Houston. About 2.3 million people live in Houston today — more than lived in all 13 colonies in 1770. NASA’s Mission Control here guided the astronauts; the first words from the Moon in 1969 were “Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.”
Vermont. Vermont was not one of the 13 colonies: New York and New Hampshire both claimed it, so in 1777 it declared itself an independent republic — and its constitution was the first in America to ban adult slavery. It joined the United States as the 14th state in 1791.
James River. In 1607 three English ships sailed up this river, named for King James I, and the colonists built Jamestown on its bank. Later, tobacco plantations worked by enslaved people lined its shores.
Point Comfort. Today: Fort Monroe, Hampton, Virginia. In late August 1619 an English ship, the White Lion, brought “20 and odd” Africans here, captured from a Portuguese slave ship — the first recorded Africans in English America. Today the spot is part of Fort Monroe National Monument.
Plymouth. In December 1620 the Mayflower’s Pilgrims began their colony here, after signing the Mayflower Compact on board to govern themselves. About half of them died that first winter; in 1621 the Wampanoag leader Massasoit made a treaty with the survivors.
Manhattan. In 1626 the Dutch “bought” this island from the Lenape for trade goods worth 60 guilders — but the Lenape probably thought they were agreeing to share the land, not selling it forever. The Dutch town of New Amsterdam at its southern tip later became New York City.
New France. Today: Quebec, Canada. France’s colony stretched from Quebec along the St. Lawrence River and the Great Lakes; most French colonists were fur traders who worked closely with Native nations. After the French and Indian War, France lost Canada to Britain in 1763.
Quebec. Samuel de Champlain founded it in 1608 on cliffs above the St. Lawrence River, and it became the capital of New France. It is the only city north of Mexico that still has its old city walls, and most people there speak French.
New Netherland. Today: New York and New Jersey. The Dutch built fur-trading posts along the Hudson River and the town of New Amsterdam on Manhattan. In 1664 English warships sailed in, the Dutch gave up without a fight, and New Amsterdam became New York.
Providence. Roger Williams, banished from Massachusetts for his ideas, founded it in 1636 on land he got from the Narragansett people. He named it for God’s “providence” (care); today it is the capital of Rhode Island.
Hartford. In 1636 the minister Thomas Hooker led about 100 settlers, driving their cattle, through the woods from Massachusetts to found it. Today it is the capital of Connecticut, and Mark Twain wrote “Tom Sawyer” in his house here.
New Sweden. Today: Delaware and nearby. Sweden’s only colony in America lay along the Delaware River; the Dutch took it in 1655, and the English took it from them in 1664. Its Swedish and Finnish settlers probably brought the log cabin to America.
Fort Christina. Today: Wilmington, Delaware. Swedish and Finnish settlers built this fort in 1638, named for Sweden’s 12-year-old Queen Christina — the first permanent European settlement in the Delaware Valley. They may have built America’s first log cabins here.
Ohio Valley. Britain and France both claimed this rich land along the Ohio River, home to the Shawnee, Lenape and other Native nations. In 1754 a 22-year-old George Washington fired some of the first shots here, starting the French and Indian War.
Boston Harbor. On the night of 16 December 1773, Patriots disguised as Mohawk Indians dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea into this harbor — the Boston Tea Party. Britain punished the town by closing the port in 1774.
King Street. Today: State Street, Boston. On 5 March 1770 British soldiers fired into an angry crowd here, killing five people, including Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native American descent. A ring of cobblestones in front of the Old State House marks the spot today.
Fort Ticonderoga. In May 1775 Ethan Allen and the Green Mountain Boys captured this fort by surprise. That winter Henry Knox dragged 59 of its cannons about 300 miles on ox sledges to Boston, forcing the British to leave the city.
Bunker Hill (Breed’s Hill). Today: Charlestown, Boston. On 17 June 1775 most of the fighting was actually on Breed’s Hill: the British took it on their third charge but lost more than 1,000 men killed or wounded. Peter Salem, a Black soldier who had been enslaved, fought here; a 221-foot granite obelisk stands on the hill today.
Long Island. Today: Brooklyn, New York City. On 27 August 1776 the British attacked Washington’s army in Brooklyn in the largest battle of the whole war and beat it badly. The battlefield now lies inside New York City, under Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery.
East River. After losing the Battle of Long Island, Washington secretly ferried about 9,000 soldiers across this tidal strait in a single night, hidden by a lucky morning fog. Today the Brooklyn Bridge crosses it.
Trenton. At dawn on 26 December 1776, after crossing the icy Delaware, Washington’s army surprised the Hessian soldiers here and captured about 900. Today Trenton is New Jersey’s capital, and the Old Barracks where the Hessians stayed still stands.
Princeton. On 3 January 1777, a week after Trenton, Washington slipped around the British army at night and beat its rear guard here. The fighting reached Nassau Hall of the college that is now Princeton University.
Valley Forge. In the hard winter of 1777–78, about 2,000 of Washington’s 12,000 soldiers died here of disease. The Prussian officer Baron von Steuben drilled the rest into a real army; rebuilt log huts stand in the park today.
Monmouth. Today: Freehold, New Jersey. On 28 June 1778, in heat near 100°F (38°C), the two armies fought all day with no clear winner — many soldiers collapsed from heatstroke. Legend says “Molly Pitcher” carried water to the troops here.
Kings Mountain. On 7 October 1780 the backcountry “Overmountain Men” climbed this wooded ridge in South Carolina and wiped out a Loyalist force in about an hour. Almost everyone who fought was American — Patriots against Loyalists.
Cowpens. On 17 January 1781 Daniel Morgan set a clever trap in this open cattle pasture: his militia fired two shots and fell back, and the charging British ran straight into his steady regulars. Nearly the whole British force was killed or captured.
Guilford Courthouse. Today: Greensboro, North Carolina. On 15 March 1781 Cornwallis won the field against Nathanael Greene but lost about a quarter of his army. Worn out, he marched north to Virginia — and toward defeat at Yorktown.
Chesapeake Bay. On 5 September 1781 a French fleet under Admiral de Grasse drove off the British navy at the mouth of this bay, so no ships could rescue Cornwallis at Yorktown. The Chesapeake is the largest estuary in the United States.
Minute Man National Historical Park. On 19 April 1775 British troops marched to Concord to seize Patriot weapons; at the North Bridge the militia fired back — Emerson’s “shot heard round the world.” All along the Battle Road back to Boston, militiamen shot at the British from behind stone walls and trees.
Independence Hall. The Declaration of Independence was signed here in 1776, and the Constitution was written here in the summer of 1787 — both in the same Assembly Room. George Washington’s “rising sun” chair still stands at the front of the room.
Liberty Bell. This bell carries the words “Proclaim LIBERTY throughout all the Land.” Abolitionists fighting slavery gave it its name; its famous crack grew so big that it has not rung since 1846.
US Capitol. Congress — the Senate and the House of Representatives — makes the nation’s laws here; enslaved workers helped build it. Under its great dome, the Rotunda displays John Trumbull’s huge paintings of the Declaration and of Cornwallis’s surrender.
Springfield. Today: Springfield Armory National Historic Site, Massachusetts. In January 1787 Daniel Shays led about 1,500 angry farmers, crushed by debts and taxes, to seize the weapons stored here; state militia fired cannon and scattered them. The weak national government had no army to send — one reason leaders wanted a new Constitution.
Federal Hall. On 30 April 1789 George Washington took the oath as the first President on a balcony here, when New York was the nation’s capital. Today’s building (1842) stands on the same spot on Wall Street, with a big statue of Washington on its steps.
Des Moines. In December 1965 students here, including 13-year-old Mary Beth Tinker, wore black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War and were suspended. In 1969 the Supreme Court ruled that students do not lose free speech “at the schoolhouse gate.”
Iowa. A farm state in the middle of the country, one of the biggest growers of corn in the US. The Tinker armband case, which protects students’ free speech, began in schools in its capital, Des Moines.
Supreme Court Building. The nine justices of the Supreme Court hear cases in this marble building. Carved above its front columns are the words “Equal Justice Under Law.”
National Constitution Center. A museum about the Constitution on Independence Mall in Philadelphia, opened in 2003. In Signers’ Hall you can stand among life-size bronze statues of 42 delegates of the 1787 Convention.
Oregon Country. Today: Oregon, Washington and Idaho. Home to the Chinook, Nez Perce and many other Native nations, this rainy, forested land was shared by the US and Britain from 1818, and thousands of settlers came on the Oregon Trail in the 1840s. In 1846 a treaty split it along the 49th parallel — still the US–Canada border.
New Spain. Today: Mexico and the US Southwest. Spain’s empire in North America stretched from Central America to today’s California and Texas, ruled from Mexico City. It ended when Mexico won its independence in 1821.
St. Louis. In May 1804 Lewis and Clark’s Corps of Discovery set out from near here up the Missouri River. Today the 630-foot Gateway Arch, the tallest monument in the US, remembers the city as the “Gateway to the West.”
New Orleans. Whoever held this port near the mouth of the Mississippi controlled the river trade, so Jefferson tried to buy it — and in 1803 Napoleon offered all of Louisiana instead. It was the South’s biggest city (and largest slave market) when the US Navy captured it in 1862; in 1960 six-year-old Ruby Bridges integrated a school here.
Santa Fe. Founded by the Spanish around 1610, it is the oldest state capital in the US. After 1821 traders drove wagons about 900 miles along the Santa Fe Trail from Missouri to sell their goods here.
Red River Basin. Today: parts of North Dakota and Minnesota. The land drained by the Red River of the North, which flows north into Canada — unusual for an American river. The US gained its southern part from Britain in the Convention of 1818, which set the border at 49°N.
Mexican Cession. Today: California, Nevada, Utah and parts of four more states. After the Mexican–American War, Mexico gave up about half its land in the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, and the US paid $15 million. Tens of thousands of Mexicans and many Native nations living there suddenly found themselves inside the United States.
Gadsden Purchase. Today: southern Arizona and New Mexico. In 1853 the US paid Mexico $10 million for this strip of desert south of the Gila River, because it offered the flattest route for a railroad to California. It completed the outline of the lower 48 states.
Louisiana. Today’s state is only a small corner of the huge Louisiana Purchase; it became the 18th state in 1812. It seceded in January 1861, lost New Orleans to the Union in April 1862, and was readmitted in 1868.
Northwest Territory. Today: Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, part of Minnesota. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 banned slavery here and promised that new states made from it would be equal to the old ones; five states came from it. The Shawnee, Miami and other nations fought hard to keep their lands but lost their British allies after the War of 1812.
Lake of the Woods. A huge lake with more than 14,000 islands on the US–Canada border. The 1818 border ran from here west along the 49th parallel to the Rocky Mountains, and an old mapping mistake left Minnesota’s “Northwest Angle” cut off from the rest of the US.
Red River (Texas–Oklahoma). Named for its reddish mud, this river forms much of the border between Texas and Oklahoma. In 1819 the US and Spain agreed to make it part of the boundary between their lands.
The Alamo. Today: San Antonio, Texas. In 1836 about 200 defenders, including Davy Crockett, Jim Bowie and several Tejanos (Mexican Texans), held this old mission church for 13 days before Mexico’s army stormed it. “Remember the Alamo!” became the Texans’ battle cry.
San Jacinto. Today: near Houston, Texas. On 21 April 1836 Sam Houston’s army surprised Santa Anna’s camp here during the afternoon rest and won in about 18 minutes, shouting “Remember the Alamo!” The victory won Texas its independence; a 567-foot monument now stands on the battlefield.
Nueces River. Texas said its southern border was the Rio Grande; Mexico said it was this river, farther north. When US troops marched into the land between the two rivers in 1846, the Mexican–American War began.
Rio Grande. In 1846 US and Mexican soldiers clashed near this river, starting the Mexican–American War. Today it forms the border between Texas and Mexico for about 1,250 miles; in Mexico it is called the Río Bravo.
Wyoming. A high, windy land of mountains and plains crossed by the Oregon Trail — you can still see wagon ruts near Guernsey. In 1869 Wyoming Territory became the first place in the US to give women the right to vote.
Washington. The northern part of the old Oregon Country became Washington, the 42nd state, in 1889 — the only state named after a president. Microsoft, founded in Albuquerque, moved to the Seattle area in 1979.
Idaho. Part of the old Oregon Country; the Oregon Trail followed the Snake River across its southern plains. Today it grows about a third of all the potatoes in the US.
Oregon. Today’s state is just the southwestern part of the old Oregon Country; it became the 33rd state in 1859. Its first state constitution even tried to ban Black people from moving there — a law not fully removed until 1926.
Nevada. Part of the land Mexico gave up in 1848, it was rushed into the Union on 31 October 1864, in the middle of the Civil War, after rich silver was found at the Comstock Lode. Hoover Dam stands on its border with Arizona.
Utah. Part of the Mexican Cession, where Mormon pioneers founded Salt Lake City in 1847. The two halves of the first transcontinental railroad met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869.
Gila River. A desert river that flows west across Arizona to the Colorado River; the Akimel O’odham (Pima) people have farmed its valley for centuries, and their ancestors dug long irrigation canals. The Gadsden Purchase added the flat land south of it.
Alaska. The US bought Alaska from Russia in 1867 for $7.2 million — about 2 cents an acre — and critics called it “Seward’s Folly.” It is by far the largest state, more than twice the size of Texas.
Hawaii. Native Hawaiians had their own kingdom until American businessmen overthrew Queen Liliuokalani in 1893, and the US annexed the islands in 1898. Japan attacked Pearl Harbor here on 7 December 1941; Hawaii became the 50th state in 1959.
Independence, Missouri. In the 1840s wagon trains bought oxen, flour and supplies here before starting the 2,000-mile Oregon Trail. Today the National Frontier Trails Museum tells their story.
Oregon City. The end of the Oregon Trail, beside the falls of the Willamette River. In 1844 it became the first incorporated US city west of the Rocky Mountains.
Sutter’s Mill. Today: Coloma, California. On 24 January 1848 James Marshall spotted shiny flakes in the ditch below the sawmill he was building for John Sutter — gold! The Gold Rush brought about 300,000 people to California, and for California’s Native peoples it brought disaster.
Lake Erie. The shallowest and warmest of the five Great Lakes, named after the Erie people who once lived on its shores. In 1825 the Erie Canal linked it to the Hudson River, so boats could travel from the Great Lakes to New York City.
Buffalo. A city at the eastern end of Lake Erie and the western end of the Erie Canal. After 1825, shipping goods from Buffalo to New York City became about 90% cheaper, and the small village boomed; Niagara Falls is only about 20 miles away.
Wangxia. Today: Macao. In 1844 China and the United States signed their first treaty in this village, the Treaty of Wangxia; it was one of the unequal treaties forced on China after the Opium War. It was signed at the Kun Iam Temple, where a round stone table from that day is still kept.
Kansas River. The first big river crossing on the Oregon Trail: families paid about $5 for a ferry or risked fording it with their wagons. It flows east to join the Missouri River at Kansas City.
Fort Kearny. An army post built in 1848 beside the Platte River to protect travelers, about 320 miles along the trail. Emigrants could pick up mail, buy supplies and fix wagons here.
Chimney Rock. This clay-and-sandstone spire rises about 300 feet above the plains of western Nebraska and could be seen for days before emigrants reached it. More travelers wrote about it in their diaries than about any other landmark on the trail.
Fort Laramie. Tired emigrants rested at this trading post and fort; goods cost three times Missouri prices, so many families left heavy furniture behind here. In 1851 and 1868 the US signed treaties with the Lakota and other nations here — promises it later broke.
Independence Rock. A huge granite dome where emigrants carved or painted their names — more than 5,000 can still be found. Wagon trains tried to reach it by the Fourth of July so they would cross the mountains before the snow.
South Pass. A wide, gentle saddle where wagons could cross the Rocky Mountains — so gentle that some emigrants did not notice they had crossed the Continental Divide. West of it the trail split into the Sublette Cutoff and the road to Fort Bridger.
Fort Hall. A fur-trading post on the Snake River, built in 1834. Nearby, Shoshone and Bannock people traded dried salmon with emigrants — most meetings on the trail were peaceful; today the area is part of the Fort Hall Reservation.
Snake River. The Oregon Trail followed this river for hundreds of miles across today’s southern Idaho, through dry, rocky canyons. Crossing it was so dangerous that many wagons stayed on the hot, dusty south side instead.
Blue Mountains. Steep, forested mountains in northeastern Oregon, one of the last big obstacles on the trail. Emigrants chopped a road through the trees and let their wagons down the slopes with ropes, racing the early snow.
The Dalles. Here the wagon road ended at the Columbia River. Families either built rafts to float their wagons through the dangerous rapids or, after 1846, paid a toll to take the Barlow Road around Mount Hood.
Platte River. Emigrants followed this wide, shallow river across Nebraska for weeks — people joked that it was “a mile wide and an inch deep.” Cholera spread fast in the crowded camps along its banks.
Fort Bridger. Mountain man Jim Bridger opened this trading post in 1843, where emigrants could rest, buy supplies and repair wagons. The route through it was longer than the Sublette Cutoff but had more water and grass.
Three Island Crossing. Today: Glenns Ferry, Idaho. Here emigrants used islands in the Snake River as stepping stones to ford to the north side, a shorter route with better grass and water. The current was strong, and many who could not cross safely stayed on the south bank.
Columbia River. The great river of the Pacific Northwest cuts a deep gorge through the Cascade Mountains. Lewis and Clark canoed down it to the Pacific in 1805; later, emigrants on rafts risked its rapids below The Dalles.
Mount Hood. Oregon’s highest peak (11,249 feet) is a snow-capped volcano. The Barlow Road, opened in 1846, went around its south side on the last stretch to Oregon City.
Laurel Hill. The scariest slope on the Barlow Road: wagons slid down a steep, rocky chute while emigrants held them back with ropes wrapped around trees. Some even dragged whole trees behind their wagons as brakes.
Oregon Trail ruts near Guernsey. Today: Oregon Trail Ruts State Historic Site, Wyoming. So many wagons rolled over this soft sandstone ridge that their wheels wore ruts up to about 5 feet deep. They are some of the best-preserved Oregon Trail ruts anywhere.
Trail of Tears State Park. In the bitter winter of 1838–39, Cherokee families on the forced removal waited for weeks to cross the ice-choked Mississippi River here. Many died of cold and sickness; the park in Missouri remembers them.
San Antonio. Founded by the Spanish in 1718 around a chain of missions, one of which was the Alamo, besieged in 1836. Today the River Walk winds through downtown below street level, lined with cafés.
Custer County. The photographer Solomon Butcher took thousands of pictures of homesteaders here, like the Mitchell family’s sod house in 1888. With almost no trees on the plains, settlers cut blocks of grassy earth to build their homes.
Lake Michigan. The only one of the five Great Lakes that lies entirely inside the United States; Chicago sits on its southwestern shore. Its sand dunes are among the largest freshwater dunes in the world.
Los Angeles. Founded in 1781 by 44 Spanish-speaking settlers, more than half of them of African or Native descent, it is now the second-largest city in the US. The Southern Pacific’s Sunset Route linked it to New Orleans in 1883, and in the Great Migration many Black families moved here from the South.
New Mexico. Most of it came from Mexico in 1848 and its southern strip in the Gadsden Purchase of 1853; it became a state in 1912, and part of the Navajo Nation lies here. Scientists at Los Alamos built the first atomic bomb, tested at Trinity in 1945, and Microsoft was founded in Albuquerque in 1975.
Bull Run (Manassas). Today: Manassas National Battlefield Park, Virginia. Crowds from Washington brought picnics to watch, expecting a quick Union win — instead the Confederates won, and General Thomas Jackson held his line so firmly that he was nicknamed “Stonewall”. Today you can walk the rolling fields of Henry Hill.
Shiloh. Confederates surprised Grant’s army at dawn near a little log church called Shiloh; Grant held on and won on the second day. About 23,000 soldiers were killed, wounded or missing — more than in all of America’s earlier wars put together.
Antietam. About 23,000 soldiers were killed or wounded here near Sharpsburg, Maryland, in a single day — still the bloodiest day in American history. Lee’s army had to turn back, which gave Lincoln the moment to announce the Emancipation Proclamation. The stone Burnside Bridge over Antietam Creek still stands.
Vicksburg. This city on high bluffs above the Mississippi was called the “Gibraltar of the Confederacy”. During the 47-day siege, families dug caves into the hillsides to hide from the shells; when it surrendered to Grant on 4 July 1863, the Union controlled the whole Mississippi River.
Appomattox Court House. Today: Appomattox Court House National Historical Park, Virginia. In the parlor of Wilmer McLean’s brick house in this little village, Lee surrendered his army to Grant, and the Civil War was nearly over. Grant let the hungry Confederate soldiers keep their horses and sent them food. Odd fact: McLean had moved here to get away from the war — his old farm was at Bull Run!
Ford’s Theatre. Five days after Lee’s surrender, John Wilkes Booth shot President Lincoln during a comedy here; Lincoln died the next morning in a house across the street. Today it is a working theater again, with a museum downstairs.
Montgomery. In February 1861 the seceded states met here, formed the Confederacy and chose Jefferson Davis as president. Almost a century later, Rosa Parks’s arrest started the 381-day Montgomery bus boycott, and in 1965 the Selma marchers ended their walk at the very same state capitol.
Arkansas. Arkansas left the Union in May 1861, after Fort Sumter, and was readmitted in 1868. In 1957 its governor, Orval Faubus, sent the state National Guard to keep nine Black students out of Little Rock’s Central High School.
West Virginia. When Virginia seceded, the mountain counties in the west refused to go along — few families there enslaved people. They broke away and joined the Union as the new state of West Virginia in 1863, right in the middle of the war.
Galveston. On 19 June 1865, two months after the war ended, Union General Gordon Granger arrived in this Texas port and announced that all enslaved people in Texas were free. Their celebration became Juneteenth, now a national holiday.
Combahee River. On a June night in 1863, Union gunboats guided by Harriet Tubman steamed up this South Carolina river and freed more than 700 enslaved people from the rice plantations. Tubman became the first woman to help lead an armed raid in the Civil War.
Boston Common. Facing the Massachusetts State House stands a bronze memorial to Colonel Robert Gould Shaw and the 54th Massachusetts, one of the first Black regiments in the Union army. Cows grazed on the Common until 1830!
Cold Harbor. Here, near Richmond, Grant ordered a head-on attack against dug-in Confederate trenches on 3 June 1864, and thousands of Union soldiers fell in about an hour — Grant later said he regretted it. The old trench lines can still be seen in the woods.
Frederick Douglass National Historic Site. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery as a young man, spent his last years in this hilltop house in Washington, D.C., which he called Cedar Hill. From the porch you can see the US Capitol, and his desk and more than 1,000 of his books are still inside.
Cobb County. A county northwest of Atlanta, home to Marietta and Kennesaw Mountain, where a big Civil War battle was fought in 1864. This site’s lessons were checked against Cobb County’s school frameworks for Georgia standards.
Promontory Summit. Today: Golden Spike National Historical Park, Utah. On 10 May 1869 the Central Pacific and Union Pacific tracks met here, and a golden spike was tapped in — a telegraph sent one word, “DONE”, across the country. Today working copies of the two locomotives, Jupiter and No. 119, steam up and face each other nose to nose.
Sacramento. The Central Pacific began building east from California’s capital in 1863. Almost every rail, engine and spike had to come by ship all the way around South America first!
Omaha. The Union Pacific began laying track west from Omaha, on the Missouri River, in 1865. Until a bridge opened in 1872, trains from the east crossed the river by ferry — and in one winter, on tracks laid across the ice!
Pittsburgh. Coal, iron and three rivers made Pittsburgh America’s “Steel City”. Andrew Carnegie’s Edgar Thomson Steel Works opened nearby in 1875 using the Bessemer process, and immigrants from Europe worked its fiery furnaces — it still makes steel today. In 1920 the city’s KDKA began the first regular radio broadcasts.
Kitty Hawk. The Wright brothers picked these windy sand dunes on North Carolina’s Outer Banks for steady winds and soft landings. On 17 December 1903 Orville flew 120 feet in 12 seconds — shorter than the wingspan of a jumbo jet. A granite monument now crowns Big Kill Devil Hill.
Allegheny Mountains. A long, rugged part of the Appalachians running through Pennsylvania, Maryland and West Virginia. To get trains over them, the Pennsylvania Railroad dug tunnels and built the famous Horseshoe Curve.
Horseshoe Curve. To climb the steep Alleghenies near Altoona, the railroad bent its tracks into a giant U-shaped bend. It opened in 1854, and long freight trains still squeal around it today; in World War II, German saboteurs were sent to blow it up but were caught first.
Baltimore. To win trade with the West, Baltimore began building the Baltimore & Ohio in 1828, one of America’s first railroads; by 1857 it reached St. Louis. Its old roundhouse is now the B&O Railroad Museum, full of historic locomotives.
Missouri River. North America’s longest river, flowing about 2,340 miles from Montana to the Mississippi. The railroad from Chicago reached its bank at Council Bluffs, across from Omaha, in 1867.
Sierra Nevada. A wall of granite mountains along eastern California. Thousands of Chinese workers blasted 15 tunnels through it for the Central Pacific, sometimes gaining only about a foot a day, and kept working through winters of deep snow.
Abilene. In 1867 a businessman built cattle pens next to the Kansas Pacific railroad in this Kansas town, and Texas cowboys drove huge herds up the Chisholm Trail to load them onto trains heading east. Later it was the hometown of President Dwight D. Eisenhower.
Washington Territory. Today: Washington State. Formed in 1853 in the far Northwest. The Northern Pacific Railroad, finished in 1883, crossed the northern plains and Rocky Mountains to reach it, and settlers poured in; it became the state of Washington in 1889.
St. Paul. Minnesota’s capital, on the Mississippi River. From here James J. Hill’s Great Northern Railway reached all the way to Seattle in 1893 — built without the free government land the other transcontinental lines got.
Seattle. A port city on Puget Sound, at the western end of James J. Hill’s Great Northern Railway (1893). Its Space Needle was built for the 1962 World’s Fair, and on clear days snowy Mount Rainier looms behind the city.
Union Stock Yards. Opened in Chicago in 1865, it became the biggest meatpacking center in the world: trains brought in cattle and hogs, and millions of animals passed through every year. The yards closed in 1971, but the old stone entrance gate still stands.
Detroit. Henry Ford made Detroit the “Motor City”: his Model T came out in 1908 and his moving assembly line in 1913. Black families came here in the Great Migration for factory jobs, and in World War II the Willow Run plant nearby built bombers.
Michigan. A Great Lakes state shaped like a mitten, plus an Upper Peninsula across the water. Detroit and Highland Park, where Ford’s factories changed how the world builds things, are here.
Highland Park plant. Ford added the moving assembly line here in 1913: instead of workers walking around the car, the car was pulled past them, and each worker did one job. Building a chassis dropped from about 12½ hours to about 1½ hours.
Dayton. Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio, and built their gliders and the 1903 Flyer here before shipping them to Kitty Hawk. Their old cycle shop is now part of a national historical park.
Ohio. A Midwest state between Lake Erie and the Ohio River. It calls itself the “Birthplace of Aviation” because the Wright brothers lived in Dayton — and astronauts John Glenn and Neil Armstrong were born in Ohio too.
Menlo Park. Today: Edison, New Jersey. Thomas Edison built his “invention factory” in this New Jersey village in 1876; the phonograph (1877) and a long-lasting light bulb (1879) came from here, and he was called the “Wizard of Menlo Park”. Henry Ford later moved the lab building to his museum in Michigan, and the town was renamed Edison.
Tuskegee Institute. Today: Tuskegee University, Alabama. Booker T. Washington led this school for Black students, and from 1896 George Washington Carver taught farmers here to rotate crops like peanuts and sweet potatoes. From 1941 the Tuskegee Airmen, the first Black military pilots, learned to fly at nearby Moton Field, where the old hangars are now a national historic site.
New York Harbor. One of the world’s great natural harbors, where the Hudson River meets the Atlantic. Millions of immigrants sailed in past the Statue of Liberty and were checked at Ellis Island before starting their new lives.
Castle Garden. Today: Castle Clinton National Monument, Battery Park, New York. Before Ellis Island, about 8 million immigrants entered America through this round stone fort at the tip of Manhattan. Built as a fort before the War of 1812, it later became a concert hall and even an aquarium — today it is Castle Clinton, where you buy ferry tickets to the Statue of Liberty.
Statue of Liberty. France gave this copper statue to the United States, and it was dedicated in 1886; Emma Lazarus’s poem on the pedestal welcomes the “huddled masses yearning to breathe free”. It was once copper-brown and slowly turned green — and even with its pedestal it is shorter than a Saturn V rocket.
Angel Island. This island station in San Francisco Bay held immigrants arriving from across the Pacific. Because of the Chinese Exclusion Act, many Chinese were kept for weeks or even months, and some carved sad and angry poems into the wooden walls — you can still read them today.
San Francisco Bay. A huge natural harbor in northern California, entered through the Golden Gate, with Angel Island and Alcatraz inside. In World War II its shipyards drew workers from all over the country, including many Black families in the Great Migration.
Titusville. In 1859 Edwin Drake drilled America’s first commercial oil well near this Pennsylvania town, striking oil at 69½ feet. Within a few years the valley was crowded with wooden derricks in a real oil rush.
Puerto Rico. A Caribbean island that the United States took from Spain after the Spanish–American War of 1898. Puerto Ricans have been US citizens since 1917, and the old Spanish fortress El Morro still guards San Juan’s harbor.
Cuba. Cuba won independence from Spain after the Spanish–American War of 1898. In October 1962 American spy planes found Soviet missile sites here, only 90 miles from Florida, and for 13 days the world feared a nuclear war.
Wusong. Today: Baoshan District, Shanghai. In 1876 a British company opened China’s first railway, a line of about 15 km from Shanghai to Wusong at the mouth of the Huangpu River. The Qing government bought it the next year — and tore it up.
Minnesota. A northern state of forests, farms and “10,000 lakes”, where the Mississippi River begins. In the late 1800s many immigrant families from Scandinavia, Germany and the Netherlands came to farm here — like the Dykhof family in the lesson.
Salado. A small Texas town on the old Chisholm Trail between Waco and Austin. Cowboys driving cattle north to Kansas passed through, and the Stagecoach Inn still welcomes travelers today.
Waco. The Chisholm Trail crossed the Brazos River at Waco, Texas. Its Suspension Bridge, finished in 1870, was the first bridge over the river, and cowboys paid a toll to drive their herds across it on the way to Kansas.
Austin. The capital of Texas, on the Colorado River of Texas. The Chisholm Trail passed between Waco and Austin, and today the pink-granite Texas Capitol is even taller than the US Capitol.
Brooklyn Bridge. When it opened in 1883 it was the longest suspension bridge in the world, hanging from four huge cables of steel wire. To prove it was safe, the showman P. T. Barnum led 21 elephants across it in 1884!
Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum. On the National Mall in Washington, D.C., this museum shows the real 1903 Wright Flyer, a Sputnik 1 replica and the Apollo 11 command module, Columbia. It is one of the most visited museums in the world — and it is free.
Thomas Edison National Historical Park. In 1887 Edison moved to a much bigger laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey, where his team built movie cameras and better phonographs. Visitors can see his lab, his huge library and a copy of the “Black Maria”, the first movie studio.
George Washington Carver National Monument. George Washington Carver was born into slavery on a farm here, near Diamond, Missouri, around 1864. In 1943 it became the first national monument honoring an African American, and you can walk a trail through the woods where young Carver studied plants.
Topeka. In Kansas’s capital, third-grader Linda Brown had to travel far to a school for Black children, though a white school was close to home. Her family’s case, Brown v. Board of Education, led the Supreme Court on 17 May 1954 to rule school segregation unconstitutional; Topeka’s Monroe School is now a national historical park.
Little Rock. In September 1957 nine Black students — the Little Rock Nine — tried to enter all-white Central High School in Arkansas’s capital; President Eisenhower sent soldiers of the 101st Airborne to escort them past angry crowds. The school is still open today, and it is also a National Park Service historic site.
Greensboro. On 1 February 1960 four Black college students sat down at the “whites only” lunch counter in Greensboro’s Woolworth’s and politely asked to be served. Sit-ins spread to dozens of cities, and in July 1960 the counter began serving Black customers; the old store is now the International Civil Rights Center and Museum.
Anniston. On 14 May 1961 a mob chased a Greyhound bus carrying Freedom Riders out of this Alabama town and set it on fire with a firebomb. All the riders escaped, and more Freedom Riders soon took their place; the old bus station is now part of the Freedom Riders National Monument.
Albany, New York. The capital of New York State, on the Hudson River. Don’t mix it up with Albany, Georgia, where the Albany Movement for civil rights took place in 1961–1962.
16th Street Baptist Church. This Birmingham church was a meeting place for civil rights marchers. On a Sunday morning in September 1963, a bomb planted by Ku Klux Klan members killed four girls — Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson and Carol Denise McNair — and the nation’s shock helped push Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act.
Edmund Pettus Bridge. On 7 March 1965 state troopers beat peaceful voting-rights marchers led by John Lewis and Hosea Williams on this bridge in Selma — “Bloody Sunday”. Two weeks later thousands crossed it again on the march to Montgomery, and the Voting Rights Act followed that August.
Lorraine Motel. Today: National Civil Rights Museum, Memphis. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. had come to Memphis to support striking sanitation workers when he was shot on the balcony outside Room 306 on 4 April 1968. The motel is now the National Civil Rights Museum, and a wreath hangs on the balcony where he stood.
University of Mississippi (Ole Miss). In September 1962 James Meredith became the first Black student at this university in Oxford, Mississippi. A violent riot broke out on campus, and President Kennedy sent federal marshals and thousands of soldiers to protect him; a statue of Meredith now stands on the campus.
William Frantz Elementary School. On 14 November 1960, six-year-old Ruby Bridges walked into this New Orleans school past shouting crowds, guarded by federal marshals, as its first Black student. For most of that year she was the only child in her class, taught by her teacher Barbara Henry.
Reflecting Pool. This long pool stretches from the Lincoln Memorial toward the Washington Monument and mirrors it in the water. On 28 August 1963 about 250,000 people crowded around it for the March on Washington, where Dr. King gave his “I Have a Dream” speech.
Trinity site. Today: White Sands Missile Range, New Mexico. At 5:29 a.m. on 16 July 1945, Manhattan Project scientists exploded the first atomic bomb in this New Mexico desert. The flash lit up the sky for many miles around, and the heat melted the sand into a green glass now called trinitite. The site is open to visitors only on a few days a year.
Los Alamos. In 1943 the Army took over a boys’ ranch school on a remote mesa in New Mexico, and scientists led by J. Robert Oppenheimer secretly designed the first atomic bombs there. All their mail went to one address: P.O. Box 1663, Santa Fe.
Vietnam. A long, narrow country in Southeast Asia. US combat troops arrived in 1965 to fight Communist North Vietnam — more than 500,000 by 1968; the last US troops left in 1973, and the war ended with the North’s victory in 1975. Today visitors come from all over the world to see the limestone islands of Ha Long Bay.
Cape Canaveral. Explorer 1, Alan Shepard, John Glenn and Apollo 11 all lifted off from this stretch of the Florida coast. Moon rockets were stacked in the Vehicle Assembly Building, whose doors — 456 feet high — are the tallest in the world.
Huntsville. At Redstone Arsenal, Wernher von Braun’s rocket team built the rocket that launched Explorer 1, America’s first satellite, in 1958, and NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center here developed the Saturn V Moon rocket. A real Saturn V now lies on its side at the U.S. Space & Rocket Center, home of Space Camp.
Bay of Pigs. In April 1961 about 1,400 Cuban exiles trained and armed by the CIA landed on this bay to overthrow Fidel Castro, but they were defeated within three days. The failure pushed Castro closer to the Soviet Union — one step toward the 1962 missile crisis.
UCLA. On 29 October 1969 a student in Leonard Kleinrock’s lab tried to type “LOGIN” to a computer at SRI — the system crashed after “LO”, so the first message on ARPANET, the internet’s ancestor, was “LO”. The room, 3420 Boelter Hall, is now a small museum.
SRI (Menlo Park). Today: SRI International, Menlo Park, California. A research institute in Menlo Park, California — not Edison’s Menlo Park in New Jersey. Its computer received ARPANET’s first message from UCLA in 1969, and SRI scientist Douglas Engelbart invented the computer mouse here.
UC Santa Barbara. A university on the Pacific coast near Santa Barbara, California; in 1969 it became one of the first four ARPANET computer sites. The campus sits right beside the ocean, with its own beach and lagoon.
University of Utah. In Salt Lake City, at the foot of the Wasatch Mountains, it was one of the first four ARPANET sites in 1969. Its computer-graphics pioneers made the famous “Utah teapot”, a 3D model still used to test graphics today.
Silicon Valley. The south end of San Francisco Bay, named for the silicon in computer chips. Intel’s first microprocessor (1971), Apple (1976), Google (1998) and the iPhone (2007) all came from here — and both Apple and Google started in a garage!
Albuquerque. Bill Gates and Paul Allen founded Microsoft in this New Mexico city in 1975, to be near the company that made the Altair computer; in 1979 they moved to Washington State. Every October hundreds of hot-air balloons fill its sky at the Balloon Fiesta.
World Trade Center. On 11 September 2001 terrorists flew two hijacked planes into the Twin Towers, and nearly 2,800 people died here. Today two huge waterfall pools fill the towers’ footprints, with the victims’ names cut into bronze around them, and One World Trade Center rises 1,776 feet nearby.
Pentagon. The five-sided headquarters of the US military stands in Arlington, Virginia, just across the Potomac River from Washington, D.C. On 11 September 2001 a hijacked plane struck it and 184 people were killed; a memorial with 184 benches now stands beside it.
Shanksville. On 11 September 2001 the passengers and crew of United Flight 93 fought back against the hijackers, and the plane crashed in a field near this Pennsylvania village instead of reaching Washington. The memorial’s Tower of Voices holds 40 wind chimes, one for each person on board.
Afghanistan. A mountainous country in Central and South Asia. The 9/11 attacks were planned by al-Qaeda, which had bases there, so in 2001 the United States went to war in Afghanistan; the last US troops left in 2021.
CERN. Europe’s particle-physics laboratory sits on the French–Swiss border near Geneva. Here Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web and put the first website online — and CERN gave the Web to everyone for free. Deep underground runs the Large Hadron Collider, a ring 27 km around.
Iraq. A country along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers — the land of ancient Mesopotamia. In August 1990 Iraq’s army invaded Kuwait, and in 1991 a US-led coalition drove it out in the Persian Gulf War.
Kuwait. A small, oil-rich country at the top of the Persian Gulf. Iraq invaded it in 1990; when a US-led coalition drove Iraq’s army out in 1991, the retreating troops set more than 600 oil wells on fire.
Miami. A big city near the southern tip of Florida, only about 230 miles from Havana. During the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis it was well within range of the Soviet missiles in Cuba.
Dallas. A big city in north Texas, one of the cities in the lesson’s missile-range calculator: Soviet missiles in Cuba could have reached it. In 1963 President Kennedy was assassinated here.
Minneapolis. Minnesota’s largest city, beside St. Paul on the Mississippi River, and one of the cities in the lesson’s missile-range calculator. The waterfall in its center once powered so many flour mills that it was called the “Flour Milling Capital of the World”.
Oakland. A port city across the bay from San Francisco. During World War II, Black families from the South came here in the second wave of the Great Migration to work in Oakland and the Bay Area shipyards.
Harlem. A neighborhood in northern Manhattan that became the heart of Black culture in the 1920s Harlem Renaissance — poet Langston Hughes, writer Zora Neale Hurston and jazz stars like Duke Ellington and Louis Armstrong. The Apollo Theater still holds its famous Amateur Night.
Dust Bowl. In the 1930s, drought and over-plowed prairie turned the southern Great Plains into a “Dust Bowl”. On Black Sunday, 14 April 1935, a wall of dust rolled over towns like Stratford, Texas, turning day into night, and thousands of farm families moved west to California.
Norris Dam. The first dam built by the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), on the Clinch River in Tennessee, begun in 1933 and finished in 1936. TVA dams across seven states stopped floods and brought electricity to farms that had never had it.
Oak Ridge. A whole city built in secret in the Tennessee hills for the Manhattan Project — by 1945 about 75,000 people lived there, yet it was on no map. Its giant plants, running on TVA electricity, made uranium fuel for the atomic bomb.
Hoover Dam. Thousands of workers desperate for Depression-era jobs built this 726-foot concrete dam across the Colorado River, between Nevada and Arizona; it was begun under President Hoover and finished in 1936 under FDR. It holds back Lake Mead and makes electricity for millions of people.
Willow Run. Ford built this enormous factory west of Detroit during World War II; at its peak it rolled out a B-24 Liberator bomber about every hour, part of America’s “arsenal of democracy”. Its airfield, Willow Run Airport, is still in use.
Richmond. Henry Kaiser’s four shipyards in Richmond, California, built 747 ships for World War II — one Liberty ship was put together in under five days. Thousands of women “Rosies” and Black workers from the South worked here; today it is the Rosie the Riveter/WWII Home Front National Historical Park.
Navajo Nation. The largest Native American reservation in the United States, spreading across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah, with its capital at Window Rock. In World War II Navajo code talkers sent battlefield messages in their own language — a code the enemy never broke.
Manzanar. Today: Manzanar National Historic Site, California. After Executive Order 9066 in 1942, more than 10,000 Japanese Americans — most of them US citizens — were forced to live behind barbed wire in this dusty camp below the Sierra Nevada. Ansel Adams photographed daily life here with Mt. Williamson rising behind, and a guard tower has been rebuilt at the site.
Omaha Beach. One of five Allied landing beaches in Normandy, France, on D-Day; American troops met the fiercest fighting here and suffered heavy losses. On the cliff above, the Normandy American Cemetery holds more than 9,000 white marble crosses and Stars of David.
