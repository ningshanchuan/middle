Look · Space

Travel through a shan-shui painting

Shan-shui (shānshuǐ, “mountains and water”) is a Chinese tradition of painting landscapes. Northern Song painter Guo Xi described three ways to look into a landscape. Change the viewpoint and trace the path your eyes take.

A painting can combine all three distances. In Guo Xi’s Early Spring, where does your eye turn from looking up to looking in?

Early Spring, Guo Xi, 1072, National Palace Museum, Taipei

Empty space does part of the painting

Liúbái is space left open on purpose. In Fan Kuan’s painting the pale gap between the mountain and the foreground reads like mist. That sense of quiet depth is part of the painting’s yìjìng, the mood and imagined world it creates.

What if you filled the mist in?

Imagine filling the gap with dark trees. The far mountain and foreground might merge, and the valley might feel shallower.

Unroll Wang Ximeng’s A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains

Begin at the right edge and move left. Watch where the blue-green peaks give way to open water. Where would you pause?

Measured from one edge of the crop, the golden-section lines sit at about 38.2% and 61.8%. Thirds sit at one-third and two-thirds.

Example · A crop 300 pixels wide

Find the distance from the left edge to each vertical guide in a 300-pixel crop. For thirds, 300 px ÷ 3 = 100 px and 300 px − 100 px = 200 px. For the golden section, the guides sit at about 300 px × 38.2% = 114.6 px and 300 px − 114.6 px = 185.4 px. Switch grids to see which pair sits closer to the center.

Make it · Two frames, two moods

Make a calm crop and a majestic crop from one photo, or sketch two thumbnails on paper. Write one sentence naming an edge you moved and the place your eye lands first.

Self-check · Which frame best conveys your mood?

See if the two frames send your eye to different places. For example, leave more quiet sky in one or bring the cliff close to the edge in the other.

Compare · Space and materials

Different routes through a landscape

In linear perspective, a set of parallel lines receding into the scene can appear to meet at one vanishing point. A shan-shui handscroll often combines shifting viewpoints (sǎndiǎn tòushì), so your eye can travel as the scroll opens.

One-point perspective and shifting viewpoints

Find the vanishing point on the left. Follow the dashed route past three viewpoints on the right. Find a shoreline receding in Turner’s painting, then find two views from different angles in Wang’s scroll.

Color and ink can both suggest depth

Fan Kuan used shades of ink and light washes for rock and mist. Wang used striking blues and greens. Turner softened distant forms with color and reflections. Pick a painting and point to one detail that suggests depth.

A common mix-up · Shan-shui can use vivid color, and a black-and-white photograph can build depth with values.
