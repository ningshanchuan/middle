Heat Transfer: Follow the Heat

Why does a metal spoon left in hot soup burn your fingers, while a wooden spoon doesn’t? Heat always flows from a hotter object to a colder one. Turn on the stove in this 3D kitchen and watch heat move by conduction, convection and radiation — then compare a metal handle with a wooden one.

Conduction

Heat passes by direct contact: fast-vibrating particles bump their neighbours. Mostly in solids.

🌀

Convection

A liquid or gas flows in a loop and carries heat with it: warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks.

☀️

Radiation

Heat travels as electromagnetic waves (mostly infrared). It needs no matter — it even crosses empty space.

Lab: the conductor race

Four rods of the same length have one end in a flame. Beads are stuck underneath with wax. As heat is conducted along each rod, the wax melts and the beads drop. Predict the winner, then press Start!

💡 Memory hintMetals are heat “highways”; wood and plastic are “traffic jams”. Pots are metal, but handles are often wood or plastic.
⚠️ Common mix-up:In the same room, a metal spoon and a wooden spoon are actually the same temperature! Metal feels colder because it conducts heat away from your hand faster.

Lab: temperature vs. thermal energy

The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move. Set the temperature and the amount of matter in each container, then put them in contact: heat flows from the hotter one to the colder one until their temperatures are equal.

⚠️ Common mix-up:Temperature is the AVERAGE kinetic energy of the particles; thermal energy is the TOTAL energy of all of them. A bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of boiling water, but the cup has the higher temperature.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S8P2d: plan and carry out investigations on the effects of heat transfer on molecular motion — collisions of atoms (conduction), through space (radiation), and currents in a liquid or gas (convection). It also supports the S8P2 big idea of energy conservation: heat flows from hot to cold until temperatures are equal (thermal equilibrium).

Classroom ideas: (1) With the stove off, ask students to predict whether the metal or wooden handle will get hot, then test it. (2) In the conductor race, have students rank the rods before pressing Start; discuss why metal feels colder than wood (it conducts heat away from your hand faster). (3) In the particle lab, compare a small cup of hot water with a big tub of warm water to separate temperature (average kinetic energy of particles) from thermal energy (total energy of all particles). Time in the simulations is sped up; temperatures are illustrative.
