Electrical energy (电能, diànnéng) tells you how much energy a device transfers. At steady power, electrical work W = Pt. In a DC circuit with steady voltage and current, W = UIt. Subtract the earlier meter reading from the later one to find the energy used.

Kilowatts measure power. Kilowatt-hours measure energy. In Chinese, one kilowatt-hour is one 度电 (dù diàn). The italic W in the formula stands for work. The upright W after a number is the symbol for watts.

Worked example · From meter readings to an electricity bill

Given a starting reading of 1284.0 kWh, an ending reading of 1391.5 kWh, and an example rate of $0.16 per kWh.

Find the energy used and the energy charge for the month.

Solution
  1. Subtract the earlier reading from the later one.
  2. Multiply by the rate. The energy charge is $17.20.

Challenge: At 110 V, what fraction of the 220 V current remains? What fraction of the power remains? Use the graph to check.

Electric power (电功率, diàngōnglǜ) is how fast electrical energy is transferred. One watt is one joule per second. Rated power is the power a device uses in normal operation at its rated voltage. Actual power is the power it uses under its current operating conditions.

Divide electrical work by time to find average power. In a DC circuit, multiply voltage and current to find electric power.

For a purely resistive load, substitute Ohm’s law U = IR to get two more ways to calculate power.

The model holds the filament at 1210 Ω. A real incandescent filament cools at lower voltage and its resistance falls. The glow is illustrative. US textbooks often use V for voltage where PEP uses U. For a purely resistive AC circuit, the same formulas give average power when U and I are their effective (RMS) values.

To measure a bulb’s power in a low-voltage lab circuit, put an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the bulb. Multiply the readings taken at the same operating point.

Same current. Which resistor releases more heat?

Resistor A · 4 Ω

Challenge: Set B to 8 Ω, keep time above zero, and switch between the two connections. Which resistor produces more heat each time? Why does the comparison reverse?

A current through a resistor transfers electrical energy into thermal energy. This is Joule heating. For constant current and resistance, Q = I²Rt. Use amperes, ohms and seconds to get joules. With resistance and time fixed, doubling current gives four times as much heat.

Compare the same run time in both cases. With equal current, the greater resistance releases more heat. With equal voltage, the smaller resistance draws more current and releases more heat. This effect is useful in a heater. Overheated wiring can start a fire.

What makes a breaker trip?

Compare an overload with a short circuit. Play the animation or scrub through the stages to see which paths carry current.

Animation stages: Normal → Fault → Protection opens

Current in this branch circuit

The numbers beside the appliances show their normal operating currents. Dots trace the current paths, and colors distinguish the wires in this diagram. Actual trip times depend on current and the device.

Household loads are generally wired in parallel, so they share the supply voltage and can operate independently. In an overload, current follows the normal load paths, but the total is too high. In a live-to-neutral short circuit, a low-resistance path bypasses the loads. Either fault can overheat wiring.

Overcurrent can melt a fuse or trip a breaker, which opens its contacts. Once the fault is fixed, a blown fuse needs replacing and a breaker can be reset. A single-pole switch or overcurrent device on the live wire disconnects the load from that wire when it opens.

Most everyday US outlets are nominally 120 V. Ordinary household outlets in mainland China are nominally 220 V. Check the appliance’s input-voltage rating even when the plug fits.

Three-pin plugs and protective earth

On an appliance designed to be grounded, the third pin connects the metal case to protective earth. If the live wire touches the case, ground provides a low-resistance fault path that helps protective devices disconnect the circuit.

Electrical safety in everyday life
  • Keep electrical devices away from water. Keep hands dry near plugs and keep objects out of outlets.
  • Avoid overloading outlets and power strips. If a cord is damaged, an outlet feels hot, or a breaker keeps tripping, stop using the equipment and tell an adult so an electrician can check it.
  • Keep the grounding pin intact and never bypass a fuse. Ordinary overcurrent protection mainly protects wiring. A ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) detects leakage and cuts power quickly to reduce shock risk.

Worked example · A kettle’s energy and current

A resistive kettle runs at a steady 1500 W on 120 V for 6 min.

Find the energy used and the current.

Solution
  1. Convert minutes to hours and watts to kilowatts.
  2. Multiply power by time for energy. Divide power by voltage for current.

The kettle uses 0.15 kWh and draws 12.5 A.

Challenge · Two appliances on one circuit

A model branch circuit is rated 120 V, 15 A. Will a 1200 W heater and a 900 W toaster together exceed that current rating? Treat both as resistive loads.

The loads are in parallel. Add their powers and divide by the supply voltage. The resulting 17.5 A exceeds the model’s rating.

Challenge · Match the same heat output

A 4 Ω resistor carries 2 A for 15 s. How long must the same resistor carry 1 A to release the same amount of heat?

Find the heat, then use the new current to find the time. Half the current produces one-quarter as much heat per second, so you need four times as long.

Challenge · A bill with a fixed charge

A household uses 125 kWh. The energy rate is $0.16 per kWh and the fixed charge is $8.00. Find the total before tax.

Calculate the energy charge, then add the fixed charge. The total is $28.00.
