N₀ is the starting count, N is the expected count after time t, and T is the half-life.

Each round is one half-life. Flip a fair coin independently for each nucleus still undecayed. Heads marks it as decayed. The chance is one in two each round, even for a nucleus that survived all the previous rounds.

After one half-life, half of the undecayed nuclei remain on average. The dashed line shows that average. A single trial can land above or below it. Choosing another isotope rescales the time axis.

RememberExpect half as many nuclei as at the start of each round.

Review atoms and isotopes. Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. A radioactive isotope has unstable nuclei that decay on their own. The m in Tc-99m means its nucleus is in a higher-energy state that can last a while, called a metastable state.

Two worked examples · Fraction and elapsed time

Example 1 · Find the fraction left

Fluorine-18 has a half-life of about 110 minutes. What fraction of its nuclei should remain undecayed after 330 minutes?

330 min ÷ 110 min = 3
(1/2)³ = 1/8 = 12.5%

About 12.5% is expected to remain.

Example 2 · Find the time to reach one eighth

Given · Tc-99m has a half-life of about 6.0 h. One eighth remains.

Find · The elapsed time.

Solve

1/8 = (1/2)³ → t = 3 × 6.0 h = 18 h

Answer · About 18 hours.

Decay constant and activity

λ = ln 2 / T, N = N₀e⁻λᵗ

A larger decay constant λ means faster decay. Activity is the rate of decay. One becquerel (Bq) means one decay per second on average. If T is in seconds, λ is in inverse seconds. Use the expected count to find the model’s expected activity.

A = λN

Nuclear medicine and radiation therapy

Where does each scanner get its signal?

A tracer lets doctors follow a process inside the body. A short-lived isotope can give a signal during the scan, then its activity drops quickly. In treatment, radiation deposits energy in a chosen target.

Common mix-upOrdinary X-ray and CT exams use an X-ray tube. Fast electrons hit a target in the tube and produce X-rays. In nuclear imaging, the signal starts with nuclear decay in the tracer. Both kinds of imaging involve ionizing radiation.
Treatment and sterilization

A Gamma Knife aims many beams of gamma rays from cobalt-60 at a target in the brain. Proton therapy uses accelerated protons to concentrate the dose in a selected region. Because the thyroid takes up iodine, doctors can use iodine-131 to treat an overactive thyroid or certain thyroid cancers.

Gamma rays can also pass through the packaging of some medical devices and kill microorganisms to sterilize them.

Reading the past with carbon-14

Date the sample

These are simulated samples. Measure one, then work out how many half-lives have passed.

Carbon-14 relative to the starting amount
—

Cosmic rays produce neutrons in the atmosphere. Some of those neutrons react with nitrogen nuclei to form carbon-14. Plants take it up from carbon dioxide, and animals get it from food. When an organism dies, it stops taking in carbon-14 through life processes, and its carbon-14 keeps decaying.

A common upper limit is about 50,000 years. Typical samples come from once-living material such as charcoal, seeds, or bone collagen. Dating charcoal found in the same layer as a bronze object can help archaeologists estimate the object’s age.

Real dates · Ötzi and Sanxingdui

Ötzi the Iceman lived about 5,300 years ago. The South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology lists radiocarbon dates of about 3350 to 3120 BCE. Six early published dates from sacrificial Pit 4 at Sanxingdui, Sichuan, suggested a calibrated range of about 1260 to 924 BCE for that pit.

South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology · Antiquity

Why calibrate the clock?

The atmospheric carbon-14 fraction has changed over time. Tree rings can provide samples of known calendar age. Researchers use measurements from dated rings and other records to build a calibration curve, which converts a radiocarbon age to a range of possible calendar ages.

t = T log₂(N₀/N)

The inverse formula runs the simple model above backward. Real measurements need calibration.

Industry and home · Invisible signals

Send simulated smoke into an ionization alarm

The block on the left marks the Am-241 source. The dots represent charged particles.

Relative current (model)

Some smoke alarms use a tiny amount of americium-241. Its alpha particles ionize the air. Charged particles move in an electric field, making a small current. Smoke particles capture ions, reducing the current and triggering the alarm. Photoelectric alarms detect smoke using light.

Thickness, welds, and materials

On a production line, a thicker sheet of paper or steel generally lets less radiation reach a detector. Thickness gauges use this change. Industrial gamma radiography can reveal hidden gaps in welds while leaving the part intact. This is nondestructive testing.

The China Spallation Neutron Source in Dongguan, Guangdong, uses an accelerator to make neutron beams. Researchers study how neutrons scatter from materials to learn about their internal structure.

Space · Power far from the Sun

From decay to electric power

One type of nuclear battery is a radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG). Heat from plutonium-238 decay keeps one side warmer than the other, and a device uses that temperature difference to make electricity. Voyager 1 and 2 launched in 1977, carrying a little nuclear warmth beyond the planets.

As of November 2026, NASA’s mission status shows both Voyagers still returning science data from interstellar space, with some instruments switched off to save power. On Mars, Curiosity and Perseverance also use RTGs for electricity.

Chang’e 3’s Yutu rover used a radioisotope heat source to stay warm. The Chang’e 4 lander and its Yutu-2 rover also used radioisotope heat during the lunar night. The lander’s thermoelectric unit powered a nighttime temperature recorder.

Enrichment · Spent fuel and nuclear icebreakers 课外拓展 · Enrichment

Unstable nuclei in spent fuel still decay, giving off radiation and heat. Water in storage pools cools it and provides shielding. After enough cooling, it can go into dry casks. Deep geological disposal uses containers, other engineered barriers, and deep rock to isolate waste for the long term.

Finland’s ONKALO facility at Olkiluoto is designed for deep geological disposal of spent fuel. It received an operating licence in October 2026. The safety regulator must verify that the facility is ready before it can handle spent fuel. The Beishan underground research laboratory in Gansu, China, is designed to study geological disposal of high-level waste.

Some icebreakers use reactor energy for propulsion on long voyages through ice-covered waters.

Radiation safety · Reading a dose

Dose ladder

Bars use a logarithmic scale. Select an entry to see what activity, time period, or standard it refers to.

This ladder compares effective doses. Effective dose accounts for the type of radiation and the sensitivity of different organs. One sievert (Sv) is 1,000 millisieverts (mSv). Actual doses vary with location, flight route, and scan protocol.

Three ways to reduce external exposureSpend less time near the source, increase distance, and use shielding suited to the radiation. Compare alpha, beta, and gamma
How does shielding differ for alpha, beta, and gamma?

Alpha particles are helium nuclei, and paper can stop them. An alpha-emitting substance can be harmful inside the body. Beta particles are electrons or positrons. Suitable plastic or aluminum can stop many of them. Gamma rays are high-energy photons, and thick concrete or lead can reduce them.

Further practice · How much iodine-131 remains?

A model starts with 640 undecayed iodine-131 nuclei. Its half-life is about 8.0 days. How many should remain after 32 days?

Show steps
n = 32 d ÷ 8.0 d = 4
640 × (1/2)⁴ = 40

The expected count is 40 nuclei. A single trial can give a different count.
