Review atomic structure

Fission splits a heavy nucleus. Fusion joins light nuclei. When either reaction releases energy, the products have slightly less total rest mass. This difference, called the mass defect, corresponds to the energy released.

How do two positive charges get close?

Read from right to left as the nuclei approach. The gray dashed line shows the energy of this encounter. The dot marks how far apart the nuclei are. A colored dotted segment marks tunneling in a fusion example.

Like charges repel. At higher temperatures, more nuclei move fast enough to get closer. Sometimes quantum tunneling lets two nuclei get past the remaining barrier and close enough to fuse.

Earth-based D–T fusion experiments use temperatures on the order of 100 million °C. The chance of fusion varies with collision energy. The curve and distances are schematic, and the example outcomes illustrate the trend.

At these temperatures electrons are free of their atoms. Positive ions and free electrons form a plasma, often called the fourth state of matter.

The Sun’s core is about 15 million °C. Gravity compresses the core, making collisions frequent. Quantum tunneling gives a small fraction of nuclei a chance to reach the range of the nuclear force even when their energy is below the barrier. The odds per encounter are tiny, but the Sun has an enormous supply of particles.

We treat luminosity as constant and use the energy of the outgoing light. Neutrinos carry away a little additional energy. The solar wind carries matter. Both are left out of this estimate.

From hydrogen to helium

In the Sun’s core, four protons go through a series of nuclear reactions to make one helium-4 nucleus. This is the proton–proton chain. After many transfers, most of the released energy leaves the Sun as sunlight.

4 ¹₁H → ⁴₂He + 2e⁺ + 2νₑ
Q ≈ 26.7 MeV

The equation summarizes a common branch of the chain. The symbols e⁺ and νₑ stand for a positron and an electron neutrino. The total energy Q includes energy released when the two positrons annihilate with surrounding electrons. The neutrinos carry away a small share.

Fire releases energy through chemical reactions. The Sun gets its energy from changes inside atomic nuclei.

Many elements heavier than helium form inside stars or during dramatic changes late in a star’s life. The carbon and oxygen in our bodies were forged deep inside distant stars.

In a hydrogen bomb, a fission explosion creates the extreme temperature and pressure that let hydrogen isotopes fuse, releasing much more energy. On November 1, 1952, the United States conducted the first hydrogen-bomb test, Ivy Mike.

TNT equivalent is a way to express the energy of a nuclear explosion. The 1945 Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombs are estimated at about 15 and 21 kilotons respectively. Ivy Mike was about 10.4 megatons.

People and history · Two Bombs, One Satellite

Qian Sanqiang organized early thermonuclear research. Yu Min led key theoretical work. Deng Jiaxian and Zhou Guangzhao helped organize theoretical research. Peng Huanwu advised the theory teams, and Zhu Guangya helped organize early research. Wang Ganchang led experimental work, and Guo Yonghuai led research in mechanics.

Deng Jiaxian began classified research in 1958. His work became public in 1986, 28 years later.

Workers made equipment, technicians ran instruments and recorded data, and military personnel staffed test sites. Their families supported them through years of secret work. We rarely hear their names.

Yu Min began classified research in 1961. His work remained secret for nearly three decades. In 1999, he and 22 other scientists received the Two Bombs, One Satellite Meritorious Medal. He also received China’s 2014 State Preeminent Science and Technology Award, presented in 2015. He was awarded the Medal of the Republic posthumously in 2019.

History of the People’s Republic of China

Arms control and a shared future

In 1955, the Russell–Einstein Manifesto urged countries to settle disputes peacefully. Governments later negotiated treaties limiting nuclear tests and the spread of nuclear weapons.

“Remember your humanity, and forget the rest.” — Russell–Einstein Manifesto, 1955
  • The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty prohibits its parties from testing in the atmosphere, outer space or underwater. It also bans other nuclear explosions that spread radioactive debris beyond the testing state’s territorial limits.
  • The Non-Proliferation Treaty opened for signature in 1968 and entered into force in 1970. It aims to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons, support cooperation on peaceful nuclear energy and advance disarmament.
  • The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty opened for signature in 1996. It covers all nuclear test explosions and has yet to enter into force.
  • On the day of its first atomic test in 1964, China declared its policy of never being the first to use nuclear weapons, at any time or under any circumstances.

The “artificial sun” and the search for fusion power

Confine a plasma with a magnetic field

Gold dots are charged particles. The solid blue line is a field line, and pale outlines mark the walls. The tube shows a helix from the side. The ring is a simplified top view. In a real tokamak the field lines twist around the ring.

Why try it, and why is it hard?

Plasma at around 100 million degrees Celsius loses energy when it touches the walls. Charged particles spiral around magnetic field lines. A tokamak uses magnetic fields to hold hot plasma inside a ring-shaped chamber and keep it away from the walls.

Soviet researchers invented the tokamak in the 1950s and reported major experimental progress in 1968.

Seawater is a source of deuterium. Tritium needs a separate supply. Fusion dies down quickly when the plasma loses the conditions needed to keep it going. It carries no risk of a runaway fission chain reaction. Long-lived radioactive waste is expected to be limited. Neutrons can make parts of the machine radioactive, and tritium requires careful handling.

Four fusion research projects

  • EAST, Hefei, China. On January 20, 2025, it set a duration record for high-confinement plasma operation at 1,066 seconds.
  • HL-3, China. On March 28, 2025, both ion and electron temperatures topped 100 million °C, a first in China.
  • ITER, France. The ITER Agreement was signed in 2006. China, the European Union, India, Japan, South Korea, Russia and the United States are building the experiment together and sharing its results.
  • The U.S. National Ignition Facility (NIF) uses lasers to heat and compress fuel. On December 5, 2022, about 2.05 MJ of laser energy reached the target and produced about 3.15 MJ of fusion energy. The laser system drew far more energy from the grid than it delivered to the target. The experiment generated no electricity for the grid.

Fusion power stations are still a research goal. Nobody knows exactly when a working fusion power station will be ready.

Binding energy per nucleon

Binding energy is the energy needed to take a nucleus apart. Higher on the graph means more tightly bound per nucleon. Joining light nuclei or splitting heavy ones can move the products closer to the peak and release energy. Iron-56 and nickel-62 are both near the top. The graph joins selected nuclei.

Calculating D–T energy from atomic masses

We use neutral-atom masses. The two starting hydrogen atoms have one electron each, and the helium atom has two, so the electron rest masses cancel. The small difference in electron binding energies is negligible at this precision.

The Lawson criterion helps predict when a plasma can meet its energy balance goal. A common measure is the fusion triple product: temperature times particle density times energy confinement time. Think hot enough, dense enough, and able to hold its energy long enough.

Show solution

One product of a D–T reaction is a helium-4 nucleus. What mass number and charge number must the other product have?

Show solution

Its mass number is (2 + 3) − 4 = 1. Its charge number is (1 + 1) − 2 = 0. The other product is a neutron.
