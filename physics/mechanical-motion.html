Measuring length

Align, look, and record

The range tells you what lengths the ruler can measure. The smallest division is the interval between neighboring marks. Line up the ruler along the object and look straight down. On this ruler, estimate one digit beyond the smallest division and write the unit.

RememberIf zero is damaged, start at a clear mark. Subtract the start reading from the end reading.
Looking at an angle causes parallax. In this model the start is already lined up. Change the viewing angle and see how the end reading shifts.
Length units, conversions, and two measuring tricks

The SI base unit of length is the meter (m). Other common units are the kilometer, decimeter, centimeter, millimeter, micrometer, and nanometer.

1 km = 1000 m
1 m = 10 dm = 100 cm = 1000 mm
1 mm = 1000 μm, 1 μm = 1000 nm

In U.S. customary units, 1 inch equals exactly 2.54 cm.

Stacking method. Line up sheets of the same kind of paper, gently flatten the stack, and exclude the cover. If 100 sheets measure 10.0 mm, what is the average thickness of one sheet?

10.0 mm ÷ 100 = 0.100 mm

Thread method. Lay a thread that does not stretch along the curve. Mark the two ends on the thread, straighten it gently, and measure between the marks.

Measuring time and understanding error

Press Start. When you see “Stop now,” press Stop as quickly as you can. Record up to five trials and compare your readings.

The reference is when the cue appears, around 3 s. If you stop early, the reference is the scheduled 3.00 s.

Why do readings differ?

The second (s) is the SI base unit of time. This stopwatch displays hundredths of a second, but reaction time affects the reading. Measurement error is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Copying a number incorrectly or pressing the wrong button is a mistake to correct.

Averaging repeated readings can reduce the effect of random error. If you always press Stop after the cue, reaction delay can still make the average too high.
Time units and a worked average
1 h = 60 min = 3600 s
1 min = 60 s, 1 s = 1000 ms

Three measurements of the same time interval give 2.96 s, 3.03 s, and 3.01 s. Add them and divide by the number of trials.

Mean = (2.96 s + 3.03 s + 3.01 s) ÷ 3 = 3.00 s

Mechanical motion and reference frames

0.0 s

Moving relative to what?

Mechanical motion is a change in position over time relative to a reference object (参照物, cānzhàowù). On a moving train, a seated passenger is at rest relative to the train and moving relative to the platform.

RememberPick the reference object first. Then look for a change in relative position. Motion and rest depend on the reference object.
An old story about a boat and a mark

In the “Examining the Present” chapter of the Lüshi Chunqiu, a traveler drops a sword into the river and marks the boat. The mark stays in the same place on the boat as it sails on. Is it still where the sword fell in? Relative to the riverbank, the mark has moved with the boat.

Who goes farther in the same time?

In uniform straight-line motion, speed and direction stay constant, and equal time intervals cover equal distances. Divide the distance traveled by the elapsed time for average speed. English textbooks use speed for how fast something moves. Velocity also includes direction.

Read the vertical axis first. A horizontal distance–time graph means the object is stopped. A horizontal speed–time graph means the speed stays the same, with rest shown at zero.
RememberOn the same distance–time axes, a steeper slope means faster motion. Multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h. Divide by 3.6 to go back.
Worked example · Average cycling speed

Given. A cyclist travels 60 m in 12 s.

Find. The average speed for this trip in m/s and km/h.

Solve. Divide distance by time, then convert the units.

Average speed = 60 m ÷ 12 s = 5 m/s
5 m/s = (5/1000 km) ÷ (1/3600 h) = 18 km/h

Answer. The average speed is 5 m/s, or 18 km/h. U.S. road signs commonly use mph. One mile per hour is about 1.61 km/h.

Equal distances. Equal times?

Release the cart from rest at A. Track the same point on the cart, its front edge. Record when it crosses midpoint B and finish C, timing both from release. Subtract the midpoint time from the finish time to get the lower-half time.

RememberUse the distance and time for the same segment. For the whole run, divide total distance by total time.
Hands-on procedure and sample data
  1. Raise one end of the ramp slightly with a block. Mark start A, midpoint B, and finish C along the ramp. Measure the segments with a ruler. Place a stop at the finish to catch the cart.
  2. Line up the front of the cart with A and release it without a push as you start timing. Use a stopwatch with lap timing to record when the front reaches B and C. For separate runs from A, place the stop at C for the whole run, then move it to B to time the upper half. Keep the slope and release method the same.
  3. A shallow ramp gives you more time, so a timing delay is a smaller fraction of the measured interval. Repeat under the same conditions and average the times for each segment.

Given. The ramp is 1.00 m long. The front reaches B at 2.00 s and C at 2.80 s. The lower half is 0.50 m.

Find the average speed over the lower half.

Subtract the two readings to find the time for the lower half. Then divide the lower-half distance by that time.

t(B→C) = 2.80 s − 2.00 s = 0.80 s
v̄(B→C) = 0.50 m ÷ 0.80 s ≈ 0.63 m/s

The cart’s average speed over the lower half is about 0.63 m/s.

Practice · Can you average the two speeds?

A cart goes 0.60 m in 4.0 s, then another 0.60 m in 2.0 s. Find its average speed for the whole trip.

Show steps
v̄ = (0.60 m + 0.60 m) ÷ (4.0 s + 2.0 s) = 0.20 m/s

The two segment speeds are 0.15 m/s and 0.30 m/s. The segments take different amounts of time. Giving both speeds equal weight would give 0.225 m/s, which differs from the overall average speed.
