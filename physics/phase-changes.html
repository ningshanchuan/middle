From ice to water vapor

At 1 atm, pure ice melts at about 0 °C (32 °F), and pure water boils at about 100 °C (212 °F). On each plateau, the sample still absorbs heat. The share of the mass in each phase changes while the temperature stays steady. At the same pressure, pure water freezes at about 0 °C and releases heat as it freezes.

Crystalline and amorphous solids

Ice is crystalline. Its particles form a repeating pattern. At a fixed pressure, a pure crystal has a definite melting point. Ordinary glass is amorphous. Its particles lack a pattern that repeats over long distances, and it gradually softens over a range of temperatures.

Left: a repeating arrangement. Right: an arrangement with no long-range repetition. Both drawings are schematic.

Worked example: read a melting plateau

Given: a 20 g sample is heated at 20 W. Ice starts melting at 42 s and finishes at 376 s. Find the melting time.

Subtract the time at the left end of the plateau from the time at the right end.

Δt = 376 s − 42 s = 334 s

Melting lasts 334 s. The sample stays at about 0 °C throughout that interval.

How do you use a thermometer?

A liquid-in-glass lab thermometer measures temperature. As the liquid inside warms, it expands and the column rises.

Measure a liquid temperature

  1. Estimate the temperature and choose a suitable range. Check the unit and the value of one small division. Each small interval here is 2 °C.
  2. Fully immerse the bulb in the liquid. Keep the bulb clear of the container’s bottom and sides.
  3. Wait for the reading to stabilize. Keep the bulb immersed and read with your eyes level with the top of the liquid column.

Looking from above or below can make the column appear to line up with a different mark. This reading error is called parallax.

Worked example: read a temperature below zero

Select brine and wait for the reading to settle. Given: each small division is 2 °C, and the column ends three divisions below 0 °C. Find the brine temperature.

Count down three divisions from 0 °C.

0 °C − 3 × 2 °C = −6 °C

The brine is at −6 °C. Use “Check the reading” to verify.

Evaporation or boiling?

Evaporation happens at a liquid’s surface and can occur below its boiling point. During boiling, vaporization occurs at the surface and throughout the liquid, where vapor bubbles form. Both processes absorb heat.

Evaporate water from a dish

Both trials start with the same amount of water. Change one condition at a time and compare how much water evaporates over the same interval.

Use this model to compare trends. In each trial, the water and air stay at the selected temperature, and the relative humidity stays fixed.

Warmer water, a larger exposed surface, and faster airflow usually speed evaporation. With other conditions fixed, more humid air slows net evaporation. Sweat absorbs heat as it evaporates, which helps cool your skin.

Practice & quiz

Apply what you learned

A cup of water is boiling. You turn up the heater. What happens to the temperature and the water left in the cup?

At the same pressure, while liquid remains, its temperature stays near the boiling point. If more heat reaches the water each second, more water vaporizes per second and the amount left drops faster.

How would you use two dishes to test the effect of airflow fairly?

Use identical dishes and equal starting amounts of water. Keep exposed area, temperature, and humidity the same. Change only airflow over the surface. After equal times, compare the mass each dish lost.

Water vapor forms drops on a window. The drops later turn to ice. What are the two changes, and which way does heat flow in each?

First condensation, then freezing. The first step is gas to liquid, and the second is liquid to solid. The water releases heat in both steps. Vapor becoming ice directly would be deposition.
