Forces, Friction & Gravity

Gravity pulls on a parked car. So why doesn’t it move? A force is a push or a pull. When the net force is zero, the object as a whole keeps the same velocity. A nonzero net force changes its speed or direction. Run a tug-of-war, then put a block on ramps with different surfaces and watch the force arrows change.

Elastic force and the spring scale

If an object returns to its original shape after the force is removed, the change is an elastic deformation. A stretched spring pulls back. A compressed spring pushes outward. This restoring force is an elastic force. A spring scale uses the extension of a spring to measure force.

Range 0–5 N, smallest division 0.2 N. Zero the unloaded scale, then record 0, 1, 2 and 3 N.

How to use the scale and interpret the data
  1. Choose a scale whose range covers the expected force. Find the value of the smallest division, the difference between adjacent marks.
  2. Gently pull and release the hook to check that it moves freely. Zero the unloaded scale in the orientation you will use.
  3. Pull gently along the scale’s axis and stay within its range. Once the pointer settles, look straight at the scale to avoid parallax and record the reading in newtons. For an object hanging vertically at rest, the reading equals its weight if air buoyancy is negligible.

Subtract the unloaded length L₀ from each length L to find the extension ΔL. A graph close to a straight line through the origin shows proportionality over the tested range. When the hook is at rest, the spring pulls it back with a force equal and opposite to the applied pull. Ignore the weight of the hook in this model.

This model follows F = kΔL from 0 to 5 N, where k is the spring constant. A real spring follows this rule only over a limited range. It can still recover beyond the proportional limit. Loading it past its elastic limit leaves a permanent change in shape.

Worked example and practice · Find the pull from the extension
Given
A spring is 6.0 cm long unloaded and 8.0 cm long under a 1.0 N pull. It stays in its proportional range.
Find
The pull when the spring is 12.0 cm long.
Solve
8.0 cm − 6.0 cm = 2.0 cm
12.0 cm − 6.0 cm = 6.0 cm
F = 1.0 N × (6.0 cm ÷ 2.0 cm) = 3.0 N
Answer
The pull is 3.0 N.

Practice: How long is the spring under a 4.0 N pull?

Show steps

The extension is 2.0 cm per 1.0 N, so 4.0 N extends the spring by 8.0 cm. Add the unloaded length of 6.0 cm to get 14.0 cm.

Weight, vertical direction and center of gravity

Weight is the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons. Mass is measured in kilograms. Weight points vertically down, parallel to a plumb line at rest. Draw the weight arrow from the center of gravity.

In the formula, G is weight (often written W in English) and m is mass. Near Earth’s surface, g is about 9.8 N/kg. Use the value given in the problem. Use 10 N/kg for estimates or when the problem specifies it.

Suspension lab · Find a sheet’s center of gravity

A uniform rectangular sheet has its center of gravity where its diagonals cross. For an irregular sheet, hang it from one hole and let it settle. Trace the plumb line from that hole onto the sheet. Choose a second hole that gives a line crossing the first. The intersection locates the center of gravity.

The center of gravity depends on how mass is distributed. A uniform ring’s center of gravity is at its center, in the empty space.

Stability lab · When does it tip?

Tilt the block slowly about one base edge. If the vertical line through its center of gravity falls inside the pivot, gravity turns the block back when released. If the line passes beyond the pivot, gravity tips it farther. At the threshold, the line goes through the pivot. A lower center of gravity or a wider base increases that angle, all else equal.

Worked example and practice · Find weight from mass
Given
A backpack has a mass of 2.5 kg. Use g = 9.8 N/kg.
Find
The magnitude and direction of the backpack’s weight.
Solve
G = mg = 2.5 kg × 9.8 N/kg = 24.5 N
Answer
Its weight is 24.5 N, vertically downward.

Practice: If the problem specifies g = 10 N/kg, what weight do you get? How much larger is this estimate?

Show steps

2.5 kg × 10 N/kg = 25 N. The estimate is 0.5 N higher than 24.5 N.

Two-force equilibrium checklist

Two forces balance each other if they act on the same object, are equal in size, point in opposite directions and share a line of action. An action–reaction pair comes from one interaction, with one force on each object.

Check every condition the diagram meets.

Worked example and practice · Balanced forces or an action–reaction pair?
Given
A book rests on a level table. Only its weight and the table’s support force act on it. Its weight is 6 N.
Find
Find the magnitude and direction of the support force and classify the pair.
Solve
Both forces act on the book. The book stays at rest, so the forces share a line of action, point oppositely and have equal magnitudes.
Fₙ = G = 6 N
Answer
The support force is 6 N vertically upward. It balances the book’s weight.

Practice: What is the partner force to the table’s upward support on the book? What is the partner force to the book’s weight?

Show steps

Swap the objects applying and receiving each force. The book pushes down on the table with 6 N. The book also pulls on Earth with 6 N. Each pair acts on two different objects.

Drop race: with and without air

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S8P3b (use Newton’s laws to explain the effects of balanced and unbalanced forces on motion) and S8P5a (fields such as gravity act across a gap). Use the tug to show how two forces can add to zero. On the ramp, compare the angle at which the block starts to slide on ice, wood and rubber (critical angle, tan θ = μs).

The tug model treats both teams and the rope as one moving system. The horizontal forces come from the ground on the two teams. With right positive and a default total mass of 300 kg, a = (F_right − F_left)/m. On the ramp, g = 9.8 N/kg. At rest f_static = mg sin θ. Sliding downhill, f_kinetic = μk mg cos θ and a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ). In the drop model, downward is positive and a = g − kv²/m, where k is the drag constant in kg/m.
