Keep both bulbs identical and compare the voltage across one bulb in each layout. This model uses an ideal battery and ideal meters. It treats bulbs as fixed resistances and uses electrical power to represent brightness. The next lab models a filament that heats up.

Series · voltages add

I = I₁ = I₂ U = U₁ + U₂ R = R₁ + R₂

Parallel · currents add

U = U₁ = U₂ I = I₁ + I₂ 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂

Memory hint. Add voltages around the series loop and add currents at the parallel junction. Two finite, positive resistances in parallel give an equivalent resistance smaller than either one.

Advanced · the power behind the glow

Compare equal bulbs by electrical power, the energy transferred per second. Connect two 12 Ω bulbs to the same 6 V source. How many times as much power does each bulb get in parallel as in series?

See the reasoning

In the fixed-resistance model, each bulb goes from 3 V in series to 6 V in parallel. With the same resistance and twice the voltage, the power is four times as large.

Ohm’s law · line or curve?

Voltage U is horizontal and current I is vertical. U.S. books often use V as the symbol for voltage. Here we use U, and the unit is still the volt (V).

Fixed resistorFilament bulb (model)

Ohm’s law is written I = U/R. At constant resistance, current is proportional to voltage. At a fixed voltage, current is inversely proportional to resistance. A bulb’s filament usually gets hotter as the voltage rises, so its resistance increases. The dashed curve is an illustrative model.

Advanced · check the axes before finding slope

With I on the vertical axis and U on the horizontal axis, the resistor line has slope 1/R. Swap the axes and its slope is R. For a bulb, U/I uses the voltage and current at the chosen operating point.

Common mix-up. Turning up the voltage makes the current through a fixed resistor rise while U/I stays constant. Compare the two traces to see how the bulb behaves differently.

What changes a wire’s resistance?

Wire resistance—
Same wire at 20 °C—
R = ρL/A

Resistivity ρ depends on material and temperature. L is length and A is cross-sectional area. Small resistances here are in milliohms (mΩ). One mΩ is 0.001 Ω.

Change one variable at a time. For the same metal and temperature, a longer wire of the same thickness has more resistance. For wires of the same length, a larger cross-sectional area gives less resistance. In this model’s temperature range, copper and tungsten wires both gain resistance as they warm.

Advanced · estimate resistance after warming

This linear expression is an approximation. Alpha is the temperature coefficient of resistance. The model neglects thermal expansion. Keep length and area fixed and compare the percentage increases for the two metals from 20 °C to 100 °C.

R(T) ≈ R₂₀ [1 + α (T − 20 °C)]

The voltmeter–ammeter method

A sliding rheostat (滑动变阻器, huádòng biànzǔqì) is an adjustable resistor wired in series here. Its slider changes how much resistance wire is in the circuit, letting you vary the current and the voltage across the test resistor.

Wire the circuit with the switch open. Put the ammeter in series with the test resistor and connect the voltmeter across it. Connect each meter’s positive terminal on the battery’s positive side and choose a suitable range. Set the rheostat to its maximum connected resistance before closing the switch.

Record three different readings. Compute R = U/I for each and average the three estimates. This model uses ideal meters. Small differences between the calculated resistances come from rounding the displayed readings.
