Work, Power & Efficiency

Your hand keeps pulling up on a backpack while you stand still. Why is the work done by that force zero? Start with force and displacement, then use the same ideas on stairs, levers, and pulleys.

When does a force do work?

Selected force FDisplacement s

Pick one force first

Work describes what a particular force does over a displacement. When a constant force points along the displacement, multiply their magnitudes. If the object stays still, or the force is perpendicular to its displacement, that force does zero work.

W = Fs, 1 J = 1 N·m

Common mix-up. During a steady level carry, the hand supports the bag upward while the bag moves horizontally. You can feel tired even though that upward force does zero work on the bag.

Worked example · A push along the floor

A constant 30 N horizontal push moves a box 4 m in the direction of the push. Find the work done by the push.

Multiply the force by the displacement in the same direction.

W = Fs = (30 N)(4 m) = 120 J

The push does 120 J of work. Both quantities refer to the direction of the push.

For a constant force at angle θ to the displacement, use the component along the displacement. Try “Pull at an angle” and see what happens as the angle grows. Then try “Brake” to see negative work.

W = (F cos θ)s

The graph smooths out individual steps. A steeper line shows a greater average vertical speed. Height stays constant at the landing. Set up a race where the earlier finisher has the lower average power.

Same work, greater power in less time

Power tells you how quickly work is done. On the stairs, multiply weight by vertical rise to get the work against gravity. Divide by the climb time for average power. With equal weights, the earlier finisher has greater average power.

P̄ = W/t, 1 W = 1 J/s

For steady motion in the direction of a constant force, P = Fs/t = Fv. For the climb, multiply weight G by average vertical speed h/t to get the average power against gravity. This measures the gain in gravitational potential energy per second.

P = Fv, P̄ = G(h/t)

W is the symbol for work. The unit W (watt) measures power.

Worked example · Compare two finishers

A weighs 450 N and climbs 4 m in 5 s. B weighs 600 N and climbs the same 4 m in 6 s. Compare their average power against gravity.

For each climber, multiply weight by rise, then divide by the climb time.

W(A) = (450 N)(4 m) = 1800 J
P̄(A) = 1800 J / (5 s) = 360 W
W(B) = (600 N)(4 m) = 2400 J
P̄(B) = 2400 J / (6 s) = 400 W

A finishes first with 360 W. B has the greater average power at 400 W.

Move the fulcrum to balance a lever

Drag either weight or the triangular fulcrum. The sliders below give you precise control of their positions.

Measure perpendicular to the force

The fulcrum is the pivot. A lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the fulcrum to the line along which a force acts. Suppose only two forces can turn the lever. If one tends to turn it clockwise and the other counterclockwise, the lever balances when the two force–arm products are equal.

F₁L₁ = F₂L₂

The model ignores the beam’s weight. It measures the arms while the beam is level. The arrow shows which end would begin to drop. Force times lever arm is torque, in N·m.

Worked example · Place the right weight

On a level lever, a 30 N weight sits 0.40 m left of the fulcrum. Ignore the beam’s weight. How far to the right should a 40 N weight sit to balance it?

Set the two turning effects equal. Divide the left torque by the right weight to find the right arm.

F₁L₁ = (30 N)(0.40 m) = 12 N·m
L₂ = 12 N·m / 40 N = 0.30 m

Place it 0.30 m to the right. Both torques are 12 N·m.

Lever classes by position

The Chinese textbook also groups levers by force. A longer effort arm means less effort than the load force. A shorter effort arm means more effort. Equal arms give equal forces.

Lifting the same load: how do force, distance, and work change?

Pulleys: trace the supporting rope

Supporting ropePull direction

Less force means pulling more rope

Trace the rope and count the n segments that pull upward directly on the load or moving block. The supporting strands are vertical and the rope does not stretch. For a steady lift, ignore rope weight, pulley weight, and friction. Then all strands have the same tension, and the pull is G/n. As the load rises by h, the combined change in supporting rope length is nh, so the free end has to move nh.

F = G/n, s = nh

Common mix-up. Count supporting rope segments. A single fixed pulley lets you pull down while the load rises, with a pull equal to the load’s weight in the ideal case.

Worked example · Follow the free end

An ideal pulley system with four vertical supporting strands lifts a 200 N load steadily through 2 m. Find the pull, rope travel, and work done by the pull.

Use the strand count to find the pull and rope travel, then multiply to find the work.

F = 200 N / 4 = 50 N, s = 4 × 2 m = 8 m
W = Fs = (50 N)(8 m) = 400 J

Pull with 50 N through 8 m. The work is 400 J.

Quick check · Pulleys

Inclined plane: same rise, different path

Compare force and distance

Push a load up a frictionless ramp at constant speed. If the push is along the ramp, its work Fs equals the work Gh of lifting the load straight up. For the same height, a longer ramp takes a smaller push over a greater distance. The work stays the same.

Fs = Gh → F = Gh/s
Worked example · Double the ramp length

You raise a 180 N load by 2 m at steady speed on frictionless ramps 4 m and 8 m long. Find the push along each ramp.

Find the work against gravity. Divide it by the length of each ramp.

Gh = (180 N)(2 m) = 360 J
F₄ = 360 J / 4 m = 90 N, F₈ = 360 J / 8 m = 45 N

Doubling the length halves the force. Both pushes do 360 J of work.

Mechanical efficiency: how much work is useful?

Input work breakdown

Keep the block’s weight and friction fixed, with at least one above zero. First vary the lift height while keeping it above zero. Then hold the height fixed and increase the load. Which change affects efficiency?

Identify the useful work first

If the job is to raise the load, useful work is Gh. The input work on the rope is Fs. The additional work raises the moving block and overcomes friction. Mechanical efficiency is the fraction of input work that serves the intended task.

η = useful work / input work

Input work is useful work plus additional work. The moving block gains gravitational potential energy, and friction increases thermal energy. The ideal model with zero additional work has 100% efficiency.

Worked example · Use a measured pull

A pulley system with four vertical supporting strands steadily lifts a 200 N load by 2 m. The pull stays at 65 N. Find the useful work, input work, additional work, and efficiency.

Multiply load weight by lift height for useful work. Multiply pull by rope travel for input work.

Useful work = (200 N)(2 m) = 400 J
s = 4 × 2 m = 8 m
Input work = (65 N)(8 m) = 520 J
Additional work = 520 J − 400 J = 120 J
η = (400 J / 520 J) × 100% ≈ 76.9%

Useful work is 400 J, input work is 520 J, and additional work is 120 J. Efficiency is about 76.9%.

Calculation practice and quiz

Set up each calculation before opening the steps

1. A motor lifts a crate

A motor lifts a 240 N crate at constant speed through 2.5 m in 5 s. Its input power is 160 W. Find the average useful power against gravity and the lifting efficiency.

Useful work = (240 N)(2.5 m) = 600 J
Average useful power = 600 J / 5 s = 120 W
Input work = (160 W)(5 s) = 800 J
η = (600 J / 800 J) × 100% = 75%
2. Deduce the rope arrangement

A pulley system steadily lifts a 300 N load by 1.5 m. The free end moves 6 m under a constant 90 N pull. Find the supporting strand count and efficiency. Assume vertical supporting strands and a rope that does not stretch.

n = 6 m / 1.5 m = 4
Useful work = (300 N)(1.5 m) = 450 J
Input work = (90 N)(6 m) = 540 J
η = (450 J / 540 J) × 100% ≈ 83.3%
3. Work backward from ramp efficiency

A steady push along a 5 m ramp raises a 250 N load by 1.2 m at constant speed. The mechanical efficiency is 75%. Find the constant push along the ramp.

Useful work = (250 N)(1.2 m) = 300 J
Input work = 300 J / 0.75 = 400 J
F = 400 J / 5 m = 80 N
