Temperature, internal energy, and heat

Temperature
A measure of how hot or cold something is. At a higher temperature, particles generally move more vigorously. Even particles in a solid keep vibrating.
Internal energy (nèinéng)
Add the energy from particles’ motion to the potential energy from their interactions to get the object’s internal energy. Internal energy depends on factors such as temperature, mass, and state. Ice has internal energy, too.
Heat (rèliàng)
Energy transferred because of a temperature difference, measured in joules (J). The net transfer by heat is from higher to lower temperature.

Molecules are always moving randomly. During diffusion (kuòsàn, 扩散), particles move in all directions, with an overall spread from higher to lower concentration. They keep moving after the gases are mixed.

Molecules also exert forces on one another. At very close range they repel, and at somewhat greater distances attraction can hold them together.

Two cups of water at the same temperature can have different internal energies. The cup with more water has more internal energy. With equal temperatures, there is no net heat transfer between them.

What does specific heat tell us?

Specific heat capacity (bǐrèróng, 比热容), c, is the energy needed to warm one kilogram of a material by 1 °C. We use about 4,200 J/(kg·°C) for water and about 800 J/(kg·°C) for dry sand. For equal masses given equal energy, the sample with the larger c warms less.

Q = cmΔt

For warming, Q is heat absorbed (J), m is mass (kg), and Δt is final minus initial temperature (°C). To find how much heat a cooling object releases, use the temperature drop (initial minus final). English-language texts often write ΔT for the temperature change. For steady heating, P is heater power (W) and τ is elapsed time (s).

Why can coasts have milder temperatures?

Water has a higher specific heat than many land materials. The ocean warms slowly as it absorbs energy in warmer seasons and cools slowly as it releases energy in cooler seasons. Winds can carry air from over the ocean onto land, reducing temperature swings near the coast. Currents and wind direction also matter.

Worked example · Heating water

How much heat is absorbed when 0.50 kg of water warms from 20 °C to 30 °C?

  1. Find the temperature rise.Δt = 30 °C − 20 °C = 10 °C
  2. Multiply specific heat, mass, and temperature change.Q = (4,200 J/(kg·°C))(0.50 kg)(10 °C)Q = 21,000 J = 21 kJ

Mixing water and conserving energy

Pour hot and cool water into an ideal insulated cup. Which sample changes temperature more?

Heat lost by hot water equals heat gained by cool water

Energy can transfer or change form, and the total energy of an isolated system stays constant. Our system is both samples of water. We ignore energy absorbed by the cup, heat exchange with the room, and evaporation, and treat water’s specific heat as constant. The energy the hot water loses is gained by the cool water. Both end at the same temperature, a state called thermal equilibrium.

m_h c(T_h − T_f) = m_c c(T_f − T_c)

The subscripts h, c, and f mean hot water, cool water, and the final state. Both sides use positive amounts of energy. The left is the heat lost by the hot water, Qh, and the right is the heat gained by the cool water, Qc. The same specific heat c appears on both sides and cancels when solving for the final temperature.

The final temperature is halfway between the two starting temperatures when the masses are equal. With unequal masses, it is closer to the starting temperature of the larger sample.

Worked example · Find the mixing temperature

Mix 0.30 kg of water at 70 °C with 0.20 kg at 20 °C in an ideal insulated cup. Find the final temperature and the heat transferred.

  1. Set heat lost equal to heat gained and cancel the common specific heat. (0.30 kg)(70 °C − T_f) = (0.20 kg)(T_f − 20 °C)
  2. Rearrange and divide by the total mass. T_f = [(0.30 kg)(70 °C) + (0.20 kg)(20 °C)]/(0.50 kg) = 50 °C
  3. Use the cool water's 30 °C rise to calculate the heat it gains. Q = (0.20 kg)(4,200 J/(kg·°C))(30 °C) Q = 25,200 J = 25.2 kJ

How much energy does each kilogram release?

A fuel’s heating value (rèzhí, 热值), q, is the energy released per unit mass in complete combustion, measured in J/kg. Use the heating value given in a calculation problem.

Q = mq

Energy budget · Engine efficiency

Useful work
Remaining energy

Efficiency η is useful work W divided by the energy Q released by complete combustion. The remaining energy still counts in the total. Much of it leaves in hot exhaust or through the cooling system. Friction also converts some mechanical energy into internal energy.

Worked example · From gasoline to useful work

An engine completely burns 0.020 kg of gasoline and delivers 215 kJ of useful work. Use q = 43 MJ/kg to find its efficiency and the remaining energy.

  1. Find the energy released by complete combustion.Q = (0.020 kg)(43 MJ/kg) = 0.86 MJ = 860 kJ
  2. Divide useful work by fuel energy.η = (215 kJ)/(860 kJ) = 0.25 = 25%
  3. Account for the rest of the energy.860 kJ − 215 kJ = 645 kJ

How much hot water makes a 35 °C mixture?

How much water at 80 °C must be mixed with 0.60 kg of water at 20 °C to reach 35 °C in an ideal insulated cup?

  1. Hot water cools by 45 °C and cool water warms by 15 °C.
  2. Set the magnitudes equal and cancel the specific heat. mₕ(45 °C) = (0.60 kg)(15 °C) mₕ = (0.60 kg)(15 °C)/(45 °C) = 0.20 kg
How much can a fuel warm water?

Burn 0.020 kg of ethanol (q = 27 MJ/kg). Of the energy released, 35% is absorbed by 1.50 kg of water initially at 20 °C. What is its final temperature?

  1. Find the share that goes into the water. Q = 0.35(0.020 kg)(27 MJ/kg) = 189 kJ
  2. Divide by mc to find the rise, then add the starting temperature. Δt = 189,000 J/[(1.50 kg)(4,200 J/(kg·°C))] = 30 °C T_f = 20 °C + 30 °C = 50 °C
How could you distinguish convection from diffusion?

Plan a comparison using two clear cups of still room-temperature water. How could a little dyed cool water help distinguish convection from diffusion? What would you use as a control?

Take equal portions of the same dyed water. Leave one at room temperature and cool the other to about 10 °C. Gently add them to the surfaces of two cups of room-temperature water using a dropper. Leave both unstirred. If the cooler dye forms a sinking plume sooner than the room-temperature dye, that is evidence of convection: denser cool water carries the dye downward. Diffusion also spreads dye in both cups over time.
