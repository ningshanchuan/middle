🥚

Hard-boiled egg

Shell = crust, white = mantle, yolk = core. The shell is thin and cracked, just like the lithosphere broken into plates.

🍎

Thinner than apple skin

If Earth shrank to the size of an apple, the crust would be thinner than the apple’s skin! Try the “True scale” button.

🍯

A solid that flows

The mantle is solid, but like stiff honey or putty it flows very slowly, only a few centimeters a year.

🧲

Earth’s dynamo

Churning liquid iron in the outer core acts like a giant generator, making the magnetic field that turns a compass north.

How do we know?

The deepest hole ever drilled (the Kola Superdeep Borehole) is only about 12 km, less than 0.2% of the way to the center. Scientists use these clues instead:

〰️

Seismic waves

Earthquake waves change speed and direction in different materials, like an ultrasound scan of the planet.

⚖️

Earth’s density

Earth’s average density is about 5.5 g/cm³ but crust is only about 2.7, so something much heavier must be deep inside.

☄️

Meteorites

Many meteorites are made of iron and nickel, left over from the same material that built Earth.

🌋

Rocks from volcanoes

Some eruptions carry chunks of mantle rock (such as green peridotite) up to the surface.

🧪

Lab experiments

Scientists squeeze rocks and metals in high-pressure machines to copy conditions deep inside Earth.

⚠️ Common mix-up:There is no ocean of lava under the crust! The mantle is almost entirely solid rock. Magma forms only in certain places, such as at plate boundaries and hot spots.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E5a: ask questions to compare and contrast the crust, mantle, inner and outer core, including temperature, density, thickness and composition. Suggested flow: ① use the Cutaway slider and the “Journey to the core” story to meet each layer; ② have students drag the drill and record temperature, pressure and density at several depths, then graph them and describe the pattern; ③ in the seismic simulation, untick “Outer core is liquid” and compare the shadow zones so students can argue from evidence why scientists conclude the outer core is liquid.

Note: depths and temperatures are typical textbook approximations and vary slightly between sources (e.g. the asthenosphere is often given as about 100–350 km, sometimes down to 660 km). The default view exaggerates the thin crust and lithosphere; the True scale button shows real proportions.
