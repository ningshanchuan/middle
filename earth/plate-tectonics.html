Plate Tectonics: The Moving Puzzle

Why do the coastlines of South America and Africa look like two puzzle pieces that fit together? Earth’s rigid outer shell is cracked into about a dozen giant plates that move a few centimeters a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Drag the time slider to watch plates pull apart, collide and slide past each other, building ridges, trenches, volcanoes and mountains!

🎯 Challenge: find the epicenter

Three seismograph stations recorded the same earthquake. P waves (about 6 km/s) arrive first, S waves (about 3.5 km/s) later. Use the gap to get each station’s distance: d ≈ 8.4 × (S–P gap in s) km, then tap the map where the quake happened.

Three kinds of plate boundaries

How two plates move relative to each other decides which landforms and events appear at their boundary.

← →Divergent

Plates move apart. Magma rises and new crust forms.

  • Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, seafloor spreading
  • Volcanoes (mostly gentle), shallow earthquakes
  • Examples: Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland, East African Rift

→ ←Convergent

Plates move together. A dense ocean plate sinks (subducts); two continents crumple up.

  • Trenches, volcanic mountains, island arcs, folded mountains
  • Strong earthquakes, including very deep ones
  • Examples: Andes, Japan, Himalayas

⇅Transform

Plates slide past each other. Crust is neither made nor destroyed.

  • Faults, offset roads and streams
  • Frequent shallow earthquakes, almost no volcanoes
  • Example: San Andreas Fault, California
⚠️ Common mix-up:Plates do not float on liquid magma. The asthenosphere is solid rock that flows very slowly; mantle convection, ridge push and the pull of sinking slabs together move the plates.
💡 Georgia connectionAbout 300 million years ago, ancient Africa collided with North America while Pangaea was forming. The collision pushed up the Appalachian Mountains, including north Georgia’s Blue Ridge. Erosion has worn them low and rounded since then.

Puzzle: rebuild Pangaea

In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift: about 300–200 million years ago all continents were joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea (“all lands”). Drag the continents back into place, then switch on the evidence layers and see whether fossils, mountains and glacier scratches line up!
