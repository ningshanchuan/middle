Weathering, Erosion & Deposition: Earth’s Sculptors

How can a river, with nothing but water and sand, carve a canyon a mile deep? Weathering breaks rock down, erosion carries the pieces away, and deposition drops them somewhere new. Water, wind, ice and gravity are sculptors that carve canyons, deltas, dunes and U-shaped valleys over thousands to millions of years. Drag the time slider and watch the land change!

Weathering: how rock breaks down

Weathering breaks rock down where it sits (before anything carries it away). There are two main kinds:

🔨

Mechanical (physical) weathering

Rock breaks into smaller pieces but stays the same material, like breaking a cracker.

  • Ice wedging: water seeps into cracks, freezes and expands about 9%, prying the crack open.
  • Root wedging: plant roots grow in cracks and split the rock.
  • Abrasion: wind-blown sand and tumbling river rocks grind and scrape.
  • Exfoliation: when overlying rock erodes away, the pressure drops and rock peels off in sheets like an onion, as on Georgia’s Stone Mountain.
🧪

Chemical weathering

The minerals in rock change chemically into new substances.

  • Oxidation: iron-bearing minerals react with oxygen and rust, turning rock and soil red, like Georgia’s red clay.
  • Carbonic acid: rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes a weak acid that dissolves limestone and carves caves.
  • Hydrolysis: feldspar reacts with water and turns into clay, the origin of middle Georgia’s kaolin.
  • Acid rain: pollution from burning fossil fuels speeds up the weathering of marble statues and limestone buildings, a human impact.

⚠️ Common mix-up:Weathering and erosion are not the same thing. Weathering breaks rock (in place); erosion moves the pieces away. Deposition happens when the moving agent slows down and drops them.
