Why is the sea salty?

Rain slowly dissolves minerals from rocks, and rivers carry these salts to the sea. When seawater evaporates only the water leaves, so the salt has built up over millions of years.

⚖️

Salinity: about 35 ‰

On average, 1 kg of seawater holds about 35 g of salt (3.5%), mostly sodium chloride. Hot, dry seas with lots of evaporation (like the Red Sea) are saltier; river mouths and melting ice make water fresher.

🔁

The global “conveyor belt”

Near Greenland and Antarctica, seawater becomes very cold and salty (freezing sea ice leaves its salt behind), so it grows denser and sinks, then creeps along the sea floor around the world. One full loop takes about 1,000 years!

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E3: the significant role of water in Earth processes, including ocean composition (salinity), ocean-floor topography (shelf, slope, abyssal plain, trench, mid-ocean ridge), and the causes of waves, currents and tides. Suggested use: run the Sea-floor dive story and have students sketch a simple profile; switch to Currents and ask students to find the patterns (warm currents leave the equator, cold currents flow toward it; gyres turn clockwise in the north and counterclockwise in the south); finish with the density lab to explain deep currents, and connect the Gulf Stream to Georgia’s coast.

Note: the sea-floor model is greatly vertically exaggerated, and current paths are simplified. Common misconceptions: waves move water toward the shore; the deepest ocean is in the middle; all currents are caused by wind.
