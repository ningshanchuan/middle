Osmosis: Water on the Move

Forget to water a peace lily for a week and it flops over like it fainted. Water it, and a few hours later it stands up again. It has no muscles and no bones. It just moves water across millions of cell membranes.

🎯Mission: wilt a plant and save it, protect a red blood cell, and find out why osmosis matters for plants AND for you!

Need a quick review? 👉 see also Diffusion & Osmosis overview · previous: Diffusion in the Body

Passive transport: no energy needed

Osmosis: water moves across the cell membrane from an area of high water concentration to an area of low water concentration.

In other words: the more stuff (salt, sugar) is dissolved somewhere, the LESS water there is, so water moves toward it.

  • Water follows salt: water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles.
  • Only the water moves: the membrane lets water through but blocks most dissolved particles.
  • Free: osmosis is a kind of diffusion, so it uses no energy.

🚀 Water potential: water flows “downhill”
Scientists use water potential Ψ (psi, in megapascals, MPa) to say how much water “wants” to move. Pure water: Ψ = 0. More dissolved stuff → more negative Ψ. Water always flows from higher Ψ to lower (more negative) Ψ.
How strong is turgor? In a firm plant cell the pressure is about 0.5–1 MPa, that is 5–10 atmospheres, more than in a car tire (about 2.2 atm)!

👩‍🏫 Teacher / parent notes

Supports Georgia GSE S7L2 (how cell structures, especially the cell membrane, control what enters and leaves, and how cells, tissues and organ systems meet an organism’s needs). Built for the “Osmosis” row of the class hyperdoc “Comparing Cell Transport”. Note: the hyperdoc blank says “(organelle)”; the answer is the cell membrane. Strictly, the membrane is a cell structure, and some textbooks don’t call it an organelle.

Chinese curriculum link: 人教版 junior-high biology introduces how cells gain and lose water (细胞的吸水和失水) in the cell chapters (细胞膜控制物质进出) and with how roots absorb water (Grade 7 or 8, depending on the edition). The terms 渗透作用, 质壁分离 (plasmolysis) and 生理盐水 are formally taught in senior-high 必修1 《分子与细胞》, “细胞的物质输入和输出”.

Suggested flow: switch on the hot, dry day, watch the plant wilt and the cell lose water, then water it. Then add salt one spoonful at a time to see plasmolysis, and discuss why a lot of fresh water flushes salt out of a pot. In the blood lab, click the three presets and have students explain each result with “water follows salt”, then read the three “why it matters” cards. Common misconceptions: salt moves into the cell (it is the water that moves); water moves only one way (it moves both ways; look at the NET flow); plant cells burst too (the wall stops them). Advanced mode introduces water potential and osmotic pressure; the mystery-cell challenge practices a halving search.
