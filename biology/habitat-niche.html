Ecological factors

Ecological factors (生态因素, shēngtài yīnsù) affect how and where organisms live. Sunlight, water, and temperature are abiotic factors. The grass rabbits eat, the foxes that hunt them, and the microbes that make them sick are biotic factors for rabbits.

An ecosystem includes living and nonliving parts. Grasses are producers, rabbits and foxes are consumers, and some bacteria and fungi are decomposers. They interact with water, air, and nonliving parts of the soil.

Common mix-upAll the rabbits of one species living in the same meadow at the same time form a population. All the populations in the meadow form a community. The community and the nonliving environment it interacts with form an ecosystem.

Quick check · Levels

One spruce tree, five warblers

A habitat is where an organism lives, like its address. A niche describes how a species fits into its community, including how it uses resources, when it is active, and how it interacts with other species. Choose a bird to see where it often looks for insects.

In northeastern North America, Robert MacArthur found that the five warbler species often looked for insects in different parts of conifer trees. This kind of resource partitioning can reduce competition for food. The markers give a rough guide. The birds’ feeding areas overlap.

Feeding at different times can also reduce competition. For example, a hawk might hunt mice in a field by day while an owl hunts them in the same field at night.

Warbler field study (1958)

Who is competing for the same resource?

Two rabbits of the same species competing for a patch of clover show intraspecific competition. A rabbit and a deer competing for that clover show interspecific competition. Competition often gets stronger when a resource is scarce.

Relationships between organisms

A fox eating a rabbit is predation (捕食, bǔshí). Ants carrying food together show cooperation (合作, hézuò). A tick feeding on a rabbit’s blood and harming the rabbit shows parasitism (寄生, jìshēng). Competition is another relationship.

Advanced · Competitive exclusion

In a stable environment, if two species use the same limiting resource in exactly the same way and one consistently wins the competition, the weaker competitor will eventually disappear from that area. Compare the two feeding situations and decide which makes coexistence more likely.

Why do fox numbers often lag behind?

More rabbits can give foxes enough food to survive and raise more young, so fox numbers may rise after a delay. More hunting can then reduce rabbit numbers, and fox numbers may fall as prey gets scarce. This feedback illustrates ecosystem self-regulation. The model treats rabbits as the foxes’ only prey. Depending on the settings, the curves can rise and fall repeatedly or level off. Drought can also change the pattern.

Common mix-upCarrying capacity can change with conditions. When drought reduces the water supply, K can drop right away. Rabbit numbers take time to respond, so they may temporarily be above K. Disease or predation can keep rabbit numbers below resource K over time.

Advanced · Logistic growth

N is the population size. The intrinsic growth rate r is the growth rate per rabbit when the population is very small and resources are plentiful. K is the carrying capacity based on resources alone. For a population above zero, the logistic model gives growth below K, zero net change at K, and decline above K. The calculation shows the rate at the selected month in rabbits per month from the logistic term alone. The simulation also subtracts deaths from disease and predation when those settings are on.

Choose Rabbit growth and turn disease and drought off. Run the model, change only r, and run it again. Use the slider to compare how steep the curves are at first and how many rabbits there are at month 72.

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

Crowding often brings rabbits into more contact, which can help some diseases spread. In the model, the fraction dying of disease per month rises as rabbit numbers approach K from below and stops rising at K. Drought is density-independent. Starting at month 18, water supports only 45% as many rabbits as usual, rounded down. Water returns to normal at month 30. That change happens regardless of rabbit density, and its effect on the rabbits depends on other conditions.
