DNA → RNA → Protein

How does a gene become your eye color, hair or muscle? The cell first copies a gene into mRNA, then a ribosome translates the mRNA into a chain of amino acids: a protein. Follow one tiny gene as it spells a word: M-A-G-I-C!

Two steps: transcription and translation

DNA is too precious to leave the nucleus, so the cell makes a working copy first.

📝

① Transcription (in the nucleus)

  • RNA polymerase unzips the DNA at one gene.
  • Using one strand as a template, it builds mRNA by base pairing: A–U, T–A, G–C, C–G.
  • RNA has no T; it uses U (uracil) instead.
  • The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.
🔤

② Translation (at a ribosome)

  • The ribosome reads the mRNA 3 bases at a time. Each 3-base “word” is a codon.
  • tRNA brings an amino acid and matches its anticodon to the codon.
  • Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds into a chain.
  • At a stop codon the chain is released and folds into a working protein.
💡 Memory hintTRANSCRIPTION = copying (same “language”: nucleic acid → nucleic acid). TRANSLATION = changing language (bases → amino acids).

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Enrichment supporting GSE S7L3a: genes determine traits by directing the making of proteins. The tiny model gene codes for 5 amino acids, M-A-G-I-C (real proteins usually have hundreds). Suggested flow: use the story to walk through transcription and translation, then have students predict the outcome of substitution, insertion and deletion mutations in the decoder and discuss why frameshifts are usually more harmful than substitutions. Real-world link: sickle-cell anemia (one base substitution changes glutamic acid to valine).

Common misconceptions: mRNA is an identical copy of DNA (it is complementary and uses U instead of T); a gene turns directly into a trait (a protein is in between); all mutations are harmful (many have no effect).
