The eye and vision

Put the image on the retina

Light enters through the cornea, pupil and lens. The cornea and lens bend light to form an inverted real image on the retina. Light-sensitive cells respond, and signals travel through the optic nerve to the brain, where vision is formed.

Try it Choose the typical eye, lock accommodation, and bring the object close. Unlock it to let the lens become rounder and bring the light back into focus on the retina.
Common mix-up In myopia, a distant object’s image forms in front of the retina. A nearby object can still look sharp. A longer eyeball is a common cause. The model produces the same effect by giving the eye extra focusing power. A concave lens spreads the incoming rays, shifting the image farther back. The diagram combines the glasses, cornea and eye lens in one equivalent lens.

Protect your vision

Look into the distance for regular breaks during close work. Spend time outdoors in daylight and wear protective eyewear for sports or experiments that could injure your eyes. If distant text looks blurry, tell a parent or caregiver and get your vision checked.

Enrichment · Calculate the image distance

Use millimeters for object distance dₒ, image distance dᵢ and effective focal length f. The equation updates with the model.

An object is 600 mm away and the equivalent focal length is about 23.08 mm. Where will the image form?

dᵢ = (1/f − 1/dₒ)⁻¹ ≈ (1/(23.08 mm) − 1/(600 mm))⁻¹ ≈ 24.0 mm

Substitute the distances into the rearranged thin-lens equation. The image forms about 24.0 mm behind the lens, on the model retina. To check, choose the typical eye, unlock accommodation and set the slider to 60 cm.

Challenge · Lock accommodation and move the object from 120 cm to 10 cm. Which side of the retina will the image form on?

Use the typical eye for both distances. The focal length stays at about 23.53 mm. When the object moves from 1200 mm to 100 mm, the image distance grows from 24.00 mm to about 30.77 mm. The image forms behind the retina.

The ear and hearing

Memory tip Air vibrates → eardrum and ossicles vibrate → cochlear fluid moves → nerve signals. The brain uses those signals to form what you hear.
Protect your hearing

Very loud sounds or long exposure to loud noise can damage cochlear hair cells. Turn down headphones, move away from loud sources, and wear well-fitting earplugs or protective earmuffs when needed. Keep cotton swabs out of the ear canal.

How a neuron sends a signal

1 / 4

A neuron is a nerve cell. Its cell body has projections called dendrites and an axon. A nerve contains many fibers, such as axons, bundled together and wrapped in connective tissue.

Memory tip Dendrites receive, the cell body combines inputs, the axon carries the signal away, and the synapse passes it on.

The central and peripheral nervous systems

The brain and spinal cord make up the central nervous system (CNS). The peripheral nervous system (PNS) includes cranial and spinal nerves that connect the CNS with the body. Sensory information travels toward the CNS, and motor commands travel toward muscles.

Common mix-up The cerebrum is one part of the brain. When you ride a bike, your cerebrum helps process visual information and choose a direction, while your cerebellum helps coordinate movement and keep your balance.

The knee jerk and its reflex arc

    A reflex is an automatic response to a stimulus. In a knee jerk, stretch activates receptors inside the quadriceps. A sensory neuron carries the signal to a nerve center in the spinal cord. A motor neuron sends a signal back to the quadriceps, the effector. The muscle contracts and the lower leg kicks forward.

    Common mix-up The five parts of the arc do five jobs. The main knee-jerk pathway uses two neurons. The sensory neuron connects directly to the motor neuron in the spinal cord. Information about the stretch can also reach the brain.
    Simple and conditioned reflexes · Pavlov’s experiment

    Food in a dog’s mouth triggers saliva through an inborn, unconditioned reflex. Ivan Pavlov repeatedly presented a sound just before food. Later the sound alone triggered saliva, a learned response called a conditioned reflex.

    Measure your reaction time

    Start, wait for the button to turn green and say “Click now!”, then press it as quickly as you can. You can also focus the button and press Space. Try five valid trials and watch the mean change. Each set records up to ten.

    Your trials

    Valid trialReaction time (ms)

    Your eyes detect the change, your brain processes the signal, and motor nerves send a command to the muscles in your finger. Pressing the button is a voluntary action. Display and input delays affect the estimate. For comparisons, repeat trials with the same device and input method.

    US worked example · Find the mean

    Three valid trials measured 240, 260 and 280 ms. Add the times and divide by the number of valid trials. The mean reaction time is 260 ms.

    t̄ = (t₁ + t₂ + … + tₙ) / n = (240 ms + 260 ms + 280 ms) / 3 = 260 ms
    Ruler-drop experiment and Chinese-style worked example
    1. Have a partner hold the ruler vertically from the top. Align its zero mark with the gap between your open thumb and index finger, just clear of the ruler.
    2. Your partner lets go without warning. Pinch the ruler as soon as you see it fall. Read the mark at the same edge of your finger that you used to line up the zero. Record misses separately and try again.
    3. Convert each distance to a time, then average the valid times. The conversion is nonlinear.

    A ruler drops 20.0 cm from rest. Use 0.200 m for the distance and 9.8 m/s² for gravity. Neglect air resistance. How long did it fall?

    d = ½gt², so t = √(2d/g) = √(2 × 0.200 m / (9.8 m/s²)) ≈ 0.202 s

    The ruler was falling until you caught it. Convert 0.202 seconds to about 202 milliseconds.

    Endocrine glands and hormones

    Endocrine glands have no ducts. Their hormones enter the bloodstream and travel around the body. Target cells respond if they have the right receptors. Nerves and hormones can work together. Both can help speed up your heartbeat when you are startled.

    What happens when there is too little or too much?
    Four hormones and examples of imbalance
    Source and hormoneMain roleToo little or too weak an effectToo much or too strong an effect
    Pituitary · Growth hormonePromotes growth of bones and other tissues.In childhood, deficiency can cause slow growth and short stature.Excess while bones are still growing in length can cause gigantism.
    Thyroid · Thyroid hormones (including thyroxine, T₄)Regulate metabolism and support growth and development.A deficiency can cause fatigue or feeling cold. A severe deficiency in infancy can harm brain development if it goes untreated.An overactive thyroid can cause a fast heartbeat or feeling hot.
    Pancreatic islets · InsulinHelps some cells take up glucose and promotes glucose storage, lowering blood glucose.Too little insulin or a weak response to it can leave blood glucose too high.Too much insulin for the body’s needs can make blood glucose too low.
    Adrenal medulla · Adrenaline (epinephrine)Helps the body respond to sudden stress, for example by speeding the heartbeat.If the adrenaline surge is weak, nerve signals and other responses can still help the body react to stress.Excess can cause a fast heartbeat and high blood pressure.

    These effects can have other causes. In type 1 diabetes the body makes little or no insulin. In type 2 diabetes, cells respond less well to insulin, and the body may also make too little. Both can lead to high blood glucose.

    Compare nerve and hormone signaling
    Two ways the body coordinates its activities
    FeatureNerve signalingHormone signaling
    RouteElectrical signals travel along neurons. Chemical messengers carry signals across most synapses.Hormones travel in the bloodstream.
    Which cells respond?Usually, cells connected to the nerve pathway by synapses. Chemical messengers also need matching receptors.Cells with the matching receptor, sometimes in different organs.
    Onset and durationOften fast and brief, as in the knee jerk.Often slower and longer-lasting, as in growth. Adrenaline can act quickly.

    Gₙ is the glucose index at step n.

    When blood glucose rises, islets release more insulin. Muscle and fat cells take up more glucose, and the liver stores more as glycogen. When blood glucose falls, islets release more glucagon, prompting the liver to release glucose. This is negative feedback because each response reduces the original change.
