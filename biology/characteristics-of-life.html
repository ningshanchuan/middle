Is It Alive? Characteristics of Life

A campfire “eats” wood, “breathes” oxygen, grows, and even makes “baby” flames. A bean seed can lie in a drawer for five years without doing anything at all. So which one is alive? Biologists use a checklist. Today you are the life detective.

🎯Mission: crack 12 “Is it alive?” cases, examine 7 samples under the microscope, and match up the US and Chinese checklists of life!

Related lessons 👉 Cell Theory & Levels of Organization (the history of cell theory lives there) · Cell Organelles (3D cell) · Microorganisms

Key idea

Living things (organisms) share a set of characteristics. To count as alive, something must show all of them, not just one or two.

The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things: the smallest thing that can carry out ALL the life processes is one cell.

  • One is not enough: fire grows, crystals grow, robots respond, but none of them is alive.
  • Cells first: every living thing is made of one or more cells (viruses are the famous argument).
  • Alive, once alive, never alive: a wooden desk came from a once-living tree; sand was never alive.

Two checklists, one big idea

In your US class you learn the list on the left. China’s 人教版 7th-grade textbook (“生物的特征”, the characteristics of living things) uses the list on the right. Different words, same idea. Tap any card to see its partner on the other side, or play the matching challenge.

🇺🇸The US classroom list (8)

🇨🇳China’s 人教版 list (7)

Spot the differences? 人教版 splits “needs nutrition”, “breathes (respiration)” and “gets rid of wastes” into three items; the US list rolls them into one: “uses energy (metabolism)”. “Keeps a stable inside (homeostasis)” is only hinted at in the Chinese middle-school list (getting rid of wastes); the word 稳态 comes in high school. And the Chinese item 7 says “except viruses”, because a virus has no cells.

        👩‍🏫 Teacher / parent notes

        Supports Georgia GSE S7L2 (how cell structures, cells, tissues, organs and organ systems work together to meet an organism’s basic needs). Built for the start of Unit 3 Cells: the “What is a Cell? / Cell Theory” notes, the “Is It A Cell?” sort and the “A Closer Look at Life” lab. The history of cell theory (Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow) is in the “Cell Theory & Levels of Organization” lesson and is not repeated here.

        Chinese curriculum link: 人教版 Grade 7, Volume 1 (2012 edition), Unit 1 “生物和生物圈”, Chapter 1, Section 1 “生物的特征” lists the 7 characteristics on the right; Unit 2 “细胞是生命活动的基本单位” teaches the microscope and the onion-skin and cheek-cell slides (stained with iodine). The new edition used from 2024 reorganizes the units, so check the edition your school uses. The terms 稳态 (homeostasis) and 新陈代谢 (metabolism) are formally taught in senior high.

        Suggested flow: watch the animation, then play the matching challenge so students discover how the two lists line up. In the case game, discuss fire, crystals and the robot (“has only some traits”) and the virus (“the edge of the definition”). The microscope lab is about evidence: tick only what you saw, e.g. “responds to a stimulus” only after adding salt water. Common misconceptions: if it moves it’s alive (plants and seeds barely move); if it grows it’s alive (crystals); dead and never-alive are the same (wood vs sand); a cell part such as the nucleus is alive by itself.
