How do I read the SIR model?

SIR stands for susceptible, infectious, and recovered. People in this model become infectious as soon as they are infected, then move to R when they recover and stay immune. Everyone vaccinated before day 0 is assumed to be protected and starts in R. The model estimates can be decimal numbers.

Worked example · Change in I

With all measures off, the day 1 model estimates 0.496 new infections and 0.167 recoveries. Subtract recoveries from new infections to find the net change in I.

ΔI ≈ 0.496 − 0.167 = 0.329

I increases by about 0.329 people. The decimal is an estimate from the model.

The three links in a chain of infection

Source of infectionA person or animal that can spread a pathogen. Isolation limits contact with an infectious person.
Route of transmissionHow a pathogen reaches another host. It might travel through air, hands, food, water, or a vector such as a mosquito.
Susceptible populationPeople who can still catch the infection. Vaccination can help protect them.

A pathogen passes from a source along a route to a susceptible person. Washing hands reduces transfer by hands. A well-fitting mask can reduce infectious particles breathed out or in.

Remember the three jobs. Control the source, interrupt the route, and protect susceptible people.

The body’s three lines of defense

0°

Line 1 blocks and line 2 engulfs. Both are innate (nonspecific) defenses. Line 3 is adaptive (specific) immunity. It recognizes particular targets and can form memory.

How do body systems work together?

Airway mucus traps particles and tiny hairs called cilia sweep the mucus toward the throat. At a cut, the circulatory system carries white blood cells nearby so some can enter the damaged tissue. Immune defense depends on several systems working together.

What happens at a second encounter?

After a first response, some B and T lymphocytes become memory cells. These cells help the body respond faster when it encounters the same antigen again. Controlling the pathogen earlier can make the illness milder.

How do vaccines help people and communities?

How many people are immune?

Enrichment · 课外拓展

The marker shows the herd-immunity threshold in this model, about 66.7%.

Assume one infectious person would infect 3 others on average in a fully susceptible group, so R₀ = 3. Here p is the immune share, and Rₑ is the average number one infectious person would infect at that share.

The model assumes even mixing and immunity that fully blocks infection. At the threshold, Rₑ equals 1. Above the threshold, spread tends to shrink. When many people are immune, susceptible people are also less likely to encounter the pathogen.

Build immune memory in advance

A vaccine supplies an antigen or gives cells instructions to make it briefly. The immune system can make matching antibodies and memory cells. Later, it can usually respond faster to the same pathogen.

Vaccinated share and immune share measure different things. How well protection works, how long it lasts, and how people mix all affect herd immunity.

Routine immunization (计划免疫, jìhuà miǎnyì) helps prevent infectious disease by giving vaccines on a schedule. Schedules can differ by vaccine.

Worked examples · Herd-immunity threshold

US-style · Interpret the model

If 75% of the group is immune, what happens to spread? The susceptible share is 25%.

Rₑ = 3 × (1 − 0.75) = 0.75

That is 0.75 new infections per infectious person, less than 1. Spread tends to shrink. Set the slider to 75% to check.

Chinese-style · Given / Find / Solve / Answer

Given R₀ = 3. Find the immune fraction p that makes Rₑ = 1. Substitute and convert the fraction to a percent.

3 × (1 − p) = 1 → p = 1 − ⅓ = ⅔

The threshold is about 66.7%. The slider uses whole percentages, so 67% is its first setting above the threshold.

Vaccine history · Smallpox and polio
  1. Mid-1500s — Written records describe variolation in China. Forms of variolation were also practiced elsewhere in Asia and in parts of Africa. The practice carried a risk of smallpox and further spread.
  2. 1796 — Edward Jenner demonstrated that inoculation with cowpox could protect against smallpox, an early success in vaccination.
  3. 1980 — After vaccination and case finding by health workers around the world, the World Health Assembly officially confirmed the global eradication of smallpox.
  4. 1955 and 1961 — The United States licensed Jonas Salk’s inactivated polio vaccine in 1955 and its first oral polio vaccines, based on Albert Sabin’s weakened strains, in 1961. Polio is short for poliomyelitis, which can cause paralysis.
  5. Polio prevention in China — Gu Fangzhou and colleagues developed a sugar-pill form of oral vaccine that was easier to give to children. In 2000, the WHO Western Pacific Region, including China, was certified free of indigenous wild poliovirus transmission.

Antibiotics, resistance, and healthy decisions

Which bacteria remain?

Each colored circle represents one bacterium. An empty circle shows a bacterium is gone. The example uses made-up counts for one antibiotic and one group of bacteria.

What can you do?

An antibiotic can kill certain bacteria or slow their growth. Antibiotics do not work against viruses. Bacteria that are already resistant can survive and multiply. Take antibiotics as prescribed. Avoid sharing them or using leftovers on your own.

  • Wash with soap and water before eating and after using the restroom. Cover a sneeze with a tissue or the inside of your elbow.
  • If you feel unwell, tell a trusted adult. Together you can decide about rest, limiting contact, or getting medical help.
