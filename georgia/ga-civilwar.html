Georgia in the Civil War

In 1860 about 462,000 Georgians — 44% of everyone in the state — were enslaved. Five years later slavery was over and Atlanta lay in ruins. How did it happen?

What were “states’ rights” and nullification?

“States’ rights” was the idea that a state could resist federal laws it believed went too far. In 1832 South Carolina declared a federal tariff “null and void” there (nullification) and threatened to leave the Union. By 1860 Southern leaders used states’ rights mainly to protect slavery.

The Georgia Platform (1850)

In December 1850 a Georgia convention at Milledgeville agreed to accept the Compromise of 1850 — but only if the North enforced the Fugitive Slave Act and did not ban slavery in new territories. It kept the Union together for ten more years.

Alexander Stephens

Georgia congressman. In November 1860 he urged Georgia’s legislature not to rush out of the Union — yet months later he became Vice President of the Confederacy and, in his “Cornerstone Speech” in Savannah, said the Confederacy’s cornerstone was slavery and white supremacy.

January 1861: the secession convention at Milledgeville

After Lincoln’s election Georgia held a convention to decide. “Immediate secessionists” wanted out now; “cooperationists” wanted to wait — to act together with other Southern states or bargain first. Decide where each leader stood, then reveal the real vote.

The Union blockade

The “Anaconda Plan” used warships to seal Southern ports, stopping cotton exports and weapons imports. In April 1862 Union rifled cannon broke Fort Pulaski’s brick walls in about 30 hours, nearly closing the port of Savannah.

📜

Emancipation Proclamation

Took effect January 1, 1863, declaring enslaved people in the rebelling states — including Georgia — free. Freedom moved forward wherever Union armies went; the 13th Amendment ended slavery for good in 1865.

⛓️

Andersonville (Camp Sumter)

The largest Confederate prison camp, opened February 1864. About 45,000 Union prisoners were held here; nearly 13,000 died of disease, hunger, bad water and exposure. At its peak in August 1864 about 33,000 men were crowded into 26.5 acres (about 20 football fields) — roughly a 6-by-6-foot space each.

“I beg to present you as a Christmas gift the city of Savannah, with one hundred and fifty heavy guns and plenty of ammunition, also about twenty-five thousand bales of cotton.”— General William T. Sherman, telegram to President Lincoln, December 22, 1864
⚠️ Don’t mix up:Chickamauga (1863) was a Confederate victory; the Atlanta Campaign (1864) was a Union victory. Sherman’s March to the Sea came AFTER Atlanta fell — from Atlanta to Savannah, not from Chattanooga.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence, Grade 8 Georgia Studies — SS8H5, Analyze the impact of the Civil War on Georgia: a. key issues and events that led to the war — slavery, states’ rights, nullification, the Compromise of 1850 and the Georgia Platform, the Dred Scott case, Abraham Lincoln’s election in 1860, and the debate over secession in Georgia; b. Georgia’s role in the war — the Union blockade of Georgia’s coast, the Emancipation Proclamation, Chickamauga, Sherman’s Atlanta Campaign, Sherman’s March to the Sea, and Andersonville.

Notes: in line with the standard and historians’ consensus, slavery is presented as the root cause and states’ rights as the main argument used to defend it (Georgia’s 1861 Declaration of Causes refers to slavery again and again). Battles are about strategy, terrain and logistics, without gory detail; Andersonville figures follow the National Park Service. March routes are schematic, traced from standard maps. The “tension thermometer” is a teaching model, not a measurement.

Link to China’s curriculum: the national textbook World History, Grade 9 Volume 2, Lesson 3 “The American Civil War” covers the North–South conflict, Lincoln’s election, the Emancipation Proclamation and the war’s meaning; this lesson adds what happened on the ground in Georgia.

Suggested use: have students rank the eight events on the tension thermometer and explain each; then use the war map to measure Sherman’s march from Atlanta to Savannah (about 300 miles) and discuss why “total war” weakened the Confederacy.
