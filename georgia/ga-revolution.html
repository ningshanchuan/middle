Revolution & Early Statehood

In 1775 Georgia was the youngest and smallest of the 13 colonies — and many Georgians did not want independence at all. Why not? And what changed their minds?

Your mission: drag the years to watch the war sweep across Georgia, decode the three parts of the Declaration of Independence, fix the Articles of Confederation — and then survive the war as a backcountry family choosing between Patriots and Loyalists.

Cause 1: French and Indian War (1754–1763)

Britain won (in Europe it was the Seven Years’ War) but was deep in debt. Georgia’s border moved south to the St. Marys River and the French and Spanish threats faded — so colonists needed British protection less.

Cause 2: Proclamation of 1763

The King banned settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains to avoid wars with Native nations. Land-hungry settlers were furious. In Georgia it meant no settling beyond the treaty lines with the Creek and Cherokee.

Cause 3: The Stamp Act (1765)

Newspapers, legal papers, even playing cards needed a tax stamp. Georgia’s Liberty Boys (Sons of Liberty) protested — yet Georgia was the only colony where stamps were actually sold, because Governor James Wright enforced the law. It was repealed in 1766.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:Kettle Creek (February 1779) was a Patriot WIN; the Siege of Savannah (October 1779) was a big French-and-Patriot LOSS. “February win, October loss.”

Who were Georgia’s three signers?

56 men signed the Declaration; 3 of them signed for Georgia. Pick 3 of these signatures, then press Check.

Memory hint“Good Hearted Warriors” = Gwinnett, Hall, Walton. All three have Georgia counties named for them.
Stories of the three signers

Button Gwinnett: a merchant who briefly led the state in 1777 and died that year after a duel with General Lachlan McIntosh — his autograph is so rare it is worth a fortune. Lyman Hall: a doctor from Midway (St. John’s Parish), sent to Congress in 1775, later governor. George Walton: in his twenties, one of the youngest signers; wounded and captured in the war, later governor and judge.

📜

Georgia Constitution of 1777

A one-house legislature held most of the power; the governor was chosen by the legislature for just 1 year. It created the first 8 counties.

🏛️

Georgia Constitution of 1789

Rewritten to match the new US Constitution: a two-house General Assembly (Senate + House) and a governor with a 2-year term.

✍️

Baldwin & Few

Abraham Baldwin and William Few signed the US Constitution for Georgia. On January 2, 1788 Georgia ratified it unanimously — the 4th state — because it wanted a strong Union to protect it against Spanish Florida and frontier wars.

🎓

University of Georgia (1785)

Chartered on January 27, 1785 — the first state-chartered public university in the US. Baldwin wrote the charter: a new republic needs educated citizens. Classes began in Athens in 1801.

★

Capitals march west

Savannah → Augusta (1786) → Louisville (1796) → Milledgeville (1807) → Atlanta (1868). As settlers pushed inland, the capital followed so lawmakers stayed near the people.

⚖️

One vote in the Great Compromise

On July 2, 1787 big and small states deadlocked over seats in the Senate. Baldwin switched his vote, making a tie that kept talks alive — leading to the deal: House by population, two senators per state.

🐎

Elijah Clarke

Wilkes County militia leader. Led a column at Kettle Creek in 1779 and helped retake Augusta in 1781. Clarke County is named for him.

🏠

Nancy Hart

Frontier heroine. Legend says Loyalist soldiers barged into her cabin and demanded dinner; she slipped away their muskets and held them all prisoner. Details come from later retellings, but Hart County and Hartwell are named for her.

🎖️

Austin Dabney

A young African American who fought as a Patriot artilleryman and was badly wounded at Kettle Creek. In 1786 the legislature formally freed him for his service and later granted him land — very rare at the time.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence, Grade 8 Georgia Studies — SS8H3, Analyze the role of Georgia in the American Revolutionary Era: a. causes as they impacted Georgia (French and Indian War, Proclamation of 1763, Stamp Act); b. the three parts of the Declaration of Independence (preamble, grievances, declaration) and the three Georgia signers; c. the significance of Loyalists and Patriots, incl. the Battle of Kettle Creek and the Siege of Savannah; d. weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation and how they led to a new federal Constitution. Also touches SS8H4a (reasons for the University of Georgia and the westward movement of the capitals).

Notes: Elijah Clarke, Nancy Hart and Austin Dabney were named in the older Georgia Performance Standards and still appear in GPB’s textbook, so they are kept here. Nancy Hart’s story comes mainly from later retellings — teach it as legend. Under the GSE three-part model, “We hold these truths…” is counted as part of the preamble; some books treat it as a separate “statement of rights.” Chart values are American Battlefield Trust estimates. The decision game is a teaching model built on real events, not one real family.

Link to China’s curriculum: the national textbook World History, Grade 9 Volume 1, Lesson 18 “American Independence” covers the Declaration, Saratoga and the 1787 Constitution; this lesson tells the same story from one state’s point of view.

Suggested use: have students trace how Savannah and Augusta changed hands in 1778–1782, then play the family game and discuss: why were so many Georgians slow to support independence?
