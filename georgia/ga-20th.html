Wars, Depression & the New Deal

A beetle smaller than a pencil eraser wrecked Georgia’s cotton. How did a bug, a stock-market crash, a president at Warm Springs and a bomber plant remake Georgia?

FDR and Warm Springs

Franklin Roosevelt caught polio in 1921 and first came to Warm Springs in 1924, hoping the buoyant 88°F (31°C) spring water would help. He founded a polio treatment center there and in 1932 built the “Little White House.” What he saw of rural poverty around Warm Springs shaped New Deal programs such as the Rural Electrification Administration. He died at the Little White House on 12 April 1945.

🧢

Eugene Talmadge: against the New Deal

Talmadge, governor 1933–1937, called himself the poor farmer’s friend. He believed in small government and states’ rights, opposed New Deal relief wages and many federal programs, and tried to keep some out of Georgia. In 1936 he ran for the US Senate and lost to the pro–New Deal Richard Russell.

Carl Vinson

Congressman from Milledgeville who led the House Naval Affairs Committee. His laws started building a navy big enough for both the Atlantic and the Pacific before WWII — he is called the “father of the two-ocean Navy.” A nuclear aircraft carrier is named after him.

🏛️

Richard Russell

Former governor, US senator from 1933, a New Deal supporter who pushed the National School Lunch Program. A powerful member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, he helped bring military bases and defense jobs to Georgia. (He also fought civil-rights laws for decades.)

🚢

Road to war: 1941

In March 1941 the Lend-Lease Act let the US lend weapons to Britain and other Allies — the “arsenal of democracy.” On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and the US entered the war. Georgia’s plants, shipyards and bases went into full gear.

Notes: the cotton field is a simplified teaching model (about 0.54 bale per acre — Georgia’s 1914 average; weevil growth rate, 15¢ cotton and $25 per acre net for other crops are assumptions).
