Land, Gold & Cherokee Removal

In 1733 Georgia was a thin strip of coast. By 1838 it covered about 37 million acres. That land was the home of the Creek (Muscogee) and Cherokee nations — so how did it change hands?

The headright system (from 1783)

After the Revolution, every head of household could claim 200 acres free, plus 50 more for each family member (enslaved people were counted too), up to 1,000 acres. Claimants picked and surveyed their own plots — so lots had odd shapes, and some land was granted twice.

The Yazoo land fraud (1795)

Bribed lawmakers sold about 35 million acres of Georgia’s western land (most of today’s Alabama and Mississippi) to four land companies for $500,000 — about 1.5 cents an acre! Voters were furious; in 1796 a new legislature cancelled the deal and burned the papers in public at Louisville.

The Compact of 1802

Georgia gave its western land claims to the US government for $1.25 million — and a federal promise to end Native land titles inside Georgia as soon as possible. For decades Georgia’s leaders used that promise to pressure Washington.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:Headright = claim it and survey it yourself (from 1783). Land lottery = the state surveyed neat lots first, then gave them out by drawing (1805–1833). Lotteries were fairer and tidier — but the land was taken from Native nations.

The cotton gin (1793)

Eli Whitney built it near Savannah. It pulled the seeds out of short-staple cotton fast, so cotton became hugely profitable — and planters wanted more land and more enslaved workers.

🚂

Railroads (from the 1830s)

The state-built Western & Atlantic Railroad set its southern end point, “Terminus,” in 1837. The town became Marthasville, then Atlanta in 1845. Rails carried cotton to the ports and created new towns.

🗺️

How a lottery worked

One drum held tickets with names; another held lot numbers mixed with blanks. A name and a ticket were drawn together: a lot number meant you won and could get the grant for a small fee ($18 per lot in 1832).

The Cherokee Nation: writing, a newspaper and the courts

In the 1820s the Cherokee built a nation with a written constitution (1827), a capital at New Echota, courts and a bilingual newspaper. They meant to show they could live the way Americans did — and stay on the land of their ancestors.

Mission 3: write with Sequoyah’s syllabary

Sequoyah could not read or write any language, yet by 1821 he created a syllabary: 86 symbols, each standing for one syllable. Tap the symbols to spell the target word.

A writing system you could learn in weeks

English letters stand for single sounds; one Cherokee symbol is a whole syllable. A Cherokee speaker who learned the symbols could read and write — and soon people were writing letters, laws and a newspaper with it.

✍️

Sequoyah

Finished the Cherokee syllabary in 1821; the Cherokee Nation adopted it in 1825. One of the very few writing systems in history created by one person.

🏛️

John Ross

Principal Chief (1828–1866). He fought removal through courts, petitions and talks; in 1836 he took a protest signed by about 15,000 Cherokee to Congress.

⚖️

Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

Georgia jailed missionary Samuel Worcester for living in the Cherokee Nation without a state license. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled the Cherokee Nation was a distinct community where Georgia’s laws had no force. Georgia ignored it, and President Jackson did not enforce it.

⛏️

Dahlonega gold rush (1829)

Gold was found in the Cherokee Nation in 1828–29, and thousands of miners — the “Twenty-Niners” — rushed in. “Dahlonega” comes from the Cherokee word for yellow. A US Mint opened there in 1838.

🦅

Andrew Jackson

President (1829–1837). In 1830 he signed the Indian Removal Act, which let him trade land west of the Mississippi for Native lands in the East.

🪶

William McIntosh (Creek)

Lower Creek leader. In 1825 he signed the Treaty of Indian Springs, giving up almost all remaining Creek land in Georgia. Creek law made selling Creek land a crime punished by death; that April he was executed by Creek law enforcers. The treaty was replaced, but by 1827 all Creek land in Georgia was gone.

Primary sources: two voices

“We wish to remain on the land of our fathers. We have a perfect and original right to remain without interruption or molestation.”— Memorial of the Cherokee Nation to Congress, 1830
“The decision of the supreme court has fell still born, and they find that it cannot coerce Georgia to yield to its mandate.”— Andrew Jackson, letter to John Coffee, April 1832

The Treaty of New Echota (1835)

In December 1835 a small group of Cherokee — the “Treaty Party,” including Major Ridge, John Ridge and Elias Boudinot — signed at New Echota, giving up all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for $5 million. They were not the elected government, and most Cherokee opposed it. The US Senate approved it by a single vote more than it needed, setting May 1838 as the deadline to leave.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:“John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it!” is probably a later invention — there’s no reliable record Jackson said it. What he really wrote was that the decision “fell still born.”

The Trail of Tears (1838–1839)

In May 1838 General Winfield Scott and about 7,000 soldiers began forcing the Cherokee out. About 16,000 Cherokee were made to leave their homes; an estimated 4,000 died of disease, cold and hunger in the camps and on the way. Drag the slider to follow the main northern route.

“We are denationalized; we are disfranchised. We are deprived of membership in the human family!”— John Ross, letter to the US Senate and House of Representatives, 1836
The Cherokee rebuilt their nation in Indian Territory (today’s Oklahoma), with Tahlequah as its capital. Today the Cherokee Nation is one of the largest tribal nations in the US, and Cherokee who escaped removal in the North Carolina mountains became today’s Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians.
