Founding the Georgia Colony

In 1732 a group of London reformers got a charter from the king for a colony with no slaves, no rum and no giant plantations. Twenty years later they handed it back to the king. What went wrong?

Your mission: pick the spot for Savannah, lay out Oglethorpe’s town plan, run the Trustee colony through 20 years of decisions, and see what changed under the royal governors.

Mission 1: where should Savannah go?

In January 1733 James Oglethorpe reached South Carolina with 114 colonists from the ship Anne. He needed a town site south of the Savannah River — between English Carolina and Spanish Florida. Click each candidate on the map (a simplified map of the coast), compare the five factors, then decide.

Why found Georgia? Three reasons

🤝

Charity (philanthropy)

Give London’s “worthy poor” — including people freed from debtors’ prison — a fresh start through hard work. The Trustees’ motto: “Not for self, but for others.” (Very few actual debtors came.)

💰

Economics (mercantilism)

Produce goods England had to buy from other countries — silk, wine, medicines — and buy English goods in return. The colony’s seal even showed silkworms.

🛡️

Defense (a buffer)

Put a “buffer” colony between rich South Carolina and Spanish Florida to block the Spanish and their allies.

🎩

James Oglethorpe

Member of Parliament, soldier and prison reformer — the only Trustee who came to Georgia. He planned Savannah, made treaties with Native leaders and led the defense against Spain.

🦅

Tomochichi

Mico (chief) of the Yamacraw. He let the colonists settle on Yamacraw Bluff, helped make a treaty with the Creek, and in 1734 sailed to London with Oglethorpe to meet King George II.

🗣️

Mary Musgrove

Daughter of a Creek mother and an English trader, she spoke both languages. She ran a trading post near Yamacraw Bluff and interpreted for Oglethorpe and Tomochichi — the bridge between the two sides.

Mission 2: lay out Oglethorpe’s Savannah

Savannah was built from identical “wards”: a square in the middle, public “trust lots” east and west, and house lots north and south. Click the parts of a ward to learn what they were for, then press “Add a ward” to watch the city grow.

Mission 3: you are a Trustee (1733–1752)

The Trustees’ three rules: ① no slavery; ② no rum; ③ 50 acres per family, which could not be sold and passed only to sons. Each turn, make a decision and watch the colony change; then see what really happened and answer “why?” — you score by explaining the history.

⚓

John Reynolds (1754–1757)

First royal governor, a navy officer. He quarreled with his council and was called home. Still, the first elected Commons House of Assembly met under him in 1755.

🧭

Henry Ellis (1757–1760)

Calmed the feuding factions, set up a budget and taxes, divided the colony into eight parishes (election districts) in 1758, and kept peace with the Creek. He left because of poor health.

👑

James Wright (1760–1776)

The longest-serving and most able royal governor. Treaties in 1763 and 1773 took huge areas of land from the Creek and Cherokee, and the rice economy boomed. When the Revolution came, he fled in 1776.

What did colonial Georgia make and trade?

🌾 Rice (coastal plantations)🔵 Indigo (blue dye)🦌 Deerskins (trade with Native peoples)🪵 Lumber & naval stores (tar, pitch)🐄 Cattle & hogs🐛 Silk (the Trustees’ plan — it failed)

Services: merchants, boatmen, blacksmiths, carpenters, interpreters and trading posts. Rice and indigo depended on enslaved labor — brutal, dangerous work in the swampy rice fields.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:The rum ban ended in 1742 and the slavery ban in 1751 — both changed near the end of the Trustee period, not after it. What was new in the royal period: a royal governor, an elected assembly, and land you could buy, sell and receive by headright.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standard: Georgia Standards of Excellence, Grade 8 Georgia Studies — SS8H2 “Analyze the colonial period of Georgia’s history”: a. explain the importance of the Charter of 1732, including the reasons for settlement (philanthropy, economics and defense); b. analyze the relationship between James Oglethorpe, Tomochichi and Mary Musgrove in establishing the city of Savannah at Yamacraw Bluff; c. evaluate the role of diverse groups (Jews, Salzburgers, Highland Scots and Malcontents) in settling Georgia during the Trustee Period; d. explain the transition of Georgia into a royal colony with regard to land ownership, slavery, alcohol and government; e. give examples of the kinds of goods and services produced and traded in colonial Georgia. Also touches SS8H1b (British interests in the Southeast).

Notes: the site scores and the Trustee game’s meters (colonists, safety, discontent) are simplified models that show consequences; the “what really happened” facts follow the New Georgia Encyclopedia and the Georgia Historical Society. The game scores explanations of history, not population or wealth, so slavery is never presented as a “winning” option. Population figures are estimates; estimates of the enslaved population in 1750 range from about 500 to about 1,000.

Link to China’s curriculum: the national textbook World History, Grade 9 Volume 1, Lesson 18 “American Independence” begins with Britain’s 13 colonies in North America — Georgia (1732) was the last of them.

Suggested use: after the game, have students write a “because… so…” paragraph explaining why the Trustees’ rules changed between 1742 and 1752. Then discuss: who gained when slavery was allowed, and who paid the price?
