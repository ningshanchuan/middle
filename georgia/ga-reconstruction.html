Reconstruction & the New South

In 1865 about 460,000 enslaved Georgians became free. So why, thirty years later, were so many of them trapped in debt and blocked from voting?

Your mission: track how freedom was won — and then chipped away — on the timeline, survive “settling-up day” on a cotton farm, and run the newsroom that names the people who shaped the New South.

Lincoln’s plan (1863)

The “Ten Percent Plan”: once 10% of a state’s 1860 voters swore loyalty to the Union and accepted the end of slavery, the state could form a new government. Most Confederates would be pardoned. Lincoln was killed in April 1865 before it could be carried out.

Johnson’s plan (1865)

President Andrew Johnson pardoned most Southerners, but rich planters and top officials had to ask him personally. States had to cancel secession, ratify the 13th Amendment and refuse to pay Confederate debts. Georgia did all three at Milledgeville in 1865 — and then passed “Black Codes” that limited freedpeople’s lives.

Congress’s plan (1867)

Angry about the Black Codes, Congress passed the Reconstruction Acts: the South was split into 5 military districts (Georgia was in the Third, with Alabama and Florida). To rejoin the Union, a state had to write a new constitution letting Black men vote and ratify the 14th Amendment.

🏫

Freedmen’s Bureau (1865–1872)

Goal: help freedpeople start new lives. Outcome: food and medicine, supervised work contracts, opened schools and helped found colleges such as Atlanta University. But it never had enough money, and the promised “forty acres” of land never came.

⚠️

Ku Klux Klan (from 1866)

Goal: restore white supremacy and stop Black men from voting or holding office. Method: threats and violence against Black leaders and Republicans. Outcome: many Black voters were frightened away from the polls — exactly what the Klan wanted.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:13th (1865) = freedom from slavery; 14th (1868) = citizenship and equal protection of the laws; 15th (1870) = the vote can’t be denied because of race (for men). “13 free, 14 citizen, 15 vote.”

Mission 2: survive settling-up day

With no land and no cash, most freedpeople became sharecroppers or tenant farmers. Sharecropper or tenant farmer — what’s the difference?

A sharecropper owned almost nothing: the landowner supplied land, mule, tools and seed and took about half the cotton. A tenant farmer owned a mule and tools and paid rent (cash or about a quarter of the crop), so kept more — but paid his own costs. Both usually bought on credit under a “crop lien”: the crop was promised to the merchant before it was even grown.

The New South: factories, railroads — and Jim Crow

After Reconstruction, Georgia’s leaders wanted a South of factories like the North. Atlanta built mills, railroads and world’s fairs. At the same time, new laws separated Black Georgians and took away their votes.

📰

Henry Grady

Editor of the Atlanta Constitution. In 1886 his “New South” speech in New York invited Northern money and mills.

🎩

Bourbon Triumvirate

Joseph E. Brown, Alfred Colquitt and John B. Gordon took turns in power (about 1872–1890): low taxes, pro-business, white supremacy — and the cruel convict-lease system.

🌾

Tom Watson & the Populists

In the 1890s Watson spoke for poor farmers and won Rural Free Delivery of mail (RFD). He first urged Black and white farmers to unite — later he turned to racist and antisemitic attacks.

🗳️

Rebecca Latimer Felton

Reformer who fought convict leasing and backed women’s suffrage; in 1922 the first woman to serve in the US Senate (for one day). She also publicly defended lynching — reformers could hold racist views too.

⚖️

Jim Crow & Plessy

“Jim Crow” laws segregated schools, trains and parks. In 1896 the Supreme Court’s Plessy v. Ferguson ruling allowed “separate but equal.” Poll taxes, the white primary and a 1908 literacy test took away Black votes.

🕊️

1906 Atlanta race massacre

In September 1906, during a governor’s race full of racist speeches, newspapers printed unproven, sensational stories. White mobs then attacked Black people in downtown Atlanta; at least 25 Black Atlantans were killed. Historians now often call it a massacre, not a “riot.”

Two roads to equal rights

“In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.”— Booker T. Washington, Atlanta Exposition speech, 1895 (the “Atlanta Compromise”)

W. E. B. Du Bois, teaching at Atlanta University, answered in The Souls of Black Folk (1903): Black Americans should demand full civil rights and higher education now, not later.

The Leo Frank case (1913–1915)

In 1913 Leo Frank, the Jewish manager of an Atlanta pencil factory, was convicted of murdering 13-year-old worker Mary Phagan — on weak evidence, amid antisemitic newspaper attacks. In 1915 Governor John Slaton changed his death sentence to life in prison. A mob then took Frank from the state prison at Milledgeville and lynched him near Marietta. In 1986 Georgia granted a pardon, admitting the state had failed to protect him.
