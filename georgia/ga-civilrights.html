The Civil Rights Movement in Georgia

After World War II, Atlanta called itself “the city too busy to hate.” Was that true — or just a clever slogan? From a Black voter turned away at the polls in 1944 to Martin Luther King Jr.’s funeral in 1968, Georgians changed their state with ballots, sit-ins, songs and lawsuits.

Your mission: follow the movement on the timeline map, crack the county unit system that let a loser win, see how tractors pushed people from farms to cities, and walk the Freedom Trail clue challenge.

Martin Luther King Jr.

Born on Auburn Avenue, Atlanta, in 1929. He led the SCLC, insisted on nonviolence, joined the Albany Movement, gave the “I Have a Dream” speech at the 1963 March on Washington and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964.

🚶

John Lewis

Chairman of SNCC (1963–1966) and the youngest speaker at the March on Washington. Arrested many times, he later served Atlanta in Congress for more than thirty years and urged people to make “good trouble.”

🏛️

SCLC vs SNCC

SCLC: founded in Atlanta in 1957, led by Black ministers, King as president. SNCC: founded by college students in North Carolina in April 1960, headquartered in Atlanta from that October, organizing in small towns like Albany.

🏙️

Hartsfield & Allen

Mayor William B. Hartsfield (until 1962) built Atlanta into an air hub and promoted the “too busy to hate” image; today’s airport carries his name. Ivan Allen Jr. (1962–1970) took down City Hall’s segregation signs on his first day, built a stadium and brought pro sports teams.

🚩

Answering Brown v. Board

In 1956, during a “massive resistance” session, legislators put the Confederate battle flag on the state flag. In 1960 the Sibley Commission held 10 hearings; about 60% of witnesses wanted total segregation, but its report recommended “local option” — let each school district decide rather than close the schools.

🍽️

Lester Maddox

After the Civil Rights Act of 1964, he turned Black customers away from his Pickrick restaurant (July 3, 1964); after losing in court he closed it rather than integrate. In the 1966 election no one won a majority, and the General Assembly chose him as governor.

One quote, two ways to read it

“A city too busy to hate.”— the slogan Mayor William B. Hartsfield used for Atlanta

Supporters say Atlanta desegregated schools peacefully through negotiation, avoided Birmingham-style violence and attracted business. Critics point out that in 1963 student leaders wrote to Mayor Allen that three years had passed without reaching their goals — the slogan ran ahead of reality.

The Albany Movement (1961–62): learning from a setback

SNCC’s Charles Sherrod and Cordell Reagon came to register voters; local groups united under Dr. William G. Anderson. More than 1,000 people were jailed. Police Chief Laurie Pritchett avoided violence in front of cameras and sent prisoners to jails in nearby counties, so the protests drew less national attention. King admitted the goals weren’t met — but he took the lessons to Birmingham, and the next spring Albany’s city commission removed its segregation laws. The movement’s freedom songs spread from here across the country.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:SCLC (1957, ministers, King) ≠ SNCC (1960, students, Lewis). The 1956 flag = a symbol of resisting Brown; the 1960 Sibley Commission = a way out that kept schools open.

Mission 2: lose the vote, win the election?

In 1946, after the courts ended the white primary, about 125,000 Black Georgians registered to vote. Moderate James V. Carmichael won the popular vote 313,389 to 297,245 — but Eugene Talmadge won the county unit vote 242 to 146 and was declared the winner. Move the sliders to see how the system worked.

Do the math: 556,326 ÷ 6,980 ≈ 80. One vote in those small counties counted about as much as 80 votes in Fulton! In 1962 a federal court struck the system down in Gray v. Sanders (upheld by the US Supreme Court in 1963), and “one person, one vote” took over.

What happened next? The “three governors” (1947)

Eugene Talmadge died in December 1946, before taking office. On January 15, 1947, the General Assembly elected his son Herman Talmadge; outgoing governor Ellis Arnall refused to leave his office; and lieutenant governor-elect M. E. Thompson claimed the job too. In March 1947 the Georgia Supreme Court ruled 5–2 that Thompson was the rightful governor. Herman Talmadge won a special election in 1948.

Mission 3: from farm to city

After World War II, tractors replaced mules and mechanical cotton pickers replaced crews of hand pickers.
