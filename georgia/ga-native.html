Native Americans of Georgia

Long before Georgia had its name, people piled up a 63-ft (19 m) earth mound beside the Etowah River — with no metal tools, no wheels and no horses. Who were they, and what happened when Spanish soldiers marched in in 1540?

How do archaeologists know how old things are?

Three ways: ① superposition — lower layers formed first, so they are older; ② artifact styles — Clovis points, for example, were only made in the Paleo era; ③ radiocarbon dating — measuring how much carbon-14 is left in charcoal or bone. The original floor of the Ocmulgee Earth Lodge was radiocarbon-dated to about AD 1015.

Europeans arrive: why did they come, and what did they bring?

In the 1500s the Spanish pushed north from Florida; in the 1600s–1700s the English pushed south from Carolina. They wanted different things — and both changed Native lives forever.

⚔️

Spain: gold, God, glory

Find riches (like those taken in Peru), spread Catholic Christianity and claim land for the king. In 1540 Hernando de Soto marched through Georgia with about 600 men and 220 horses. Later Spain built missions on the barrier islands.

🦌

England: trade and land

English traders from Carolina swapped guns, cloth and iron kettles for deerskins hunted by Native men — tens of thousands of skins were shipped to England each year. England also wanted land, and wanted to keep Spain boxed into Florida.

🦠

The invisible invaders

Europeans brought smallpox, measles and other diseases Native people had never met. Within a few years of de Soto’s visit, the powerful chiefdoms began to collapse — archaeologists see disease as a major cause. Many towns lost most of their people; survivors regrouped into the Creek and Cherokee societies of later times.

Spanish missions on the barrier islands

From the late 1500s through the 1600s, Spanish Franciscan friars built missions on the islands of the Guale people — the most important was Santa Catalina de Guale on St. Catherines Island. Native people were pressed to become Catholic, to work for the missions and St. Augustine, and to give corn. In 1597 the Guale rose up and burned the mission. After 1680 raids by Westo warriors armed by the Carolina English, the missions retreated south.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standard: Georgia Standards of Excellence, Grade 8 Georgia Studies — SS8H1 “Evaluate the impact of European exploration and settlement on American Indians in Georgia”: a. describe the characteristics of American Indians living in Georgia at the time of European contact — culture, food, weapons/tools and shelter; b. explain reasons for European exploration and settlement of North America, with emphasis on the interests of the Spanish and British in the Southeastern area; c. evaluate the impact of Spanish contact on American Indians, including the explorations of Hernando de Soto and the establishment of Spanish missions along the barrier islands.

Notes: the four prehistoric eras (Paleo, Archaic, Woodland, Mississippian) were required in the older GPS standard (SS8H1a); the current GSE focuses on the contact era. This lesson keeps the prehistoric eras as background (the dig, the mounds) and answers SS8H1a with “The 1700s: Creek and Cherokee.” Era dates are common approximations and vary slightly between sources. The de Soto route follows the widely used reconstruction described in the New Georgia Encyclopedia (based on Charles Hudson’s work); historians still debate it. The mound volume (about 4 million cubic feet) is a rough model estimate from a truncated pyramid.

Link to China’s curriculum: the national textbook World History, Grade 9 Volume 1, Lesson 15 “Searching for New Sea Routes” covers the Age of Exploration and European expansion; this lesson looks at the same story from the side of the people Europeans met.

Suggested use: remind students that the Muscogee (Creek) and Cherokee nations exist today with their own governments. Have students compare two kinds of evidence — archaeology and the Spanish chronicles — and what each can and can’t tell us.
