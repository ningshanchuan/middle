Evidence cards

A tsar was a Russian emperor. Before the war, the tsar still held broad powers, and the elected assembly had limited power. Heavy wartime losses and shortages weakened his government.

Women workers in Petrograd marched for bread, and strikes spread. Some soldiers ordered to suppress the protests joined them, taking away the force the tsar relied on in the capital.

After the tsar stepped down, the Provisional Government took office and kept Russia in the war. The Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers’ and soldiers’ delegates, also held considerable influence in the capital.

The Bolsheviks and Soviet power

The Bolsheviks were a revolutionary socialist party led by Vladimir Lenin. “Peace, land, bread” spoke to soldiers, farmers, and city residents. The Bolsheviks overthrew the Provisional Government and established Soviet Russia, the world’s first socialist state. They held on to power in the civil war that followed.

Common mix-up: February ended tsarist rule. October ended the Provisional Government. The names follow the Julian calendar Russia used at the time. On today’s Gregorian calendar, the revolutions began in March and November.

October’s influence reaches China

The October Revolution gave communist movements in many countries a real-world example. In China, Li Dazhao and others wrote about the revolution and spread Marxist ideas. The 1919 Paris Peace Conference decision to transfer Germany’s rights in Shandong to Japan sparked the May Fourth Movement.

Around the time of the May Fourth Movement, Chinese Marxists formed study groups and early Communist organizations. They founded the Communist Party of China (CPC) in 1921. Representatives of the Communist International, a group based in Moscow, attended its first congress.

New Economic Policy · Tax in kind

Model rule: pay a two-sack tax, then keep up to six sacks for home use. The rest is available to sell.

Each bar represents the whole harvest. The sack counts are model assumptions. In practice, collections and taxes varied by region, household, and harvest. How much grain reached markets also depended on transport, prices, and what people chose to sell or buy.

Worked example: how much can be sold from a 12-sack harvest?

US-style worked example

Start with 12 sacks. Under the tax rule, subtract 2 sacks for the state and 6 for the household.

The family can sell 12 − 2 − 6 = 4 sacks.

Under requisition, the state takes 12 − 6 = 6 sacks. That leaves 12 − 6 − 6 = 0 sacks to sell.

Chinese-style worked example (Given, Find, Solve, Answer)

Given 12 sacks harvested, a household allowance of 6 sacks, and a tax of 2 sacks.

Find the number of sacks available to sell under each policy.

Solve. The family can sell 12 − 2 − 6 = 4 sacks under the tax and 12 − 6 − 6 = 0 sacks under requisition.

Answer. The family can sell 4 sacks under the tax and 0 under requisition.

During the civil war, the Soviet government requisitioned grain to supply the army and cities, expanded state ownership, and restricted private trade. Years of war devastated the economy. Forced collections weakened incentives to grow extra food and fueled resistance. By 1921, economic recovery was urgent.

In 1921, Lenin introduced the New Economic Policy (NEP). Peasants paid a tax in produce and could sell their surplus. Some private trade and small businesses returned, while the state kept control of major industries. Renewed trade helped the economy recover.

Four republics and a centrally planned economy

On December 30, 1922, the Soviet republics of Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and Transcaucasia formed the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, or USSR.

Under Stalin, central planners set production targets and directed investment. Five-Year Plans began in 1928 and prioritized heavy industry, expanding coal, power, and machine production. Light-industry products such as cotton cloth grew far more slowly.

The state forced many rural families into collective farms. This policy, called collectivization, disrupted farming. Heavy state grain collections worsened the 1932–33 famine in Ukraine and other regions. At the height of the Great Purge in 1937–38, many people were arrested, imprisoned, or executed.

Memory cue: 1922 marks the founding of the USSR. 1928 marks the start of the first plan. Read the output figures alongside evidence about families in the countryside and cities.

How do two historians interpret the same era?

Robert Allen: What drove industrial growth?

Economic historian Allen uses data and a model to assess different policies. He argues that state planning and investment in heavy industry drove rapid industrial growth, and that collectivization added little to overall economic growth.

Allen’s industrialization study

Sheila Fitzpatrick: How did industrialization change city life?

Social historian Fitzpatrick studies how city residents got by under the planned economy. She emphasizes persistent shortages of food, clothing, and other basics, and the daily work of finding those goods.
