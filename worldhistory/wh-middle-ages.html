The Middle Ages & the Islamic Golden Age

In 476 the Western Roman Empire fell. Over the next thousand years Europeans built castles and cathedrals, Constantinople kept Rome’s traditions alive, scholars in Baghdad translated the world’s books into Arabic — and a plague killed about a third of Europe in just a few years. Were these really the “Dark Ages”?

Millions of people; rough historians’ estimates (e.g. McEvedy & Jones, Atlas of World Population History) — studies differ a lot. The Black Death (1347–1351) killed about one-third to one-half of Europe’s people in a few years, often estimated at 25–50 million; the plague returned many times afterwards.

🔗 After the plague: what changed?

Workers became scarce — and valuable

With far fewer peasants left, lords had to offer higher wages and lighter labour duties. Many serfs won their freedom, and the manor system slowly broke down.

Peasant revolts

When rulers tried to freeze wages by law, peasants rebelled — the Jacquerie in France (1358) and Wat Tyler’s Revolt in England (1381).

People questioned old ideas

Nobody then knew the cause. Some people blamed Jewish communities, and many were attacked — a tragedy historians remember. Others began to question old authorities, which helped prepare the way for the Renaissance.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:“Arabic numerals” were invented in India; Arab scholars carried them to Europe, so Europeans called them Arabic. Paper came from China, reaching Samarkand and Baghdad in the 700s and Europe later.
