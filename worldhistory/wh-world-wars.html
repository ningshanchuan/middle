The World Wars: from one gunshot to the atomic age

In June 1914 two shots on a street in Sarajevo pulled every great power in Europe into war within six weeks. Barely 21 years later an even bigger war began — at the Marco Polo Bridge in China and on the border of Poland — and roughly 70 million people or more died. Why did the alliances fall like dominoes? And why did the Western Front barely move for four years?

🎯 Your mission: knock over the 1914 alliance dominoes; as a staff officer, find a way to break the trench stalemate in four battles; drag the timeline and watch World War II’s map change from 1937 to 1945 — including China, the main Eastern battlefield.

🕯️ The Holocaust and the Nanjing Massacre

The Holocaust was Nazi Germany’s planned, state-run genocide: about 6 million Jewish people were murdered, along with millions of others, including Roma, disabled people and political prisoners. Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest death camp, was liberated by the Soviet Red Army in January 1945.

In December 1937, after capturing Nanjing, Japanese troops carried out a massacre lasting about six weeks; China’s official figure and textbooks give more than 300,000 Chinese victims (the Tokyo war-crimes tribunal found over 200,000).

After the war the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials prosecuted war crimes.

The Chinese People’s War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression

China’s resistance began with Japan’s invasion of northeast China in 1931. Full-scale war began in 1937. In Shanghai, refugees waited to leave. The war ended in 1945.

Estimates vary. Historians put Chinese deaths in 1937–1945 at 15–20 million. China’s State Archives reports over 35 million military and civilian casualties over the full period of resistance, including the wounded.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Georgia Standards of Excellence (2016): Grade 6 SS6H3 “Explain conflict and change in Europe to the 21st century” — a. the aftermath of WWI (rise of communism, the Treaty of Versailles, rise of Nazism, worldwide depression), b. the impact of WWII (the Holocaust, origins of the Cold War, rise of superpowers); Grade 7 SS7H2 (breakup of the Ottoman Empire and the Middle East after WWI) and SS7H3c (the US role in rebuilding Japan after WWII). The catalog labels this lesson SS6H6–7 (older GPS numbering); under the 2016 GSE it is SS6H3.

China curriculum: unified-edition World History Grade 9 Vol. 2, Unit 3 (World War I; the Treaty of Versailles and the Nine-Power Treaty) and Unit 4 (fascist aggression; World War II — Stalingrad, D-Day, the Declaration by United Nations, Yalta). The China strip matches Chinese History Grade 8 Vol. 1: the May Fourth Movement (the Shandong question at Paris), the 18 September Incident, the 7 July Incident and the nationwide War of Resistance, Taierzhuang, the Chinese Expeditionary Force, victory. The textbook’s view: China’s War of Resistance was an important part of the World Anti-Fascist War, and China was its main Eastern battlefield. Lesson numbers vary by printing.

Notes: the WWII map is schematic — status is shown by modern country borders, and Japanese-occupied China is a simplified polygon; dates are approximate. Death tolls are mid-points of commonly cited ranges. The trench mission’s outcome model is simplified, but the battle facts (Neuve Chapelle’s ~35-minute bombardment, the Somme’s 7-day bombardment, roughly 400+ tanks at Cambrai, about 11 km gained on the first day at Amiens) follow standard accounts. The Holocaust and the Nanjing Massacre are presented factually and calmly; the US Holocaust Memorial Museum and the Memorial Hall of the Victims in Nanjing Massacre have student resources.
