The Age of Revolutions: science, politics and steam

In 1700 the fastest thing on land was a horse. 150 years later trains roared along at over 30 mph (50 km/h); kings learned that their people could put them on trial or throw them out; and a falling apple helped people calculate the paths of planets. How did all of this happen in one chain?

Steam engine lab: what did Watt actually improve?

First run Newcomen’s engine (1712): every stroke sprays cold water into the cylinder, so it cools and must be heated again — burning coal for nothing. Then switch to Watt’s engine: the steam goes to a separate, always-cold condenser, so the cylinder stays hot. Compare the coal!

Why did Watt’s engine matter so much?

Newcomen engines burned so much coal that they only made sense next to coal mines, pumping out water. Watt’s separate condenser used roughly a quarter of the coal, and after 1781 his sun-and-planet gear turned up-and-down motion into spinning motion that could drive spinning and weaving machines. Factories no longer had to sit beside rivers for water wheels — the “Age of Steam” began (China’s textbook marks 1785, when Watt’s improved engine went into use).

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Georgia standards: there is no single Georgia middle-school standard for this lesson (the catalog labels it “World History”). It gives background for SS6H (Latin American independence, Europe), economics (SS6E/SS7E: economic systems, factors of production) and Grade 8 SS8H3 (the American Revolution in Georgia); see this site’s U.S. History lessons for American details. The Scientific Revolution links to middle-school science (Newton’s laws, the solar system).

China curriculum: unified-edition World History Grade 9 Vol. 1, Unit 6 — constitutional monarchy in Britain (the 1640 English bourgeois revolution, the 1688 Glorious Revolution, the 1689 Bill of Rights), American independence (Lexington, the Declaration of Independence, Saratoga, the 1787 Constitution), the French Revolution and Napoleon’s empire (the Bastille, the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the Civil Code); Unit 7 — the First Industrial Revolution (spinning jenny, Watt’s improved steam engine, Stephenson’s locomotive, the factory system, Britain as the “workshop of the world”). The China strip matches Chinese History Grade 8 Vol. 1: the Macartney embassy (1793), the Opium War (1840) and the Treaty of Nanjing. Lesson numbers vary by printing.

Notes: the map is schematic (modern borders); industrialization dates are rough starting decades. Coal output, railway length and world population are approximate standard estimates. The steam model is simplified for teaching: “shovels” and efficiency show relative size only (Newcomen about 1%, Watt about 2–3%; Watt’s engine is commonly said to use about a quarter of the coal). The Haitian Revolution and Latin American independence are added from standard world-history accounts; China’s Grade 9 Vol. 2 covers them under resistance by colonized peoples.
