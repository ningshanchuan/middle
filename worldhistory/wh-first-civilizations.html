First civilizations: Mesopotamia & Egypt

Why did the world’s first cities all grow up beside rivers? More than 5,000 years ago, between two rivers in today’s Iraq and along the Nile in Egypt, people dug canals, built cities, invented writing — and wrote down laws.
Your mission: drag the time slider and watch 3,000 years of kingdoms and empires rise and fall; choose the site for a new city; work out how many stone blocks the Great Pyramid needed; write your name in hieroglyphs and cuneiform; then take a seat in Hammurabi’s court and judge cases by his Code.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:“Mesopotamia” (Greek for “between the rivers”) is a region — the land between the Euphrates and the Tigris, mostly in today’s Iraq — not a country. Babylon was one kingdom (and city) inside it. Egypt is in north-east Africa; Mesopotamia is in south-west Asia. Pyramids were tombs for pharaohs; Mesopotamia’s stepped towers, ziggurats, were temples.

Who built the pyramids?

Archaeologists have dug up the workers’ town at Giza: bakeries, breweries and the workers’ own tombs. So the builders were mainly organised, fed and paid workers plus farmers serving in shifts — not whipped slaves as in old movies. During the Nile flood, when the fields were under water, lots of farmers were free to work.

Data figure: how tall is a pyramid?

Compare with American landmarks. For about 3,800 years the Great Pyramid was the tallest human-made structure on Earth.

Approximate values: Great Pyramid originally about 146.6 m (today about 138.5 m); Step Pyramid of Djoser about 62.5 m; Ziggurat of Ur originally about 30 m (archaeologists’ estimate); Statue of Liberty with pedestal about 93 m, Washington Monument about 169 m, Georgia State Capitol about 83 m (National Park Service and other public sources).

Scribe school: write your name in ancient scripts

Around 3200 BCE the Sumerians of Mesopotamia pressed cuneiform (“wedge-shaped”) signs into wet clay with a reed; at about the same time the Egyptians invented hieroglyphs. Type your name (English letters) and see.

Main mission: Hammurabi’s court

⚠️ Don’t mix up:“An eye for an eye” applied only between people of the same class. The Code divided people into ranks, and the same injury was punished very differently for a noble, a commoner or an enslaved person — it protected the ruling class. It is the oldest fairly complete written law code to survive, but not the oldest law code: the earlier Code of Ur-Nammu survives only in pieces.
