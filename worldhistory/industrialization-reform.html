The First Industrial Revolution

Drag the slider or tap a date. Trace where the power comes from and what it moves.

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the second half of the 1700s. Much spinning and weaving had taken place in homes or small workshops. The factory system brought workers, machines, and power together under schedules set by owners. Towns such as Manchester grew as more people came to find work.

Common mix-up. Spinning turns fibre into yarn. Weaving interlaces yarn to make cloth. The spinning jenny spun yarn.

How working people lived and organized

What would the law allow in a British cotton mill in late 1836?

Choose an age to see the standard rules for cotton mill workers once the 1833 Factory Act’s age limits had phased in.

Read the evidence · The law and its enforcement
“a longer Time than Forty-eight Hours in any One Week, nor for a longer Time than Nine Hours in any One Day”

This limit extended in stages to children under 13. The Act allowed certain water-powered mills to make up time lost when the water supply failed. There were few inspectors. In an 1836 report, boys told an inspector they had worked from Friday morning into Saturday afternoon with only a few breaks.

1833 Factory Act · Hours quotation in section VIII · UK National Archives · 1836 inspector’s report

At work and at home

Many workers put in long days at a pace set by the machines. Women and children also worked in the mills. Some families lived in crowded housing near factories, sharing water supplies and toilets. Close quarters and poor sanitation helped disease spread.

How did workers act together?

In the early 1800s, textile workers known as Luddites broke machines they saw as a threat to wages and livelihoods. Workers also formed unions to press for better pay and conditions together. Chartists used meetings and petitions to demand political rights.

What did the People’s Charter of 1838 demand?

The charter called for votes for adult men, a secret ballot, and other electoral reforms. People in different towns collected signatures for shared demands. Parliament rejected the petitions at the time, but many demands later became law.

The Second Industrial Revolution: power, communication, and transport

Choose a technology. Follow the diagram to see how it moved energy, carried a voice, or powered a vehicle.

In the late 1800s, electric power, steel, and chemicals became major industries. Steel mills, railways, and power networks cost a lot to build. Some companies grew or merged. When one company came to dominate a market, it could gain a near-monopoly, giving customers few alternatives.

Railways and steamships carried raw materials, goods, and people over long distances. In 1866, a lasting transatlantic telegraph cable began carrying messages across the ocean without waiting for a ship. These connections also helped industrial powers draw raw materials from colonies.

Technologies from both industrial revolutions were used side by side. Coal and steam remained in use as electric power spread.
