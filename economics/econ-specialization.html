Specialization & Trade: Southwest Asia, South and East Asia, and Africa

What do a bouquet of Kenyan roses and a phone assembled in China have in common? Follow the work from fields and factories to ports, and see what each place does well and why they need one another.

Why specialize?

Countries have different resources, equipment and skills, so making everything they need is hard. Each can focus on what it makes at a lower opportunity cost, sell some of it and buy other goods or services.

Two countries, two goods

Harbor and Highland each have six work hours. When making both goods, each spends three hours on each. A bolt is a roll of cloth. Tea is packed in crates. Try switching to specialization and trade.

Challenge yourself to find the amounts that give both countries more of each good than when they make both.

Show the reasoning

Harbor needs at least 7 crates to beat its original 6. Highland needs to keep at least 10 to beat its original 9, so it can trade away at most 18 − 10 = 8 crates. Trading 7 or 8 pairs also gives each country more cloth.

Output = hours worked × units made per hour. Both bars use the same scale.

Opportunity cost: what do you give up?

In an hour Harbor can make four bolts of cloth or two crates of tea. Highland can make one bolt or three crates. One extra bolt costs Harbor half a crate of tea and Highland three crates. Harbor gives up less tea for each bolt.

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up. Here Highland gives up only one-third of a bolt for each extra crate of tea. That’s less than Harbor gives up, so Highland specializes in tea.

Comparative advantage means having a lower opportunity cost. A bolt of Harbor’s cloth costs 2 crates ÷ 4 bolts = ½ crate per bolt. A crate of Highland’s tea costs 1 bolt ÷ 3 crates = ⅓ bolt per crate. At one bolt per crate, each country gives up less to get an extra unit through trade than by making it at home.

How can OPEC affect oil prices?

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) coordinates its members’ oil policies. If demand and other conditions stay the same, cutting output can raise prices and increasing output can lower them. Demand and production elsewhere matter too.

As of November 2026, members in Southwest Asia are Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq and Kuwait. In Africa they are Algeria, Libya, Nigeria, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea and the Republic of the Congo. The UAE left in May 2026.

How does productive capacity grow?

Climate and soil in a tea-growing area are natural resources. Pickers’ skills are human capital. Machines in the tea factory are capital resources. Literacy and training help people operate equipment and track quality. Entrepreneurs bring resources together and take on risk to start businesses.

For example, a car factory in Türkiye can train workers and improve its equipment to make more cars in the same number of hours. Higher productivity can raise real GDP per person and help improve living standards. Income distribution and access to health care and education matter too.

Interdependence means places rely on each other. Buyers need supplies, sellers need markets, and making one product can take work in several countries. Trade barriers affect these links. A tariff adds a tax, a quota limits quantity, and an embargo bans certain trade.

Who decides what gets made?

In a traditional economy, customs guide production. In a command economy, planners direct it. In a market economy, buyers and sellers make decisions through prices. A mixed economy combines markets with government decisions. These groupings are approximate and vary by industry and over time.

  • Command endNorth Korea: central planning dominates, and market trading also exists.
  • Stronger state role in key industriesChina calls its system a socialist market economy. Its policy gives markets a decisive role in how resources are used, alongside government guidance and state companies in key industries. In Saudi Arabia, private businesses operate alongside the country’s main oil producer, which the state controls.
  • Mixed, more market-orientedTurkey, Israel, India, Japan, South Korea, Nigeria, South Africa and Kenya: private firms and prices guide much activity. Governments regulate, provide public services and may own firms.

Compare the systems by asking what to make, how to make it and who gets it. Planners might decide which bread to bake, which ovens to use and which shops receive the loaves. In a market, bakers can decide based on what customers want, what ovens cost and what shoppers will pay.

Traditional practices can coexist with markets. Some herders follow customs passed down across generations and also sell livestock at markets.

Practice · A different trade price

Harbor makes 24 bolts and Highland makes 18 crates. They trade 3 bolts for 6 crates.

How much cloth and tea does each country have after trading?

Show steps

Harbor keeps 24 bolts − 3 bolts = 21 bolts and receives 6 crates. Highland receives 3 bolts and keeps 18 crates − 6 crates = 12 crates.

Harbor ends up with 21 bolts and 6 crates. Highland ends up with 3 bolts and 12 crates. Compared with making both, each country has more of one good and the same amount of the other.
