Ancient Chinese Science: the Four Great Inventions

What do a dollar bill, a library book, the compass app on a phone and Fourth of July fireworks have in common? Each one goes back to something first made in China: paper, printing, the compass and gunpowder. Chinese textbooks call them the Four Great Inventions (四大发明, sì dà fāmíng). They reached Europe through Arab traders, centuries before there was a United States. Spin the south-pointing spoon, make a sheet of paper, print a word yourself, then drag the year slider to watch each invention travel west.

The four inventions in one minute

Read one line on each card, then open it for the dates, the people and the link to your own life.

🧭

Compass

指南针 zhǐnánzhēn · “south-pointing needle”

A magnet that always lines up north–south.

Open
  • Warring States period (475–221 BC): the sinan, a spoon of magnetic stone.
  • Northern Song dynasty (960–1127): sailors begin to steer by a magnetic needle.
  • Compare: Columbus crossed the Atlantic by compass in 1492, about 400 years after Chinese ships used one.
📜

Paper

造纸术 zàozhǐshù · “the craft of making paper”

A light, cheap material to write on.

Open
  • Before paper, people wrote on heavy bamboo strips or costly silk.
  • Paper existed by the Western Han dynasty (202 BC–AD 8). In AD 105, Cai Lun (蔡伦, Cài Lún), an official of the Eastern Han court, improved it using bark, hemp, rags and old fishing nets.
  • Compare: the first paper mill in North America opened near Philadelphia in 1690, almost 1,600 years after Cai Lun.
🖨

Printing

印刷术 yìnshuāshù · “the craft of printing”

Many copies without copying by hand.

Open
  • Tang dynasty (618–907): woodblock printing, a whole page carved on one board. The Diamond Sutra of 868 is the oldest printed book with a clear date.
  • Northern Song: Bi Sheng (毕昇, Bì Shēng) invents movable type, one small clay block per character, reusable again and again.
  • Compare: Gutenberg printed with metal type in Germany about 1450, some 400 years after Bi Sheng. The US Declaration of Independence (1776) was printed with movable type.
🎆

Gunpowder

火药 huǒyào · “fire medicine”

Three powders that burn in a flash.

Open
  • Alchemists looking for a medicine of long life found it by accident; a recipe was written down in the middle of the Tang dynasty.
  • It was used in war by the end of the Tang; in the Song and Yuan dynasties (960–1368) came fire arrows, the bamboo “fire lance” and metal guns.
  • Compare: Fourth of July fireworks, and “the rockets’ red glare” in the US national anthem (1814), both depend on it.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes (curriculum links)

Curriculum: 2022 Compulsory Education History Standards, ancient China, science and culture (in outline): know papermaking, printing, the compass and gunpowder and their contributions to world civilization. Textbook: unified-edition Chinese History, Grade 7 Vol. 1 (Qin–Han: Cai Lun improves papermaking; the sinan of the Warring States) and Vol. 2 (Sui–Tang: woodblock printing; “Song–Yuan science and foreign contact”: Bi Sheng’s movable type, the compass at sea, gunpowder weapons and their spread west).

Suggestions: (1) use the Surface slider to explain why the sinan sits on a smooth bronze plate (less friction, so the spoon can turn freely); (2) when students pick a “normal” block in the game, discuss why type must be carved in mirror image; (3) end the animation with Marx’s assessment and discuss the inventions’ impact on Europe and world history.

About the year slider: the English is written for US middle-schoolers new to Chinese history, so terms carry pinyin and a meaning, with a “meanwhile in the world” line and familiar anchors (Columbus, the Philadelphia paper mill, the Declaration of Independence). The map is schematic, drawn on today’s borders of China, and the routes show only the general direction. Dates the textbook gives (AD 105, 868, Northern and Southern Song, end of the Tang, mid-8th century, 12th century) follow the textbook; the other arrival dates are commonly cited approximations, there to build a sense of time rather than to be memorised. Try having students drag the slider to fill in a table of “what reached where, and when”, then discuss why most of the inventions reached Europe through the Arabs.
