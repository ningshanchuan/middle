One country, many peoples: unity, defense and diplomacy

In 1949 the United States refused to recognise the new People’s Republic of China. In 1972 a US president flew across the Pacific to Beijing. Inside the same country live 56 ethnic groups, five autonomous regions, and two cities that run on a different economic system. How did all of that come about? A “Great Wall of steel”: building modern armed forces

Before 1949 the People’s Liberation Army (PLA, 中国人民解放军) was almost entirely foot soldiers. Then a navy, an air force and a missile force were added. Open each card to see that service’s story.

⚓

The Navy

Founded 23 April 1949 at Baimamiao, Taizhou, Jiangsu Province.

Open
  • It began with a few captured old ships and grew to build its own destroyers and nuclear submarines.
  • 2012: China’s first aircraft carrier, the Liaoning (辽宁舰), entered service.
  • 2019: the Shandong (山东舰), the first carrier built entirely in China, entered service.
  • Compare: the US Navy dates from 1775, so China’s modern navy is very young.
✈️

The Air Force

Founded 11 November 1949.

Open
  • Soon its pilots were flying in the Korean War (which China calls the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea), learning in combat.
  • It moved from copying foreign designs to its own: the J-10 and the stealthy J-20 fighters and the big Y-20 transport plane.
  • The “J” stands for jiān (歼), short for “fighter”.
🚀

The Rocket Force

Formed in 1966 as the “Second Artillery Corps”; renamed the Rocket Force on 31 December 2015.

Open
  • 16 October 1964: China tested its first atomic bomb. In 1966 it tested a nuclear-armed missile and set up a strategic missile force.
  • The plain name “Second Artillery” was chosen to keep it secret.
  • The Rocket Force is the core of China’s strategic deterrence — its job is to make any attack on China too risky to try.
  • The stated goal today: armed forces that obey the Party’s command, can fight and win, and keep excellent conduct — “world-class” by mid-century.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows China’s national History Curriculum Standard for Compulsory Education (2022 edition) and the facts, dates and wording of the national unified-edition textbook Chinese History, Grade 8 Volume 2: Unit 4 “Ethnic Unity and National Reunification” and Unit 5 “National Defense and Diplomatic Achievements” (lesson numbers vary slightly by printing). It presents the topic as students in mainland China learn it, including the textbook’s terms and China’s standard map (with Taiwan Province and the South China Sea islands). The map is schematic.

The English is written for US middle-schoolers new to Chinese history: key terms carry pinyin and a meaning, with familiar anchors (the size of Alaska, the age of the US Navy). A useful discussion: compare how the textbook describes Nixon’s 1972 visit with how a US textbook describes it.

Suggested use: students drag the slider to fill in a table of the five autonomous regions and their founding dates, compare the return dates of Hong Kong and Macao, then use the ordering and sort games to fix the three stages of diplomacy (1950s: opening doors; 1970s: breakthroughs; after 1978: all-round diplomacy).
