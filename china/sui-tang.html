Sui & Tang: a prosperous, open age

In the year 750, what was the biggest city on Earth? Not Rome and not Constantinople: it was Chang’an (长安 Cháng’ān), capital of Tang China, with about a million people, when Paris and London were small towns. The short Sui (隋 Suí) dynasty reunited China, dug a canal longer than the drive from New York to Miami, and invented exams for government jobs. The Tang (唐 Táng), which lasted almost 300 years, built on that to create a golden age open to the world. Explore a 1,400-year-old bridge and the chessboard city of Chang’an, then drag the year slider to dig the canal yourself.

Words you will need (click to open)

Grand Canal 大运河 Dà Yùnhé

A man-made waterway over 2,000 km (1,250 miles) long linking north and south China — the longest canal of the ancient world, more than three times as long as New York’s Erie Canal and 1,200 years older.

Imperial examinations 科举 kējǔ

Government jobs won by passing written tests instead of by being born into the right family. Begun under the Sui, the system lasted about 1,300 years, until 1905.

Golden age 盛世 shèngshì

A time of peace and plenty. The Tang had two famous ones, named after the emperors’ reign titles: Zhenguan (贞观, the years 627–649) and Kaiyuan (开元, the years 713–741).

Tubo 吐蕃 Tǔbō

The kingdom of the ancestors of today’s Tibetans, on the high plateau to the west. A Tang princess married its king in 641.

Envoys to Tang 遣唐使 qiǎn Táng shǐ

Official missions that Japan sent to China, with students and monks, to learn everything from writing to city planning. Japan’s old capitals, Nara and Kyoto, were laid out as copies of Chang’an.

An Lushan Rebellion 安史之乱 Ān–Shǐ zhī luàn

An eight-year civil war (755–763) started by the general An Lushan. The Tang survived it, but never fully recovered.
