China’s seas and islands

Could you steer a cargo ship all the way down China’s coast, from Dalian to Sanya, through three straits, without running aground once? First get to know China’s four seas and its main islands, then see what the ocean gives China — and how to protect it. Your mission: earn the rank of captain.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:China’s inland seas (内海 nèihǎi) are the Bohai Sea and the Qiongzhou Strait — not the Yellow Sea. And the southernmost point of China’s territory is Zengmu Ansha in the Nansha Islands (about 4° N), not Sanya on Hainan Island.

    Ocean resources: fish, oil & gas, salt and ports

    Switch on the “🐟 Resources” and “⚓ Ports” layers on the map above to see where each one is.

    🐟

    Fish and seafood

    Four big fishing grounds: Yellow–Bohai, Zhoushan, South China coast and Beibu Gulf. Zhoushan (舟山渔场) is the biggest.

    More

    The traditional “big four” catches are large yellow croaker, small yellow croaker, hairtail and cuttlefish. China also farms fish, shrimp, shellfish and kelp along its coasts on a huge scale.

    🛢️

    Oil and gas

    The continental shelves of the Bohai, East China and South China Seas hold rich oil and gas. The Bohai has China’s biggest offshore oilfield.

    More

    In the northern South China Sea, engineers now tap gas in deep water: the “Deep Sea No. 1” field, producing since 2021, works in about 1,500 m (almost a mile) of water.

    🧂

    Sea salt

    The Changlu salt fields on the Bohai coast are China’s biggest sea-salt producer. Others include Budai in Taiwan and Yinggehai in Hainan.

    Why there?

    Making salt from seawater needs flat, muddy coasts for big shallow pans, plus lots of sun, little rain and steady wind so the water evaporates fast.

    ⚓

    Ports and shipping

    China’s coast is lined with ports. Shanghai has handled the most containers in the world for years, and Ningbo–Zhoushan moves the most cargo by weight.

    What is a “TEU”?

    Container traffic is counted in TEUs: one twenty-foot (6 m) container = 1 TEU. A big container ship carries over 20,000 TEU.

    Protect the ocean: the summer fishing ban

    Every year from 1 May, China bans most fishing in the Bohai, Yellow and East China Seas and in the South China Sea north of 12° N, for three to four-and-a-half months. Summer is when fish spawn and the young grow. Manage a fishing ground for 20 years: choose the size of the fleet and the length of the ban, and watch the fish and the catch.

    When did the summer ban start?

    China began the summer ban in the Yellow, Bohai and East China Seas in 1995, and in the South China Sea in 1999. It has since been made longer; it now usually starts at noon on 1 May.

    What else protects the sea?

    Marine nature reserves; stopping pollution from the land flowing into the sea; releasing young fish to restock; and fish farming, which takes pressure off wild fish.

    Does the US do this too?

    Yes. NOAA Fisheries sets fishing seasons, catch limits and closed areas for many US fish — for the same reason: give the fish time to breed.

    👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

    This lesson follows the Geography Curriculum Standard for Compulsory Education (2022 edition), “Getting to know China”: territory and seas. It matches the PEP Geography textbook, Grade 8 Volume 1, Chapter 1 “China in the world”, Section 1 “Territory” (the seas China borders, inland seas, main islands, the South China Sea Islands, the southernmost point). Ocean resources and protection (fishing grounds, offshore oil and gas, sea salt, ports, the summer fishing ban) go beyond that section; some textbook editions have a separate lesson on “China’s ocean resources” (check your edition). The map follows China’s standard map, drawn from shared/chinamap.js (including Taiwan Province, the Diaoyu Islands and the South China Sea Islands); neighbouring countries come from world-atlas. It is schematic.

    The depths in the voyage and the fish numbers in the ban simulation are simplified models meant to show patterns, not real values. Port figures are rounded 2023 values; the areas and average depths of the four seas are commonly cited reference values.

    Suggested use: (1) students name the four seas, three straits and main islands from north to south on the map; (2) after the voyage, they list in order the seas and straits the ship passed; (3) use the fishing-ban simulation to discuss sustainable development — catching fish today while keeping enough for tomorrow.
