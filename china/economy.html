China’s Economy: Transport, Farms & Factories

In 2008 China opened its first high-speed rail line. Fifteen years later it had enough high-speed track to circle the Earth at the equator. How do you move 1.4 billion people — and their food, coal and phones — around a country the size of the USA? Drag the year slider and watch the network light up, then find out where the rice, the sheep and the factories are, and why.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports the 2022 Compulsory-Education Geography Curriculum Standard (“Understanding China”): use maps and data to explain the pattern of China’s transport network and how transport shapes the economy and society; explain where farming and industry are found, and why farming must suit local conditions. Textbook: Grade 8 Vol. 1, Ch. 4 China’s Economic Development, §1 Transport, §2 Agriculture, §3 Industry.

Ideas: ① drag the year slider and have students describe how the network grew east first, then west, and why (people, cities, terrain); ② on the Main railways layer find which lines cross at the hubs Zhengzhou, Xuzhou, Zhuzhou and Lanzhou; ③ on the Farming layer connect the 400 mm rainfall line and the Qinling–Huaihe Line to “crops in the east, herds in the west; rice in the south, wheat in the north”; ④ on the Industry layer discuss why factories cluster on the coast, along the Yangtze and along railways; ⑤ use the Map challenge as a quick check. The “8 vertical + 8 horizontal” grid comes from the 2016 national rail plan and is enrichment. For US students, compare with the Interstate Highway System, the Corn Belt and the Great Lakes manufacturing belt.
