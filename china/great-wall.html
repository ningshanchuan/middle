Can dirt really stop an army?

Rammed earth (夯土 hāngtǔ) is damp soil poured between wooden boards and pounded hard, one layer of about 10–20 cm (4–8 in) at a time. Pounded earth gets rock-hard — in the dry northwest, Han walls built this way still stand after 2,000 years.

No soil in the Gobi — what then?

Near Yumen Pass, Han builders stacked a layer of gravel, then a layer of reeds or tamarisk branches, again and again. The reeds work like rebar, holding the gravel together against wind. Use what’s local — 就地取材 (jiù dì qǔ cái).

Why did the Ming switch to brick?

Ming kilns could fire millions of hard bricks, and brick shrugs off rain — important in the wet mountains near Beijing. Many bricks are stamped with the year and the names of the workshop and officials in charge, so bad bricks could be traced!

How did real beacons send messages?

“Smoke by day, fire by night.” The Ming added cannon shots: the more smoke columns and shots, the bigger the raid. A warning could cover hundreds of kilometres in a day — far faster than any rider.

Cross-sections, to scale (approx.)

Ming wall at Badaling: on average about 7.8 m (26 ft) high, 6.5 m (21 ft) wide at the base and 5.8 m (19 ft) across the top (commonly published figures). The Han wall is drawn at a typical surviving size — sections vary a lot. Basketball hoop: 3.05 m (10 ft); school bus: about 3.2 m (10.5 ft) tall.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Curriculum: Grade 7 Book 1, Unit 3 (Qin unification — Meng Tian drives back the Xiongnu; the wall from Lintao to Liaodong) and Book 2, Unit 3 (the Ming wall from the Yalu River to Jiayu Pass; Qi Jiguang). The standard asks students to see the Great Wall as a symbol of the Chinese nation and a marvel of world architecture, and to read maps and pictures as historical evidence.

Added beyond the textbook: the 2009 (Ming) and 2012 (all dynasties) survey figures; Badaling’s dimensions (commonly published); Han gravel-and-reed construction; inscribed Ming bricks; the space myth (eye resolution of about one arcminute is an approximation; Yang Liwei said after his 2003 flight that he did not see the wall, as reported at the time). The numbers in the “hold the pass” and beacon games (labour points, 3 minutes per hop, 8/12 km visibility) are simplified game values, not historical data. Wall routes on the map are schematic.

Suggested use: (1) let students find the cheapest design that still holds, then discuss why Qin–Han used earth and Ming used brick (technology, climate, terrain, money); (2) use the year slider to show the wall was not built by one dynasty at one time; (3) use the space test to practise checking a claim against evidence.
