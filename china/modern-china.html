Modern China: Crisis & Resistance

What would you do if warships sailed halfway around the world to force your country to keep buying an illegal drug? In 1840 that happened to China. Britain started the Opium War, and China — then ruled by the emperors of the Qing dynasty (清朝 Qīng cháo, 1644–1912) — lost. For the next 80 years foreign powers forced one unfair treaty after another on China. How did the Chinese people fight back, and what did they try in order to save their country? Drag the years, click the map and find out.

Words you will need (click to open)

The powers 列强 lièqiáng

The strong industrial countries that attacked or pressured China: mainly Britain, France, Russia, the United States, Japan and Germany.

Unequal treaty 不平等条约 bù píngděng tiáoyuē

A treaty signed at gunpoint. Only one side — China — had to give up land, pay money and open ports, so China lost part of its right to run its own affairs (its sovereignty).

Treaty port 通商口岸 tōngshāng kǒu’àn

A port city that a treaty forced China to open, where foreigners could live and trade. Before 1842 foreign ships were allowed to trade at only one port, Guangzhou (once spelled “Canton”).

Indemnity and the tael 赔款 péikuǎn · 两 liǎng

An indemnity is money the loser of a war is forced to pay the winner. China paid in silver, weighed in taels: one tael (两 liǎng) is about 37 grams, or 1.3 ounces.

Semi-colonial, semi-feudal 半殖民地半封建

“Semi-colonial”: China still had its own government, but foreign powers controlled much of its trade, taxes and territory. “Semi-feudal”: the old system of emperor and landlords was still in place. This is how China’s textbooks describe the country between 1840 and 1949.

Who’s who 人物

Lin Zexu (林则徐 Lín Zéxú): the official who destroyed the opium. Hong Xiuquan (洪秀全): leader of the Taiping uprising. Li Hongzhang (李鸿章): official who built factories and a navy. Empress Dowager Cixi (慈禧 Cíxǐ): the real ruler of the late Qing. Kang Youwei (康有为): leader of the 1898 reform. Sun Yat-sen (孙中山 Sūn Zhōngshān): leader of the 1911 Revolution. Chen Duxiu (陈独秀): founder of New Youth magazine.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports the “Modern and Contemporary Chinese History” strand of the 2022 national compulsory-education history curriculum standard (modern period): foreign aggression and the unequal treaties; China gradually becoming a semi-colonial, semi-feudal society; the Taiping movement, the Self-Strengthening Movement, the Hundred Days’ Reform, the 1911 Revolution and the New Culture Movement. Textbook: national unified edition, Chinese History, Grade 8 Volume 1, Units 1–4 (Lessons 1–12).

Suggestion: play the animated map first and ask students to notice how treaty ports spread from the coast up the Yangtze and from south to north. Then use the 3D staircase to build the “technology → institutions → ideas” logic, and discuss why each attempt failed and what it left behind. The treaty game works well as a class competition.

The English is written for US middle-schoolers meeting Chinese history for the first time: a hook question, pinyin and a plain meaning for each key term, and “meanwhile in the US” anchors (the California Gold Rush, the Civil War, the Spanish–American War). The year-slider map lets students discover for themselves how treaty ports spread from the coast up the Yangtze River and from south to north. The photographs and paintings in the “Real photos” section work well as primary sources: ask who made each image and whose point of view it shows. The lesson presents the period as students in mainland China learn it; maps are schematic.
