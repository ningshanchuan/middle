Beijing: The Capital & Its Central Axis

Why would a city of more than 20 million people be built around one perfectly straight line? This line is 7.8 km (4.8 mi) long, runs right through the emperor’s throne, and has been Beijing’s backbone for more than 700 years. In 2024 it became a World Heritage Site.
Your mission: walk the Central Axis, watch Beijing grow ring by ring, design a courtyard house that stays warm in winter, then plan a one-day trip and earn the rank of Axis Expert.

📐 Why build a city around one line?

The axis shows the ideal order of a Chinese capital: the most important buildings sit on the line, and everything else is arranged symmetrically on both sides. An ancient book, the Kaogongji (考工记), says: “ancestral temple on the left, altar on the right; court in front, market behind.” The emperor sat facing south, so his left (east) got the Ancestral Temple and his right (west) got the Altar of Land and Grain.

🌎 Washington, D.C. has an axis too

The National Mall runs west from the Capitol past the Washington Monument to the Lincoln Memorial: about 3.2 km (2 mi), also a planned city axis. Beijing’s Central Axis is about 2.4 times as long, and more than 500 years older.

🏛️ World Heritage (2024)

In July 2024, “Beijing Central Axis: A Building Ensemble Exhibiting the Ideal Order of the Chinese Capital” joined the World Heritage List as China’s 59th World Heritage Site. It has 15 parts, including the Bell and Drum Towers, Wanning Bridge, Jingshan, the Forbidden City, Tian’anmen, the buildings of Tian’anmen Square, Zhengyangmen, the Temple of Heaven, the Xiannongtan altar and Yongdingmen.

➡️ The line keeps growing

Today the line extends north to the Olympic Green, where the 2008 “Bird’s Nest” stadium and “Water Cube” stand on either side of it, and south toward Beijing Daxing International Airport, opened in 2019.

🗺️ Where is Beijing?

Beijing sits at the northern tip of the North China Plain. Mountains wrap around it on the west, north and northeast, and the plain opens to the southeast, like a bay of flat land, nicknamed the “Beijing Bay”. Close to the Bohai Sea, it is the crossroads between Northeast China, North China and the Northwest.

Mission 2 · Design a courtyard house that’s warm in winter

A siheyuan (四合院 sìhéyuàn) is a traditional Beijing home: four buildings around a courtyard. The lanes between rows of them are hutongs (胡同 hútòng).     Beijing has a temperate monsoon climate. In winter, cold dry winds blow from the northwest (Siberia and Mongolia), so winters are cold and very dry. In summer, warm wet winds blow in from the ocean, so summers are hot and rainy: June–August bring about three-quarters of the year’s rain. Washington’s rain is spread fairly evenly through the year.

    💡 Memory hints

    Axis order (south → north)Yongdingmen; Temple of Heaven and Xiannongtan on either side; Zhengyangmen, the Square, Tian’anmen, the Forbidden City; Jingshan, Wanning Bridge, Drum and Bell: 7.8 km on one line.
    Four city functionsPolitics, culture, international exchange, science and tech innovation.
    ⚠️ Common mix-up:“Temple on the left, altar on the right” is from the emperor’s view, sitting in the north facing south. So the Ancestral Temple is on the EAST side, which is on the RIGHT of a north-up map!
    ⚠️ Common mix-up:Beijing and Washington are at similar latitudes, but Beijing’s winter is colder and drier and its rain is packed into summer. That is the monsoon at work, not a much higher latitude.
    👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

    Supports the 2022 Compulsory-Education Geography Curriculum Standard (“Understanding China”): use maps to describe the location of the capital, Beijing, and explain its city functions; use sources to describe how the city has changed and how its heritage is protected. Textbook: PEP Geography Grade 8 Vol. 2, Chapter 6 “The Northern Region”, Section 4 “The Capital of the Motherland: Beijing” (section numbering varies by edition).

    Suggested flow: ① walk the axis in Mission 1 and have students name the symmetric pairs (“temple left, altar right”); ② use the city slider to compare Yuan Dadu, Ming–Qing Beijing and the ring roads; ③ in the courtyard mission, explain “sitting north, facing south” with the noon sun height at the winter and summer solstices, then link it to the winter monsoon; ④ compare the climate charts with Washington to show that heat and rain arrive together in a monsoon climate; ⑤ set the day-trip mission as homework and discuss the trade-offs.

    Beyond the textbook (for reference): the dates, heights and distances of the axis buildings; ring road lengths and completion years (2nd ≈ 32.7 km, 1992; 3rd ≈ 48 km, 1994; 4th ≈ 65 km, 2001; 5th ≈ 98 km, 2003; 6th ≈ 188 km, 2009); the city-growth map is schematic; the courtyard sunlight uses latitude 39.9°N and a simplified 0.9 m eave overhang; opening times, visit lengths and travel times in the day trip are game settings. The Forbidden City and the National Museum close on Mondays (except public holidays).
