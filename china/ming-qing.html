Chinese History · Grade 7 (Vol. 2) Unit 3

Ming & Qing (to 1840): a unified multi-ethnic state grows stronger

In 1405 a fleet with more than 20,000 sailors left China — 87 years before Columbus crossed the Atlantic, and on far bigger ships. Where did they go? And what happened to China over the next 400 years? Sail the route yourself, build the emperors’ palace in 3D, then drag the year slider from 1368 to 1840 and watch two dynasties, the Ming and the Qing, shape the China on today’s map.

Ming dynasty明朝 Míng cháo · 1368–1644

A dynasty is a line of rulers from one family. The Ming (“bright”) emperors, the Zhu family, ruled China for 276 years. When it began, Europe was still in the Middle Ages.

Qing dynasty清朝 Qīng cháo · 1644–1912

China’s last dynasty, founded by the Manchu people from the northeast. “Qing” sounds like “ching”. This lesson stops in 1840, just before the Opium War.

Four emperors to knowYongle · Kangxi · Yongzheng · Qianlong

Chinese emperors are usually called by the name of their reign. Yongle (永乐 Yǒnglè, ruled 1402–1424) of the Ming sent Zheng He to sea and built the Forbidden City. Kangxi (康熙 Kāngxī, 1661–1722), Yongzheng (雍正 Yōngzhèng, 1722–1735) and Qianlong (乾隆 Qiánlóng, 1735–1796) of the Qing secured the borders. George Washington was US president during Qianlong’s last years.

Zheng He & the “Western Oceans”郑和 Zhèng Hé · 西洋 Xīyáng

Zheng He (say “jung huh”) was a Ming admiral and envoy. The “Western Oceans” meant the seas west of the South China Sea: today’s Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean, not Europe.

Forbidden City紫禁城 Zǐjìnchéng

The emperors’ palace in the middle of Beijing; “forbidden” because ordinary people could not enter. It covers about 72 hectares (178 acres), roughly 135 American football fields.

Two big ideas统一多民族国家 · 闭关锁国

“Unified multi-ethnic state” (tǒngyī duō mínzú guójiā): many peoples living in one united country, the main thread of this unit. “Closed-door policy” (bì guān suǒ guó, “shut the passes, lock the country”): the Qing rule that tightly limited contact and trade with other countries.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes (curriculum links)

Curriculum: 2022 Compulsory Education History Standards, ancient China, “Ming and Qing (before the Opium War)” (in outline): stronger autocracy; Ming foreign relations (Zheng He, Qi Jiguang); consolidation of the unified multi-ethnic state (Taiwan, Yaksa, the Dzungars, Tibet); the closed-door policy; architecture such as the Forbidden City. Textbook: unified-edition Chinese History, Grade 7 Vol. 2, Unit 3 (lessons such as “Ming rule”, “Ming foreign relations”, “Ming science, architecture and literature”, “Consolidating the unified multi-ethnic state”, “Stronger Qing autocracy”).

Suggestions: (1) after the route game, discuss the conditions (a strong state, advanced shipbuilding and navigation, Zheng He’s ability) and significance (exchange with Asia and Africa; a feat in world navigation, more than half a century before the European voyages); (2) use the Build slider to discuss the palace and the move of the capital; (3) after the map, have students sort Qing measures by region: southeast, northeast, northwest, southwest.

For US classrooms: the English is written for students with no background in Chinese history. Chinese terms carry pinyin and a meaning on first use, with “meanwhile in the world” anchors (Columbus 1492, the Mayflower 1620, the Declaration of Independence 1776) and miles/feet next to metric units. How far each of Zheng He’s voyages reached, the world-history anchors and the unit conversions on the year slider go beyond the Chinese textbook and are added for context. All maps are schematic. A good discussion: why does the textbook stress that Zheng He’s voyages were peaceful, and how does that compare with what US textbooks say about European exploration?
