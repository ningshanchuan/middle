Ancient Chinese Science: Quakes, Stars & Numbers

In the year 138, a bronze dragon in the capital, Luoyang, spat out a ball with a clang. Nobody in the city felt a thing. Days later a messenger galloped in: an earthquake had struck Longxi (today: Gansu), hundreds of kilometres to the west. The machine was Zhang Heng’s seismoscope. Today you work like an ancient Chinese scientist: catch quakes, chase π, calculate on an abacus and track the Sun through the year.

Your mission: clear 4 labs, win 12 stars and rise from Pupil (学童) to Grand Astronomer (太史令), Zhang Heng’s real job title.

Words you will need (click to open)

Seismoscope 地动仪 dìdòngyí

Built by Zhang Heng (张衡) of the Eastern Han in 132. It showed which direction an earthquake came from.

Pi 圆周率 yuánzhōulǜ

How many times the diameter fits around a circle: about 3.14159… Zu Chongzhi (祖冲之) pinned it down to 7 decimal places around the year 480.

Cutting the circle 割圆术 gēyuánshù

Liu Hui’s method (3rd century): draw polygons with more and more sides inside a circle until they hug the circle.

Abacus 算盘 suànpán · 珠算 zhūsuàn

A bead calculator. Each upper bead is worth 5, each lower bead 1. Abacus calculation is on UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

Armillary sphere 浑仪 húnyí · 简仪 jiǎnyí

A skeleton globe of rings for measuring where stars and the Sun are. In the Yuan, Guo Shoujing (郭守敬) redesigned it as the simpler “simplified armillary”.

24 solar terms 二十四节气 èrshísì jiéqì

The year split into 24 parts by the Sun’s position, a farmer’s calendar. On UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list since 2016.

Lab 1

Zhang Heng’s seismoscope: which way did the quake come from?

The history book of the Later Han says: the seismoscope was cast in fine bronze, shaped like a wine jar, with eight dragons outside, each holding a bronze ball, and eight open-mouthed toads below. When an earthquake struck, the dragon facing its direction dropped its ball into a toad’s mouth. Trigger quakes yourself, then take the Station Master challenge.

Could it predict earthquakes?

No. It was a seismoscope: after a quake happened, it showed which direction it came from. It could not warn ahead of time.

What was really inside?

The original is lost. The history book only says “a central pillar inside, with eight channels to the sides”. Every model you see is a modern reconstruction: Wang Zhenduo’s 1951 model uses an upright pillar; a 2005 Chinese team used a hanging pendulum. The textbook invites you to compare reconstructions and think about how it worked.

The quake of 138

The record says a dragon once dropped its ball while nobody in Luoyang felt anything, and scholars doubted the machine. Days later a messenger arrived: there had been an earthquake in Longxi (in today’s Gansu). After that, everyone admired its cleverness.

Lab 2

Chase π like Zu Chongzhi

Draw a hexagon inside a circle, then keep doubling the sides: 12, 24, 48… The polygon’s perimeter creeps up on the circle’s. A polygon inside gives a value below π; one outside gives a value above. π is trapped in between.

    Fraction hunter: find Zu Chongzhi’s “close ratio”

    What exactly did Zu Chongzhi find?

    He showed π lies between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927, and gave two fractions: 22/7 (the “rough ratio”) and 355/113 (the “close ratio”). His book is lost; historians think he may have worked up to a 24,576-sided polygon, which is what this demo does.

    How far ahead was he?

    The textbook says this led the world by nearly 1,000 years. Around 1424, al-Kāshī in Samarkand went further; Europeans rediscovered 355/113 only in the 1500s (approximate dates).

    How, with no calculator?

    With counting rods (算筹 suànchóu): little bamboo sticks laid out on a table to form numbers. It meant taking square roots again and again to many digits. A staggering amount of work.

    Lab 3

    The abacus: ancient China’s calculator

    Beads above the bar are worth 5 each; beads below are worth 1. A bead counts only when pushed against the bar. Each rod is one place: ones on the right, then tens, hundreds… Click beads to try it, then take the timed challenge!

    Abacus rhyme: how do you add 3?

    If you run out of lower beads, use “三下五去二”: push down an upper bead (+5) and take away two lower beads (−2). 5 − 2 = 3. The Chinese idiom “three down, five off two” comes from this and means getting a job done quickly.

    Lab 4

    Armillary sphere and the 24 solar terms: follow the Sun for a year

    The rings of an armillary sphere are a skeleton of the sky: horizon, meridian, celestial equator and the Sun’s path (the ecliptic). Drag the time and the sky turns around the North Pole; drag the date and the Sun walks around the ecliptic. Every 15° it walks is one solar term.

    春雨惊春清谷天，
    夏满芒夏暑相连，
    秋处露秋寒霜降，
    冬雪雪冬小大寒。

    Each line takes one character from each term, in order through spring, summer, autumn and winter. Chinese kids learn this song to remember all 24.

    Guo Shoujing’s calendar

    In the Yuan dynasty, Guo Shoujing and others made the Shoushi Calendar with a year of 365.2425 days, the same as today’s Gregorian calendar, but about 300 years earlier.

    On the seismoscope: the page follows the textbook and the Later Han biography of Zhang Heng (“the dragon facing the quake drops its ball”); the internal mechanism is a modern reconstruction (Wang Zhenduo’s 1951 upright-pillar model; the 2005 suspended-pendulum model by a team of Chinese seismologists) and is still debated, matching the textbook activity asking students to compare reconstructions.

    Beyond the textbook (optional): Liu Hui’s method and the 3,072-gon, the 24,576-gon hypothesis, al-Kāshī (c. 1424), 355/113 in 16th-century Europe, abacus history and the “三下五去二” rule, the structure of the simplified armillary, solar longitudes of the terms and day-length maths, and the world comparison dates. All timeline dates are approximate.

    Suggestions: (1) after the seismoscope game, discuss detecting vs predicting; (2) in the π lab, have students predict how many more correct digits one more doubling gives; (3) after the abacus, contrast counting rods with the abacus (Zu Chongzhi used rods); (4) use the wheel to link Sun position → season → farm work, and ask which term today falls in.
