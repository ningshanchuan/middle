Silk Road Explorers & Traders

No maps you could trust, no GPS — just camels, sailing ships and your own two feet. How did people cross deserts, frozen mountains and whole oceans, carrying silk, holy books and even a giraffe thousands of miles?

Your mission: follow Zhang Qian, Xuanzang, Jianzhen, Zheng He and Marco Polo to collect all five passport stamps; then lead your own camel caravan out of Chang’an to trade, and size up Zheng He’s treasure ship against Columbus’s — and rise from Apprentice to Silk Road Legend.

Mission 2: run a caravan to Samarkand

It’s the Tang dynasty and you are a merchant in Chang’an. With 200 silver coins, buy goods, camels and water, travel west to Samarkand — and back if you dare. Every city has different prices: buy low, sell high! Watch out for sandstorms, bandits and running dry. Score = profit + a bonus for arriving safely.

Did any merchant really walk all the way from Chang’an to Rome?

Hardly ever. The Silk Road worked like a relay race: goods changed hands from city to city, and each stretch was run by different traders. In the Tang dynasty the busiest Silk Road merchants were the Sogdians, from the Samarkand area — many of them lived in Chang’an.

What was a “guosuo” (过所)?

A Tang-dynasty travel permit from the government, listing the people, animals and goods in the group. Guards checked it at every pass. Real Tang permits have been dug up near Turpan.

Mission 3: Zheng He’s treasure ship vs Columbus’s Santa María

Zheng He’s first voyage set sail in 1405; 87 years later Columbus crossed the Atlantic with three small ships. Put the two flagships side by side — and find out why historians still argue about how big the treasure ships really were.

🎯 Guess: how many Santa Marías long is the treasure ship?

Data: treasure ship from the Ming History (“44 zhang long, 18 zhang wide”), converted with a Ming foot of about 0.31 m (results vary with the foot used); the smaller estimate comes from some historians of shipbuilding. No plans of the Santa María survive, so its size is an estimate too.

Why do some historians doubt the giant size?

Wood can only be so strong: a wooden ship over 100 m long would bend (and could break) in big waves, and a 2.5-to-1 length-to-width shape is unusually fat. So some scholars think the biggest treasure ships were more like 60–75 m long; others defend the old records. Either way, they dwarf Columbus’s ship.

Mission 4: what went west, what came east?

The Silk Roads carried more than goods: plants, animals, inventions, music and ideas all travelled too.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

A cross-unit deep dive based on China’s History Curriculum Standard for Compulsory Education (2022) and the unified-edition Chinese History textbooks: Grade 7 Vol. 1, the lesson on the Silk Road (Zhang Qian, the Silk Road); Grade 7 Vol. 2, cultural exchange in the Tang (Xuanzang, Jianzhen), the Yuan lessons (Marco Polo) and Ming foreign relations (Zheng He). Lesson numbers vary by printing.

Added beyond the textbook (please treat as enrichment): detailed routes, stops and dates of each journey (from the Shiji, the biography of Xuanzang, the account of Jianzhen’s voyages and Marco Polo’s book — all schematic); route distances (great-circle sums along the schematic routes, approximate); the reason each of Jianzhen’s attempts failed; prices and events in the caravan game (an invented teaching model); conversion of the treasure-ship size and the scholarly debate; the estimated size of the Santa María. Maps are schematic: China uses the standard map (shared/chinamap.js); world coastlines come from Natural Earth 110m.

Suggested use: have students fill in a table comparing the five travellers (purpose – route – hardships – results); use the caravan game to discuss why silk was so expensive by the time it reached the West; use the ship comparison to discuss what historians do when written records and engineering evidence disagree.
