China’s Four Geographic Regions

Why do people in northern China eat noodles and dumplings, while southerners eat rice at almost every meal? The answer is geography. Chinese geographers split the country into four big regions: the North, the South, the dry Northwest and the high, cold Qinghai–Tibet region. The four regions side by side

Different land and weather mean different farms, food, houses and ways to travel. Chinese has a phrase for it: 因地制宜 (yīn dì zhì yí), “suit what you do to the place”.

⚠️ Common mistake:The North–South boundary is the Qinling–Huaihe line (a mountain range plus a river), not the Yangtze and not the Yellow River. The Northwest is the largest region, but the Qinghai–Tibet region has the fewest people.

📏 Who decided where the regions end?

Nature did. North vs South: the Qinling Mountains (秦岭 Qínlǐng) and the Huai River (淮河 Huái Hé), roughly where January averages 0 °C (32 °F) and a year brings 800 mm (31 in) of rain. North vs Northwest: the 400 mm (16 in) rain line. Less than that and farming needs irrigation, much like the 100th meridian that separates farm country from ranch country in the US. Qinghai–Tibet vs the rest: the 3,000 m (9,800 ft) height line, the edge of the Tibetan Plateau.

🌎 What would these feel like in the US?

Beijing’s winter is about as cold as Chicago’s, but its summer is as sticky as Washington, D.C.’s. Guangzhou feels like Florida: mild winters, soaking summers. Ürümqi, deep inside Asia and far from any ocean, has winters like North Dakota and very little rain. Lhasa sits at about 3,650 m (12,000 ft), more than twice as high as Denver, so even July averages only about 16 °C (61 °F), with fierce sunshine.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports the 2022 Compulsory-Education Geography Curriculum Standard (“Understanding China”): locate the four geographic regions on a map, compare their main natural and human differences, and use data to explain how natural factors shape production and daily life. Textbook: Grade 8 Vol. 2, Ch. 5 Geographic Differences; Ch. 6 Northern Region (Northeast, Loess Plateau, Beijing); Ch. 7 Southern Region (Yangtze Delta, Hong Kong & Macao, Taiwan, Pearl River Delta); Ch. 8 Northwest (Tarim Basin); Ch. 9 Qinghai–Tibet (Sanjiangyuan, Lhasa). Chapter order varies slightly between editions.

Ideas: ① show the boundaries and ask what each line means (800 mm/0 °C, 400 mm, 3000 m); ② use the focus buttons region by region to fill in a comparison table; ③ play “Which region?” in teams and discuss why after each answer.
