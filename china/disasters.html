Natural Hazards: Quakes, Typhoons & Sandstorms

China is huge, and its natural hazards are many, widespread and frequent. Nobody can stop an earthquake or turn a typhoon around — but forecasting, stronger buildings and walls of trees can make hazards far less harmful. Can you do it?

Your mission: design quake-proof buildings, run the warnings for a whole typhoon season, read the floods and droughts, then plant a Great Green Wall. Rise from Safety Cadet to Disaster-Response Chief!

Earthquakes: where — and why?

China sits on the southeast of the Eurasian Plate, squeezed by the Indian (Indian Ocean) Plate from the south and the Pacific Plate from the east. That makes it one of the most earthquake-prone countries. Drag the magnitude slider to see how fast the energy grows.

Magnitude vs intensity?

Magnitude (震级) measures the energy a quake releases — one number per quake. Intensity (烈度) measures how hard the shaking hits a particular place — usually highest near the epicentre. The Wenchuan quake was magnitude 8.0; near its epicentre the intensity reached XI on China’s 12-level scale.

The 2008 Wenchuan earthquake: disaster, rescue and rebuilding

Open each card. This is a sad chapter of history — read it with respect.

12 May 2008 · 14:28The ground shakes

A magnitude-8.0 earthquake struck Wenchuan County, Sichuan, with its epicentre at Yingxiu (映秀). It was the most destructive and widespread earthquake since the founding of the People’s Republic, and the hardest to respond to. Nearly 70,000 people lost their lives.

Why here?The Longmen Shan fault

As the Indian Plate pushes north, the Tibetan Plateau is squeezed east. Where the plateau’s edge meets the Sichuan Basin, along the Longmen Shan (龙门山) mountains, the rock suddenly broke and slipped.

Hours and days afterRescue

Soldiers, firefighters, medical teams and huge numbers of volunteers came from all over the country, crossing mountains on foot when roads were cut, racing to save lives. A saying sums it up: “When one place is in trouble, help comes from all directions.”

19–21 May 2008National mourning

China held three days of national mourning. Flags flew at half-mast, and at 14:28 on 19 May the whole country stood in silence for three minutes.

The following yearsPaired help and rebuilding

Provinces and cities were each paired with a damaged county to help rebuild homes, schools and hospitals, and new schools were built to stricter earthquake standards. Beichuan town was rebuilt at a new site; the ruins of the old town were kept as a memorial.

Since 200912 May: Disaster Prevention Day

Every 12 May is now China’s National Disaster Prevention and Reduction Day. Around that date, schools across the country practise earthquake drills — the same drill you’re about to do below.

    Mission: run the typhoon warning desk for a season

    Typhoons form over the warm western Pacific and head northwest. Where they go depends mostly on an invisible giant: the subtropical high (副热带高压). Strong high → the storm keeps going west; weak high → it curves north early. ① Steer your forecast so the dashed path matches the observed track. ② Use the forecast winds to issue blue, yellow, orange or red warnings to coastal areas. ③ Evacuate in time when it’s red. Too little warning leaves people in danger; too much wastes money and effort.

    Mission: plant the Great Green Wall

    Every spring, northwest winds lift dust from deserts and worn-out grasslands and blow it toward North China. In 1978 China launched the Three-North Shelter Forest Program (三北防护林 — northwest, north and northeast), planned to run until 2050. Plant trees, shrubs and straw checkerboards on the schematic below to cut Beijing’s dust days. Remember the rule: trees where trees can live, shrubs where shrubs can, grass where only grass can — too little rain and a tree will die! Goal: 8 dust days or fewer by 2050. Careful: if overgrazing goes on, the grasslands wear out too.

    👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

    Supports the 2022 Compulsory-Education Geography Standard (“Understanding China”): use data to name China’s main natural hazards and their distribution; explain the main prevention and mitigation measures with examples; learn basic self-protection; use plate tectonics to explain where earthquakes happen. Textbook: PEP Grade 8 Vol. 1, Ch. 2 §4 Natural Hazards; Grade 7 Vol. 1, The Movement of Plates; Grade 8 Vol. 2, Northwest China (desertification and the Three-North Shelter Forest).

    Beyond the textbook or simplified: the shake table, typhoon tracks, flood/drought and shelter-belt sims are teaching models, not measurements; warning levels follow the CMA warning-signal wind thresholds in simplified form (average wind only); the hazard calendar is approximate; the Wenchuan section follows the textbook and adds the Longmen Shan fault, the national mourning days, paired assistance and Disaster Prevention Day.

    Ideas: ① let students change only the floor count first to discover resonance; ② in the typhoon game, ask how the track changes with a strong or weak subtropical high; ③ discuss the warning dilemma — too little vs too much; ④ for desertification, stress “the right plant for the place” and human causes (overgrazing, over-cultivation). When discussing Wenchuan, be mindful of students’ feelings and focus on rescue, rebuilding and self-protection.
