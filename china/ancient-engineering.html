Ancient Engineering: Bridges, Canals & Palaces

How does a stone bridge survive 1,400 years of floods and earthquakes? How does a canal lift a grain boat about 40 m (130 ft) up and over hills? And why does a wooden palace held together without nails sway in an earthquake but not fall? No manual today: you are the engineer. Load it, run it, shake it.

Your mission: finish 5 engineering missions, win stars and rise from Apprentice (学徒) to Grand Master Builder (大匠).

Words you will need (click to open)

Zhaozhou Bridge 赵州桥 Zhàozhōu Qiáo

Designed by the Sui craftsman Li Chun (李春) about 1,400 years ago in today’s Hebei. The oldest surviving open-spandrel stone arch bridge in the world.

Open spandrel 敞肩拱 chǎngjiān gǒng

“Open shoulders”: instead of filling the shoulders of the big arch with solid stone, you open small arches in them.

Canal lock 船闸 chuánzhá

A box of water between two gates. Fill it and the boat rises; drain it and the boat sinks down. A water elevator.

Grain shipping 漕运 cáoyùn

Moving tax grain from the rich south to the capital by water. For centuries the Grand Canal’s main job.

Mortise and tenon 榫卯 sǔnmǎo

A peg (tenon, 榫) on one timber slots into a hole (mortise, 卯) in another. No nails, no glue.

Dougong brackets 斗拱 dǒugǒng

Stacks of wooden blocks (斗 dǒu) and arms (拱 gǒng) on top of a column. They hold the wide roof eaves out and pass the weight down to the column.

    Why can a stone arch carry so much?

    Stone cracks easily when stretched but is incredibly strong when squeezed. An arch turns the weight into a squeeze that runs along the ring down into the banks. As long as that force line stays inside the ring, every stone is pressed tight.

    Why does an arch push the banks apart?

    The feet of an arch push down and also outward (the horizontal thrust). The flatter the arch, the harder it pushes. A taller arch pushes less but makes the road steep. Li Chun chose a flat arch for an easy road, then made it lighter with small arches to cut the push.

    How big is the real bridge?

    About 50.82 m (167 ft) long, with a main span of 37.02 m (121 ft), a rise of about 7.23 m (24 ft) and about 9 m (30 ft) wide. The span is about three US school buses parked end to end.

    Missions 2 · 3

    Canal locks: an elevator made of water

    A canal that crosses high ground needs a way to lift boats. The answer is the lock (船闸). Each gate has a small sluice (valve): open the valve to level the water first, and only then can the gate swing open.

    Get the grain from Hangzhou to the capital, Beijing, before the canal freezes! 1 second = 1 day and you have 180 days. First choose your load:

    Why can’t you just push the gate open?

    When the water is a few metres higher on one side, it presses on the gate with hundreds of tonnes of force. No crew could move it. Once the valve levels the water, the pushes cancel and the gate swings easily.

    China’s first locks

    The Qin dynasty’s Lingqu Canal used single water gates (陡门 dǒumén). In 984, under the Song, the official Qiao Weiyue built a lock with two gates near today’s Huai’an. It is often called one of the world’s first true locks, roughly 400 years before Europe’s (approximate).

    The canal’s rooftop: Nanwang

    The highest stretch of the Beijing–Hangzhou Grand Canal is near Nanwang in Shandong (about 40 m above sea level, approximate). Ming engineers dammed the Wen River and led it here, splitting it to flow both north and south to feed the locks.

    Why is “soft” safer?

    A stiff frame takes the full kick of the quake and its joints snap. Wooden joints can turn a little and rub, turning the quake’s energy into heat. Layer upon layer of dougong works a bit like a car’s shock absorbers.

    Why aren’t the columns buried?

    The columns stand on stone bases (柱础 zhùchǔ) instead of being sunk into the ground. In a quake the column feet can rock and slide a little instead of being yanked along by the ground.

    How big is the Hall of Supreme Harmony?

    It is the largest hall in the Forbidden City: about 26.92 m tall, about 35.05 m (115 ft) with its stone terrace, covering about 2,377 m² (approximate). The whole palace covers about 720,000 m², roughly 135 American football fields.

    Standards and textbook: 2022 national History Curriculum Standard (compulsory education), ancient China: achievements in science, technology and culture; national unified textbook Grade 7 Vol. 2, Lesson 1 (Sui: Grand Canal, Zhaozhou Bridge, Li Chun), the Song–Yuan lessons (Yuan: Beijing–Hangzhou Grand Canal) and the Ming lesson on science, architecture and literature (Beijing and the Forbidden City).

    Beyond the textbook (optional): the bridge’s ~700 t weight saving, ~16.5% extra flood area and ~2,800 t self-weight are commonly published approximations; Qiao Weiyue’s 984 lock, the Nanwang water divide, the Tonghui River, the Yingxian Pagoda, the Lugou Bridge dimensions and the 1679 Sanhe–Pinggu earthquake are enrichment. The bridge force line, lock water pressure and earthquake sims are simplified illustrative models for understanding principles; their numbers are not engineering calculations.

    Suggestions: (1) let students “break” the bridge first (solid shoulders, flat arch, thin ring), then have them name Li Chun’s three gains: less stone, less weight, better flood flow; (2) after the lock mission, have students retell “open valve → level water → open gate → move boat → close gate”; (3) after the quake sim, discuss stiff vs flexible and link to joints, brackets and stone bases; (4) use the timeline for “time → dynasty → achievement” reading practice.
