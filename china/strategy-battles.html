Strategy & Famous Battles

How does a small army beat one ten times its size? Not with sharper swords — with sharper thinking: terrain, food supplies, timing and whether the soldiers actually want to fight.

Your mission: learn eight big ideas from Sunzi’s Art of War, make the commander’s call in five textbook battles, cock a Qin crossbow and inspect the Terracotta Army — and climb from Soldier to Marshal.

Mission 1: crack the Art of War

The Art of War (《孙子兵法》 Sūnzǐ Bīngfǎ) was written by Sun Wu (孙武), a general at the end of the Spring and Autumn period — about 2,500 years ago, older than the Roman Republic’s big wars. People around the world (sports coaches and business leaders too) still read it. Tap a card to see what it means and which battle used it.

Are Sun Wu and Sun Bin the same person?

No. Sun Wu lived at the end of the Spring and Autumn period and wrote the Art of War. Sun Bin (孙膑) lived in the Warring States period, about 150 years later — said to be his descendant — and came up with “besiege Wei to rescue Zhao”. In 1972 bamboo books of both men’s military writings were dug up together in a Han tomb at Yinqueshan, Shandong Province.

📊 The battles in numbers: army sizes and results

⚠️ Don’t mix up:“Borrowing arrows with straw boats” and Zhuge Liang “borrowing the east wind” come from the novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms (written over 1,000 years later), not from the history book Records of the Three Kingdoms. Mission 3: cock the Qin crossbow

The Terracotta Army pits held hundreds of bronze crossbow triggers (弩机 nǔjī). A crossbow can be drawn, held and aimed before you shoot — so an ordinary farmer-soldier could learn to hit hard and far quickly. Work the trigger step by step, then set the draw weight and angle to hit the straw target.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This is a cross-unit deep dive based on China’s History Curriculum Standard for Compulsory Education (2022) and the unified-edition textbook Chinese History, Grade 7 Volume 1: Unit 2 (the change from Xia to Shang to Zhou; the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods; the Hundred Schools), Unit 3 (Qin unifies China) and Unit 4 (the Three Kingdoms; the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties). Lesson numbers vary by printing.

Added beyond the textbook (please treat as enrichment): march routes and decision details for each battle; army sizes (from the Shiji, Sanguozhi and Jin Shu, labelled “traditional figures”); the chapter sources of the Sunzi quotations; numbers and sizes of the Terracotta pits (approximate museum figures); crossbow speed and range (a simple physics model, not measurements); the layout of Pit 2 and the “how it fights” drill (an interpretation). The commander’s choices are teaching scenarios. All maps are schematic and drawn on China’s standard map (shared/chinamap.js); today’s province lines are only for orientation.

Suggested use: start with the quick challenge, then let groups take one battle each and compare why the smaller side won (timing, terrain, supplies, morale, alliances). Finish with the table: why might ancient histories exaggerate army sizes?
