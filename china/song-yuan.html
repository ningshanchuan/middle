Liao, Song, Xia, Jin & Yuan: peoples, trade and change

A thousand years ago — about 500 years before Columbus — who was already paying with paper money, and whose ships could hit a rock and still not sink? The answer to both is Song-dynasty China (宋 Sòng, 960–1276). In these four centuries China was split among rival states, its richest region shifted from north to south, sea trade boomed, and finally the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty put the country back together. Board a Song ship first, then drag the year slider to watch the states rise and fall.

Dynasty 朝代 cháodài

A line of rulers from one family, and the period they ruled. In this lesson several states exist side by side — imagine North America split among four or five rival countries.

Song 宋 Sòng · say “soong”

Two halves: the Northern Song (960–1127, capital Kaifeng) and, after the north was lost, the Southern Song (1127–1279, capital Lin’an, today’s Hangzhou).

Liao, Xia, Jin 辽 Liáo · 夏 Xià · 金 Jīn

Three northern states founded by other peoples: the Khitan (Liao), the Tangut (Western Xia) and the Jurchen (Jin). They fought the Song, traded with it, and borrowed from each other.

Yuan 元 Yuán · say “yoo-EN”

Founded by the Mongol ruler Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan. In 1279 it reunited the whole country — a territory far bigger than today’s lower 48 US states.

Jiaozi 交子 jiāozǐ · “jyow-dzuh”

The world’s first paper money, used in Sichuan about 1,000 years ago — more than 600 years before the first banknotes in Europe (Sweden, 1661) or in colonial America (Massachusetts, 1690).

Province 行省 xíngshěng

The Yuan divided the country into provinces run by officials sent from the capital. China’s provinces today — a bit like US states, but with less self-rule — grew out of this system.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes (curriculum links)

Curriculum: 2022 Compulsory Education History Standards, ancient China, “Liao, Song, Xia, Jin and Yuan” (in outline): coexisting regimes and ethnic integration, the southward shift of the economic centre, overseas trade, Yuan unification and the province system, and Song–Yuan science. Textbook: unified-edition Chinese History, Grade 7 Vol. 2, Unit 2 (lessons such as “Liao, Western Xia and the Northern Song”, “The Jin and the Southern Song”, “The Song economy”, “Yuan rule”, “Song–Yuan science and foreign contact”).

Suggestions: (1) play the map and have students name the three stages (began, sped up, completed) and the reasons; (2) use the 3D flooding test to explain watertight compartments and link it with the compass at sea as technical conditions of Song sea trade; (3) after the province game ask: where does today’s “province” come from? (the Yuan province system).

The English is written for US middle-schoolers with no background in Chinese history: names carry characters, pinyin or a say-it hint, with familiar anchors (Miami’s latitude, the size of the lower 48 states, what was happening in Europe and North America). On the year slider, borders and capitals are schematic. The “meanwhile, elsewhere” notes and the extra figures in captions (the length of the Quanzhou wreck, the pagoda’s height, the dates of the first Western banknotes) are enrichment beyond the textbook.
