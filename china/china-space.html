China in Space: from Dongfanghong to Chang’e

The Moon always shows Earth the same face. So how do you talk to a robot parked on the far side — where Earth is never in the sky? In 2019 China became the first country to pull it off.

Your mission: fly from China’s first satellite in 1970 to today. Build the “Magpie Bridge” relay, soft-land on the far side, dock a Shenzhou with the Tiangong space station, and pick the right Long March rocket for each job. Collect all 12 stars to rise from Cadet to Chief Designer!

Mission 1: build the “Magpie Bridge” — why a relay?

Radio travels in straight lines, like light — it can’t go through the Moon. Slide the lander round to the far side and watch the signal. Then drag the Queqiao (鹊桥 Quèqiáo, “Magpie Bridge”) relay satellite to a spot that can “see” both Earth and the lander.

What is the Earth–Moon L2 point?

About 65,000 km (40,000 mi) beyond the Moon lies the Earth–Moon L2 point (a Lagrange point). There the pulls of Earth and Moon work together so a satellite can circle Earth in step with the Moon, using very little fuel. Queqiao (launched May 2018) doesn’t sit right on L2: it flies a big loop around it — a “halo orbit” — so the Moon never blocks its view of Earth.

Mission 2: soft landing on the far side

You’re flying a Chang’e lander 1,800 m (5,900 ft) above the surface, falling at 45 m/s (100 mph). The Moon has no air, so parachutes are useless — only your braking engine can slow you. Throttle up, hover near 100 m, dodge the boulders and set down gently on flat ground.

Lander controls

Keys: ↑ ↓ throttle, ← → side jets. Safe landing: ≤ 3 m/s on flat ground.

Mission 3: dock Shenzhou with Tiangong

Both ships race around Earth at 7.7 km/s (17,000 mph) — yet they must meet at a crawl, about 0.2 m/s (slower than a toddler walks). Tap the thrusters: each tap changes your speed a little. There are no brakes in space; to stop you fire the opposite way.

Thrusters

Keys: → speed up, ← slow down, ↑ ↓ up/down. To dock: closing speed 0.05–0.3 m/s, up/down miss ≤ 0.5 m.

☆☆☆ ★ docked · ★ stop at the 19 m hold point · ★ ≤ 0.2 m/s with ≥ 40% fuel
What are “orbit effects”? Why does pushing up make you drift backward?

In orbit, higher means slower to go round. Fire upward (away from Earth) and you climb to a slightly higher orbit — so you slowly fall behind the station. Fire downward and you creep ahead. This game uses simplified Hill (Clohessy–Wiltshire) equations for that effect and runs time 4× faster. The real Shenzhou plans each burn with computers and a ground team.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows China’s national History Curriculum Standard for Compulsory Education (2022 edition), strand “Contemporary Chinese History — achievements in science and culture”, matching the unified-edition textbook Chinese History, Grade 8 Volume 2, Unit 6 lesson on science and culture achievements (“Two Bombs, One Satellite”, crewed spaceflight, lunar exploration; lesson numbers differ slightly between printings), and links to the new-era lesson in Unit 3. The English is written for US middle-schoolers new to the topic, with pinyin and meanings for key names.

Material beyond the textbook (extension): Queqiao’s halo orbit and line-of-sight geometry; the simplified physics of landing and docking (the docking game uses simplified Hill/Clohessy–Wiltshire equations with time ×4; the landing game uses lunar gravity 1.62 m/s² and a 1,500–7,500 N throttleable engine); Long March figures (approximate); launches per year (approximate); landing-site coordinates; Chang’e-6 and Tianwen-2 (2024–2025, newer than some printings).

Suggested use: ① run the timeline and have students order Dongfanghong-1 → Shenzhou-5 → Chang’e-1 to -6 → Tianwen-1 → Tiangong, and tell the four programmes apart (Shenzhou, Tiangong, Chang’e, Tianwen); ② in Mission 1, ask students to explain why the far side needs a relay — then why Queqiao doesn’t sit exactly on L2; ③ read the Moon map: why are all Apollo sites on the near side? ④ discussion: the textbook’s “spirit of crewed spaceflight” — enduring hardship, fighting hard, tackling tough problems, dedication.
