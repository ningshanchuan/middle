The Yangtze & the Three Gorges Dam

How do you lift a 3,000-ton ship 113 m (370 ft) — as high as a 37-storey building — in about 40 minutes? The answer is on the Yangtze River, right next to the biggest power station on Earth.

Your mission: ride 6,300 km (3,900 mi) from a glacier on the Tibetan Plateau down to Shanghai; run the Three Gorges reservoir through flood season; then operate the giant ship locks and ship lift to get a convoy past the dam as fast as possible. Mission 2: flood alert! Run the Three Gorges reservoir

The reservoir can be set anywhere from 145 m to 175 m above sea level. Higher water stores more and makes more power — but leaves less room to catch a flood. Pick a season and a level and watch the readouts; then send in a flood and see if the dam can hold it.

Mission 3: get the convoy past the dam

The dam leaves the water upstream about 113 m higher than downstream. Ships have two ways up: ① the double-lane, five-step ship locks (双线五级船闸) — a staircase of 5 water “steps” that takes several big ships at once but needs about 3½ hours; ② the ship lift (升船机) — a giant elevator, about 40 minutes, but one ship of up to 3,000 tons at a time. How does a lock work? Why fill the chamber before opening the gate?

A lock chamber is a giant pool with a gate at each end. The ship sails in, the gate behind it closes, and water from upstream flows in through tunnels, lifting the water — and the ship. When the water is level with the next step, the pressure on both sides of the gate is equal and the gate can swing open. If the levels differ, thousands of tons of water press the gate shut. Each chamber of the five-step locks is 280 m long and 34 m wide — longer than two and a half football fields.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports the 2022 Compulsory-Education Geography Curriculum Standard (“Understanding China”): use maps and data to describe the main features of the Yangtze and its influence on society and the economy, and explain why water resources should be developed and used wisely. Textbook: PEP Geography Grade 8 Vol. 1, Ch. 2 §3 Rivers (developing the Yangtze); the benefits and problems of the Three Gorges Project can be linked to the regional units of Grade 8 Vol. 2 (sections vary by printing). The English compares with the Mississippi and Hoover Dam for US students.

Extension material (approximate, simplified for teaching): distances and heights on the profile; the storage curve (≈17.15 billion m³ at 145 m, ≈39.3 billion m³ at 175 m) and the power formula; the “safe release ≈ 55,000 m³/s” and the two floods are teaching scenarios, not the real operating rules; convoy timings (≈3.5 h per lockage, ≈40 min per lift trip) are simplified — in reality several groups follow each other up the lock staircase; comparison values are commonly quoted approximations.

Suggested use: ① during the ride, have students record “dividing point – stretch – main feature” (upper: big drops, rich water power; middle: winding Jing River, lakes store floods; lower: wide, deep, shipping); ② in the dam sim, discuss why the reservoir is low in flood season and high in the dry season; ③ use the benefits/challenges panel for a structured debate on weighing gains and costs and sustainable development; ④ link the locks to the physics idea of connected vessels (连通器).
