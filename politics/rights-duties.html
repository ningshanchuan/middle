Going to school: is it a right — or a duty?

In China’s Constitution the answer is: both. Chapter II lists the basic rights (基本权利, jīběn quánlì) every citizen has and the basic duties (基本义务, jīběn yìwù) every citizen must carry out — side by side. Sort them into the tree, follow one citizen from birth to old age, and try to keep the scales level.

🌏 How is this different from the US?

Both constitutions protect rights. They are written differently and stress different things.

ChinaUnited States
Where they are listedChapter II of the Constitution, “Fundamental Rights and Duties of Citizens” — rights and duties in one chapterThe Bill of Rights (first ten amendments) and later amendments; duties are mostly set by ordinary laws
Voting age1818
EducationThe Constitution makes education both a right and a duty; nine years are compulsoryNot named in the federal Constitution; each state’s constitution and laws provide public schools and require attendance
Economic and social rightsThe Constitution lists rights such as work, rest and material help when old, ill or unable to workThe federal Constitution focuses on civil and political rights; programmes such as Social Security come from ordinary laws
Military serviceThe Constitution calls military service according to law an honourable duty of citizensAll-volunteer forces since 1973; men aged 18–25 must register with the Selective Service
⚠️ Common mix-up:A right is something you may use — or choose not to use. A duty must be carried out; you cannot give it up. Education and work are special in China’s Constitution: they are both, so a school-age child cannot drop out by saying “I give up my right”.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows mainland China’s own course: the “rule-of-law education” theme for Grades 7–9 in the 2022 national curriculum standard for Morality and Rule of Law (道德与法治), and Unit 2 “Understanding Rights and Duties” of the national unified textbook, Grade 8 Volume 2 (Lesson 3 “Citizens’ rights”, Lesson 4 “Citizens’ duties”). Definitions use the textbook’s wording; provisions follow Chapter II of the current Constitution (as amended in 2018). The English version is written for American middle-school students; the US comparison is factual only and does not rank either system.

Suggestions: the ages in the life slider come from the Compulsory Education Law (school from age six), the Labour Law (no hiring under sixteen), Article 34 of the Constitution (voting at eighteen) and the Military Service Law. The four pairs in the balance activity are only examples of “rights and duties are united” — they do not mean each right has exactly one matching duty. Ask students for their own examples.
