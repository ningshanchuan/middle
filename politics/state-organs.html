How China works · Civics · State organs

A law to write, a bribe to investigate, a case to judge: who does what?

China’s Constitution sets up different state organs (国家机构, guójiā jīgòu), each with its own job. At the centre is the National People’s Congress (全国人民代表大会, NPC): Chinese students learn that the other organs are created by it, answer to it and are supervised by it. Higher and lower levels: lead or supervise?

Inside each family of organs, the link between higher and lower levels is not the same — a classic exam trap.

GovernmentsLead: all local governments are under the State Council
Supervisory commissionsLead: a higher commission leads the lower ones
CourtsSupervise: a higher court supervises the trial work of lower courts
ProcuratoratesLead: a higher procuratorate leads the lower ones
⚠️ Common mix-up:Two Chinese words sound almost alike: 监察 (jiānchá, supervision) and 检察 (jiǎnchá, procuratorial work). Supervisory commissions watch over public officials; procuratorates approve arrests and prosecute cases in court. They are different organs.

🌏 How is this different from the US?

Both countries have bodies that make laws, run the government and judge cases. How those bodies relate — and what some titles mean — is different.

ChinaUnited States
Overall designDemocratic centralism: the other organs are created by the people’s congress, answer to it and are supervised by itSeparation of powers: three co-equal branches that check and balance each other
Head of statePresident (国家主席): elected by the NPC; represents the state and promulgates laws that the NPC or its Standing Committee has passedPresident: chosen by voters through the Electoral College; both head of state and head of government; can veto bills
Head of governmentPremier of the State Council: nominated by the President, decided by the NPCThe President
Top courtSupreme People’s Court: its president is elected by the NPC; it judges cases independently according to law; supervising the Constitution belongs to the NPC, not the courtsSupreme Court: justices are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, for life; it can review whether laws fit the Constitution
ProsecutorsPeople’s procuratorates are a separate kind of state organ (“legal supervision organs”), created by the people’s congressesProsecutors belong to the executive branch (Department of Justice, district attorneys)
Watching public officialsSupervisory commissions (since 2018): a separate organ just for this jobNo single body: inspectors general, the Justice Department, Congress and the courts share the job

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows mainland China’s own course: the “rule-of-law education” and “national conditions” themes for Grades 7–9 in the 2022 national curriculum standard for Morality and Rule of Law (道德与法治), and Lesson 6 “State Organs of China” (the organs of state power, the President, the administrative, supervisory and judicial organs) in Unit 3 “The People Are Masters of the Country” of the national unified textbook, Grade 8 Volume 2. Definitions use the textbook’s wording; the organs follow Chapter III of the current Constitution (as amended in 2018, including the supervisory commissions). The English version is written for American middle-school students; the US comparison is factual only and does not rank either system.

Suggestions: the chart shows the national level only; the three arrows (creates, answers to, supervises) describe the administrative, supervisory, trial and procuratorial organs in relation to the congress, while the President is elected by the NPC. The Constitution’s state organs also include the Central Military Commission, which this textbook lesson does not treat separately. The Civil Code vote (2,879 for, 2 against, 5 abstentions) and Presidential Order No. 45 are real; use them to discuss how the organs divide the work and cooperate.
