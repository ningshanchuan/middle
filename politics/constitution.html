Which law is the boss of all the other laws?

A country has hundreds of laws. If two of them disagree, which one wins? In China the answer is the Constitution (宪法, xiànfǎ). Chinese students learn that it is “the fundamental law of the state” — the rulebook that every other rule has to follow. Build the pyramid of laws yourself and find out why it sits at the top.

Compare: the Constitution vs. ordinary laws

Why is the Constitution “fundamental”? The Chinese textbook gives three reasons: what it says, how strong it is, and how hard it is to change. Try the vote experiment to feel the third one.

The ConstitutionOrdinary laws
What it saysThe most basic questions of national life: what kind of state China is, its basic system and tasks, citizens’ basic rights and duties, how state organs are set upOne area of life each (schools, traffic, contracts…)
How strongSupreme legal force. Other laws are written on its basis. Every organisation and person must follow itWritten on the basis of the Constitution; if a law goes against it, the law is invalid
How to changeStricter: proposed by the NPC Standing Committee or at least 1/5 of NPC deputies, then passed by at least 2/3 of all NPC deputiesPassed by more than half of all NPC deputies (or of the NPC Standing Committee)

🌏 How is this different from the US?

Both countries have a written constitution, and in both it is the highest law. The ways of changing it and checking on it are different.

ChinaUnited States
Current constitutionAdopted in 1982 (the fourth since 1954)Written in 1787, in force since 1789
How it is amendedProposed by the NPC Standing Committee or 1/5 of NPC deputies; passed by 2/3 of all NPC deputies. Amended five times (1988–2018)Proposed by 2/3 of both houses of Congress (or a convention), then ratified by 3/4 of the states. 27 amendments
Who checks that laws fit itThe National People’s Congress and its Standing CommitteeThe courts (judicial review), with the Supreme Court having the final say
How state power is organisedThe people’s congress system with “democratic centralism”: other state organs are created by the congress, answer to it and are supervised by itThree separate branches (Congress, President, courts) that check and balance each other
⚠️ Common mix-up:The Constitution is the fundamental law, but it is not a giant book containing every rule. Ordinary laws fill in the details. Both are real law that the state enforces — they differ in content, strength and how they are changed.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows mainland China’s own course: the “rule-of-law education” theme for Grades 7–9 in the 2022 national curriculum standard for Morality and Rule of Law (道德与法治), and Unit 1 “Upholding the Supremacy of the Constitution” of the national unified textbook, Grade 8 Volume 2 (Lesson 1 “Safeguarding the authority of the Constitution”, Lesson 2 “Ensuring the Constitution is implemented”). Definitions use the textbook’s wording; constitutional facts follow the current Constitution (adopted 1982, fifth amendment 2018). The English version is written for American middle-school students meeting China’s system for the first time; the US comparison is factual only and does not rank either system.

Suggestions: “administrative regulations” and “local regulations” in the pyramid go a little beyond the textbook (which focuses on the Constitution–ordinary law relationship); they make “nothing may contradict the Constitution” visible. The timeline lists only the points of each amendment that matter for this unit, not every change. The 3,000 deputies in the vote experiment are a round number; ask “Is exactly 1,500 more than half?” and “Is exactly 2,000 at least two-thirds?”.
