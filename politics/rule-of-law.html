Rule of Law: What Makes a Rule a Law?

Your school says “don’t be late.” The law says “don’t steal.” Both are rules — so why does breaking one get you detention and the other can get you arrested? In China, middle-schoolers study this in a course called Morality and Rule of Law (道德与法治, Dàodé yǔ Fǎzhì). Here is what they learn: what law is, which acts break it, how the law shields people under 18, and how a whole country is “governed by law.”

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

This lesson follows China’s national curriculum standard for Morality and Rule of Law (2022 edition, “rule-of-law education” theme for grades 7–9) and uses the definitions and wording of the national unified textbook 《道德与法治》: Grade 7 Volume 2, Unit 4 “Entering the World of Law”; Grade 8 Volume 1, Unit 2 Lesson 5 “Being a Law-abiding Citizen”; Grade 9 Volume 1, Unit 2 Lesson 4 “Building a Law-based China”. Unit numbers refer to the edition used from 2016; the newly revised edition reorders some chapters. The “six protections” follow the Law on the Protection of Minors (in force 1 June 2021); ages of criminal responsibility follow Amendment XI to the Criminal Law.

For US classrooms: the lesson presents China’s legal system in the terms of China’s own textbook; the “Compare with the US” cards are factual side-by-sides and do not rank either system. Suggested flow: hook discussion “how is a school rule different from a law?” (5 min) → groups explore diagrams 1–3 (15 min) → students build diagram 4 and work the case files alone (15 min) → quiz and sorting (10 min). Extension: compare how the two countries regulate minors online and the age of criminal responsibility.
