Life Online: Digital Citizenship

A photo can bring faraway family into your day. It can also give away where you are. How do you decide what to share, what to trust, and how to treat the people on the other side of the screen?

Make deliberate choices

The internet moves information quickly and lets people across oceans learn, work, and talk together. Before you go online, ask what you want to accomplish, what you need to verify, and what you need to protect.

Personal information and privacy

Should I share this?

Imagine posting where strangers can see it. Select a card, then choose what you would do with that information. You can also drag the card.

Why these groups?

Keep passwords and sign-in codes secret. Share details such as your full name plus school or live location through a trusted channel when needed. Ask before posting group photos. Check your own photos for background clues and location tags that could reveal a private location, and remove those clues. A public event link or your own art without personal details is okay to share.

Try the example password meter

Choose from fixed examples to compare length and predictable patterns. This simplified comparison runs on your device. These examples are public, so keep them out of real accounts.

Check the audience and purpose

Personal information can identify you on its own or with other clues. Examples include a name, contact details, a recognizable face or a precise location. Before posting a photo, check for uniforms, street numbers and location tags.

RememberMake passwords long, hard to guess, and unique to each account.

Use a password manager for long, unique passwords, or make a passphrase from randomly chosen unrelated words. If available, turn on multi-factor authentication for an extra check when you sign in.

Spot rumors and misinformation

Can you trust this post?

The posts and source notes are invented. Open the notes, then decide what the evidence supports.

Lateral reading checklist

Lateral reading means leaving the post and opening other sources to check who is behind it and what supports it. A rumor spreads before it is confirmed. False or misleading information can spread by mistake. Hold off on sharing if the evidence is thin.

  1. Check the source. Search elsewhere for who runs it and what they know about the topic.
  2. Trace the evidence. Find the original notice, image or data and check the date, place and context.
  3. Compare independent, reliable sources. Several copies of one post still lead back to one source.
In-class check

Respond to cyberbullying

Case file: An image in the group chat

Protect yourself and support others

Cyberbullying is bullying through chats, games or social platforms. It can include repeated insults, threats or sharing an embarrassing image. One post can do repeated harm when people keep sharing it.

RememberAvoid replying or forwarding. Save screenshots, account names and times privately. Tell a trusted adult promptly, report it to the platform and block the account if needed.

As a bystander, check in privately, offer to go with the person to get help, and report harmful content.

In-class check

Make room for life offline

Plan the free time

Imagine 90 minutes before dinner. Set aside 30 for chores. Drag the slider to divide the rest between watching videos and taking a walk.

Worked example: How much time is left to walk?

Given: There are 90 minutes before dinner. Chores take 30 minutes and videos take 25 minutes.

Find: How many minutes are left for a walk?

Solve: Subtract the time for chores, then the time for videos.

Answer: There are 35 minutes left for a walk.

Notice what gets crowded out

Signs of problematic use include repeatedly going past your planned stopping time, losing sleep or time for study, activity or friends, and continuing even when it causes problems. Try a planned stop time, turning off autoplay or charging devices away from the bed.

Common mix-upOne long session tells you little on its own. Look for a pattern of losing control and disruption to daily life. If it keeps happening, talk with a trusted adult.

Rights and responsibilities online

Try it: Does criticizing a comment justify posting the writer’s home address?

You can criticize the idea and explain why. Keep the other person’s home address private.

You can express opinions and exchange ideas online. Respect others’ rights, including privacy and reputation, and follow the laws that apply.

China · Online protection

Article 72 in the online protection chapter of China’s Law on the Protection of Minors requires a parent or other guardian’s consent to process personal information online for children under 14, with exceptions set by law or administrative regulation.

Read Article 72

United States · Children’s online privacy

The U.S. Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) Rule generally requires covered websites and online services, such as commercial sites aimed at children, to verify a parent’s consent before collecting personal information online from children under 13.

Read the Federal Trade Commission’s guidance

Be a responsible digital citizen

Share something useful

You want to remind neighbors about a book swap and find a beautiful poster someone drew. What would you do with the image before posting?

Use your voice to help

Posting and sharing leave a digital footprint. You can use it to help others by sharing checked information and responding kindly. Respect copyright by checking that you have the right to reuse a work and following any conditions, such as giving credit.

Common mix-upGiving credit tells readers who made the work. To repost it, check whether the law or the creator’s permission allows that use.

How do recommendations shape what you see?

Enrichment

Switch to Advanced to try the feed simulation.

This model feed has 12 slots. Each represents one post. Open any post to see how the next feed changes, or drag the slider to revisit earlier rounds.

A recommender can use clicks and watch time as clues about your interests. Seeing more of a topic gives you more chances to click it again, creating a feedback loop. In this model, posts can crowd around one topic. When the sources or viewpoints you see also narrow, it can contribute to a filter bubble.

Challenge: Click one topic three times

Reset and click one topic three times. Turn on the three-slot minimum to compare the same clicks under both rules. You can also seek out different topics, independent sources, and other viewpoints yourself.

Model rule: Each topic starts with four slots. Each click shifts one slot from each other topic to the selected topic, with at least one slot kept per topic. Checking the box replays the same clicks with a three-slot minimum. Real platforms use different rules.
