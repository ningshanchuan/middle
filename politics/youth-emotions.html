Body and mind grow together

Puberty is the process of physical development toward adult maturity. Hormones carry chemical signals that help drive changes such as growth spurts and voice changes. The timing and pace of puberty vary from person to person.

Adolescence (青春期, qīngchūnqī) includes puberty and changes in how you think and relate to others. You may want to make more of your own decisions or care more about what friends think. Hormone changes, sleep, experiences and relationships can all affect your mood. It’s common to feel excited and nervous at the same time.

Thinking for yourself

Sort the everyday choices

Choose an action, then choose the category that fits it best.

Find your own reasons

Independent thinking (独立思考, dúlì sīkǎo) means forming your own judgment and hearing other views. To practice critical thinking (批判精神, pīpàn jīngshén), ask questions, check evidence, explain your reasons and suggest improvements.

Common mix-up: a reasoned disagreement shows independent thinking. Rejecting a suggestion solely because someone else made it is defiance for its own sake.

Remember: ask “What supports this?” and “What evidence could change my mind?”

Boys and girls: friendship and respect

Who gets which job?

A robotics group needs someone to adjust the sensor and someone to present the project. A teammate suggests assigning jobs by gender. Explore both responses.

Get to know the person

Boys and girls can become friends through shared interests and learn from each other’s strengths. A gender stereotype (性别刻板印象) is an assumption about someone based only on gender. When sharing jobs, ask people what they enjoy, what they can do and what they want to learn.

Respect includes personal boundaries and privacy. Ask before posting a photo of someone. If a friend says “no,” respect that choice.

Emotions can spread and overlap

Same scene, different tone.

Joy, anger, sadness and fear are familiar examples of basic emotions (喜怒哀惧, xǐ nù āi jù). Emotions can spread from person to person through expressions, tone of voice or actions. This is called emotional contagion (情绪感染). People may respond to the same cues differently.

One event can bring mixed emotions. Moving away can mean feeling sad about leaving old friends and excited about meeting new ones at the same time.

Manage emotions and choose a response

Breathe with the box

Breathe in gently, pause, breathe out gently, then pause again. Each side of the square takes four seconds, so one full round takes sixteen seconds. Go at a comfortable pace and return to your usual breathing if holding feels uncomfortable.

Give yourself another option

Relaxation (放松训练)
When an emotion feels strong, a brief pause and slower breathing can give you time to choose what to do next.
Reframing (改变认知评价)
Try turning “I got a question wrong, so I must be terrible at this” into “I got one question wrong. I can look at where I got stuck.”
Reach out to someone you trust
Try “I’ve been thinking about this for days. Could you listen for a minute?” If a feeling keeps interfering with sleep or daily life, talk with a trusted adult.

Empathy and seeing another point of view

One event, two perspectives

Two friends both smile after a performance. Open each perspective, then think about what you could ask.

Ask, then listen

Empathy (共情, gòngqíng) means trying to understand someone else’s experience and feelings. You might catch a friend’s joy when they laugh. That’s emotional contagion. To understand why they’re happy, try asking and listening to what they choose to share.

Emotions (情绪) can shift as a situation changes. Rehearsing with a friend can bring moments of nerves and joy. The trust and affection (情感) that grow through many rehearsals can last much longer.

Look and read: how do artists express feelings?
In my youth I knew no taste of sorrow. I loved to climb the tower.
I loved to climb the tower, and pretended to be sad to write a new verse.
Now I know sorrow’s taste. I start to speak, then stop.
I start to speak, then stop, and say, “How cool the air is. What a fine autumn.”

Xin Qiji (Southern Song), “Chou Nu’er: Written on a Wall on the Road to Boshan”

The repeated “I start to speak, then stop” lets you hear the hesitation. In the last line, the speaker talks about the cool autumn air and leaves the sorrow for us to sense.
