Sun file: an ordinary but special star

The Sun is just a medium-sized yellow star, but because it is so close, it supplies almost all the energy on Earth.

⚛️

Nuclear fusion

In the core, hydrogen nuclei join to make helium and release huge energy. The Sun fuses about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen every second.

⏱️

8 min 20 s

The Sun is about 150 million km away, so sunlight takes 8 min 20 s to arrive. You see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago.

🌍

109 Earths wide

The Sun is about 1.39 million km across, yet it is only medium-sized; some stars are 1,000 times wider.

⌛

Middle-aged

The Sun is 4.6 billion years old, about halfway through its life. In about 5 billion years it will become a red giant and finally a white dwarf.

⚠️ Common mix-up:In everyday life red means “hot” and blue means “cold”, but for stars it is the opposite: blue stars are the hottest and red stars are the coolest. Think of a gas-stove flame: the blue part is hottest.

The Sun’s 11-year heartbeat: the sunspot cycle

Sunspots are cooler, darker patches where tangled magnetic fields poke through the Sun’s surface. Their number rises and falls about every 11 years. Drag the year slider (or press ▶) to watch the Sun change from 1950 to 2030.

Why are sunspots dark?

They are not really cold: a sunspot is about 3,500–4,500 °C, just cooler than the 5,500 °C surface around it. Strong magnetic fields block hot gas from rising there, so the spot looks darker. On its own in the night sky, a sunspot would shine brighter than the full Moon.

What happens at solar maximum?

With more spots come more solar flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs), so Earth gets more (and stronger) auroras, and satellites, radio and power grids are more likely to be disturbed. Cycle 25 peaked around 2024; in May of that year a huge geomagnetic storm brought auroras as far south as Florida.

Solar wind & auroras: Earth’s magnetic shield

The Sun constantly blows out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind. Earth’s magnetic field acts like a shield and turns most of them aside, but some are guided along field lines toward the North and South Poles, where they hit the upper atmosphere and make it glow: an aurora. Speed up the wind or launch a coronal mass ejection (CME) and see the shield get squeezed and the aurora spread south.
