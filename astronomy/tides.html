The Sun makes tides bigger or smaller

The Sun’s tide-raising effect is less than half of the Moon’s, but it either teams up with the Moon or plays tug-of-war with it. Here are four key moments in one trip of the Moon around Earth (seen from above the North Pole, Sun on the left).

Spring tideNeap tide
Sun–Earth–MoonIn a straight lineAt a right angle (90°)
Moon phaseNew Moon, Full MoonFirst & third quarter
PullsAdd togetherPartly cancel
Tidal rangeLargest: highest highs, lowest lowsSmallest: highs and lows are close
How oftenAbout every 2 weeks (twice a month)About every 2 weeks (twice a month)
⚠️ Common mix-up:Spring tides have nothing to do with the season spring! The name comes from water “springing up”. They happen all year, about every two weeks.

⚖️ Who pulls harder? Tide-raising strength

The Sun’s gravity on Earth is actually about 180 times stronger than the Moon’s, but tides depend on the DIFFERENCE in pull across Earth. That difference falls with the cube of distance, so the much closer Moon wins.

Moon at its average distance (384,400 km) = 1.00 (approximate values).

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E2 (model the effects of the relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon) and connects to S6E3e (causes and effects of waves, currents and tides). The 3D model uses the “equilibrium tide” idea: the bulges are hugely exaggerated, and Earth’s tilt, continents blocking the water, and the real-world tide delay are left out. Good questions: Why is there a high tide on the side facing away from the Moon? (That water is pulled less than Earth’s center, so it is “left behind”.) Why is high tide about 50 minutes later each day? (The Moon moves along its orbit, so Earth must turn about 50 extra minutes to face it again.)

Activity: have students use the tide-chart simulation to find two spring-tide days and two neap-tide days and match them to Moon phases; then compare with a real tide table for a local port (e.g. Savannah or Tybee Island).
