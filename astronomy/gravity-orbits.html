⚠️ Common mix-up:The pull is the same size on both objects (the two arrows are equal). Earth pulls on you and you pull on Earth just as hard; Earth is so massive that it barely moves.

Kepler’s discovery: elliptical orbits, faster near the Sun

About 400 years ago Johannes Kepler found that planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and that a planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun. Each colored wedge is the area the planet sweeps out in the same amount of time, and all the wedges have equal area.

How much would you weigh on other worlds?

Your mass (how much matter you are made of) is the same everywhere, but your weight (gravity’s pull on you) depends on which world you stand on. Click a world for details.

💡 Memory hintMass = how much stuff (kg), never changes. Weight = gravity’s pull (newtons), changes from world to world.
⚠️ Common mix-up:Jupiter is over 11 times wider than Earth, yet its surface gravity is only about 2.5 times Earth’s, and huge Saturn’s is about the same as Earth’s. Surface gravity depends on mass AND on how far the surface is from the center.

Who orbits whom? The barycenter

The Moon doesn’t simply circle Earth: Earth and Moon BOTH circle their shared center of mass, the barycenter. The heavy one sits close to it and only wobbles; the light one swings around a big circle. Pick a real pair, guess whether the barycenter is inside or outside the bigger body, then check.

🔭 Why astronomers care about the wobble

Jupiter makes the Sun wobble around their barycenter at only about 12 m/s, slower than a bicycle! Yet sensitive instruments can measure this kind of wobble in a distant star’s light, and that is how many unseen exoplanets were found.

💡 Memory hintThe barycenter is like a seesaw’s balance point: the heavy kid sits close to it and the light kid far away.
⚠️ Common mix-up:Pluto and Charon’s barycenter lies in open space between them, so they are more like a “double dwarf planet” than a moon simply circling Pluto.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1: developing and using a model to explain how the interaction of gravity and inertia governs the motion of objects in the solar system. It also connects to Grade 8 S8P5 (gravity depends on mass and distance). Suggested flow: have students predict before every launch; use the cannonball story to conclude that an orbit = inertia + gravity; step 6 doubles the planet’s mass to show that more mass means more gravity. On the gravity bench, students use the ×2 buttons to state the rules. In the Kepler sim, ask why the equal-area wedges are short and wide near perihelion. Finish with the weight table to separate mass from weight.

Notes: Newton’s mountain is about 1,100 km tall (exaggerated so it can be seen) and there is no air resistance. The planet-mass slider keeps the planet’s size fixed so that mass is the only variable. Kepler-sim speeds assume a 1 AU orbit around the Sun.
