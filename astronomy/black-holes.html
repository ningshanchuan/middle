Black Holes & Gravity Waves

What would happen if you squeezed the whole Earth down to the size of a marble? It would become a black hole: gravity so strong that not even light can escape. In 2015, humans “heard” two of them collide for the first time, by catching the ripples they sent through space itself.

You’ll be able to: explain why squeezing mass makes a black hole, calculate the Schwarzschild radius of anything, predict how objects move near a black hole, and read a gravitational-wave “chirp”.

Spacetime as a stretchy sheet

Einstein’s general relativity says mass curves spacetime, and objects follow those curves: that is gravity. Picture a stretchy sheet: put a heavy ball on it and it sags, so marbles rolling past curve around it. Squeeze the same mass into a smaller ball and the dip gets deeper, until a “hole” forms that not even light can climb out of.

🧮 How small would it need to be to become a black hole?

Anything of mass M becomes a black hole if it is squeezed inside its Schwarzschild radius: r = 2GM/c² (G is the gravitational constant, c the speed of light). Pick an object or drag the mass slider.

💡 Memory hintEvery 1 Sun of mass ≈ 3 km of radius. A 10-Sun black hole is about 30 km in radius, roughly the size of a big city.

Orbit a black hole: stable orbit, plunge or slingshot?

Launch a test particle near a black hole. Far away it moves like a planet around the Sun (Newton’s gravity). Close in, general relativity makes gravity stronger than Newton predicts: inside 3 Schwarzschild radii there are no stable circular orbits at all. Distances are measured in Schwarzschild radii (rs).

🚀 Launch pad

⚠️ Common mix-up:A black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner. From far away, a 10-Sun black hole pulls on you exactly like a 10-Sun star would. It’s only dangerous up close.

Simplified model: close to the black hole, gravity uses the Paczyński–Wiita “pseudo-Newtonian” formula a = GM/(r − rs)², which reproduces “no stable orbits inside 3 rs”. It is not a full general-relativity calculation, and speeds very near the horizon are not exact.

Gravitational waves: two black holes spiral together

Two black holes circling each other lose energy as gravitational waves, so they spiral closer and faster until they merge into one. As a wave passes, it stretches space one way and squeezes it the other. LIGO measures that stretch with laser “rulers” 4 km long, detecting changes far smaller than a proton. The signal, rising in both pitch and strength, is called a chirp.

Beyond grade 6: general relativity, the Schwarzschild radius r = 2GM/c², scientific notation, log scales, gravitational waves and the chirp mass all go well beyond the grade-6 standard; use them as enrichment or for an astronomy club. Suggested flow: squeeze the sheet all the way → calculate the Schwarzschild radius of Earth and of yourself → compare “Newton only” with “Relativity hint” on the launch pad → hear the chirp and read the LIGO timeline.

Model notes: the rubber sheet is an analogy with known flaws (real spacetime is 4-dimensional, and the sheet needs gravity pulling “down” to work); sizes in the 3D scene are compressed on a log scale. The launch-pad and gravitational-wave sims are simplified models, as labelled on the page.
