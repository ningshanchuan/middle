Orbits, Kepler’s Laws & Going to Mars

Why can’t a spacecraft leave for Mars whenever it wants, but has to wait about 26 months for the next chance? The answer is hidden in three laws Johannes Kepler found 400 years ago, and in Newton’s law of gravity.

What you’ll be able to do: predict whether a launch falls, orbits or escapes; test all three of Kepler’s laws with real data; and pick your own launch day to send a probe to Mars.

Kepler’s three laws lab

Kepler had no telescope data, only Tycho Brahe’s 20-plus years of naked-eye measurements. It took him about ten years of calculations to find three rules. Now test them yourself.

🪐 Launch planets, time their years

Challenge: Mission to Mars (Hohmann transfer)

The cheapest route is an ellipse that touches Earth’s orbit at one end and Mars’s orbit at the other. The probe coasts half of it, about 8.5 months. The catch: when you leave, Mars must already be ahead of Earth by about 44°, so it arrives at the meeting point at the same time you do. Pick a launch date, set your burn, and launch!

Model notes: Earth’s and Mars’s orbits have their real sizes and periods but are simplified to circles (Mars’s real orbit is a little stretched, e = 0.093). Only the Sun’s gravity is computed; the “burn” is your speed relative to Earth after escaping Earth’s gravity. The target zone is 4.5 million km wide in radius, far more generous than a real mission.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1 (especially element d: develop and use a model to explain how gravity and inertia govern the motion of objects in the solar system); the Mars mission also connects to S6E2 (positions in the Earth–Moon–Sun system change over time in predictable ways). This lesson goes beyond grade 6: it uses the real gravitational constant, numerical integration, log–log graphs, the quantitative form of Kepler’s 3rd law (T² = a³) and Hohmann transfer orbits. Suggested flow: in Newton’s cannon, have students predict what happens near 7.9 km/s and 11.2 km/s; in the Kepler lab, test the laws one by one (the 3rd-law table lets students “discover” T²/a³ ≈ 1); use Figure 1 to discuss why Jupiter’s moons lie on a different line (a different central mass); then run the Mars challenge.

Simplifications: Newton’s cannon ignores air resistance and Earth’s spin; the Mars mission uses circular orbits and only the Sun’s gravity, so its window dates can be off from the real ones by weeks. Figure 2’s real launch dates let students judge how good the model is, which is a great discussion in itself.
