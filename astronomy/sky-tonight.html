Figure: how the sky turns

This is the “celestial sphere” seen from the side: you stand in the middle and the flat line is your horizon. Earth’s axis points at the celestial pole, and every star travels once a day along a path parallel to the celestial equator. Change the latitude and see which stars never set (circumpolar) and which never rise at all.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1 (scientific views of the universe: brightness, distance and patterns of stars) and S6E2 (relative motions of the Earth, Moon and Sun: Moon phases, day and night). The map uses a real catalog (about 5,000 stars brighter than magnitude 6); planet positions are computed day by day from JPL approximate orbital elements and the Moon from a short formula. Positions are normally good to better than 1°, plenty for checking outdoors.

This goes beyond grade 6: sidereal time, horizon coordinates (altitude/azimuth) versus equatorial coordinates (right ascension/declination), the logarithmic magnitude scale (5 magnitudes = 100× in brightness), and estimating a star’s temperature from its B−V colour index.

Activities: (1) use the “Now” button to make tonight’s chart, go outside and record which planets and which Moon phase you see; (2) observe the Moon at the same time for a week and check with “+1 day” that it moves about 13° east each day; (3) move the sky-darkness slider from city to countryside, discuss light pollution and join the Globe at Night citizen-science project.
