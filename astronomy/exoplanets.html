Exoplanets: Worlds Around Other Stars

A planet is billions of times fainter than its star and sits right next to it, so we almost never see it directly. So how have astronomers found more than 6,000 planets around other stars? By watching for tiny shadows and tiny wobbles.

You’ll be able to: read a light curve to find a planet, measure its size and the length of its year, and decide whether it could have liquid water.

The transit method: a planet crosses its star

If a planet’s orbit is lined up edge-on to us, once every orbit it passes in front of its star and blocks a little starlight. A telescope measures the star’s brightness over time and draws a light curve. How deep the dip is tells us the planet’s size; how often it comes tells us the length of its year.

Radial velocity: the star wobbles too

A planet and its star both orbit their shared center of mass. When the star moves toward us its light waves are squeezed and its spectral lines shift toward blue; when it moves away they stretch toward red. That’s the Doppler effect. The first planet found around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (1995), was found this way.

The habitable zone: not too hot, not too cold

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a rocky planet with Earth-like air could keep liquid water on its surface. A brighter star pushes the zone farther out; a dim red dwarf’s zone hugs the star. Change the star’s temperature and drag the planet around.

Zone edges: Kopparapu et al. (2014) fit, from “runaway greenhouse” (too hot) to “maximum greenhouse” (too cold), valid for stars of about 2,600–7,200 K. “Equilibrium temperature” assumes the planet reflects 30% of light and has no greenhouse effect (Earth’s is −18 °C / 0 °F; its air warms it to about +15 °C / 59 °F).

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1: obtain, evaluate and communicate information about current scientific views of the universe (S6E1.b stars and galaxies; S6E1.f compare physical attributes of planets). Students use our Solar System as the reference to compare real exoplanets’ sizes, orbits and temperatures, and they draw conclusions from models of data (light curves and velocity curves).

Beyond grade 6: depth = (Rp/Rs)², Kepler’s third law for the period, the Doppler formula and the habitable-zone fit go beyond the grade-6 standard; use them as enrichment or connect them to math (squares, square roots, log scales). Suggested flow: transit sim → planet hunter (reading data) → compare what each method measures (transit: size; radial velocity: mass) → habitable-zone challenge to wrap up.

Model notes: the transit curve assumes the star is equally bright all over (real stars are darker near the edge, which rounds the bottom of the dip); the star’s mass is estimated from its radius (main-sequence approximation); the 3D orbit is compressed and time fast-forwards between transits. Planet-hunter data are computer-generated, not real observations.
