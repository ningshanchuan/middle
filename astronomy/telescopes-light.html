Telescopes & Invisible Light

Your eyes see only a thin slice of the light in the universe. What would the sky look like if you could see radio waves, or X-rays? And why do we launch some telescopes into space but build others on mountaintops?

🎯 You will be able to: name the bands of the electromagnetic spectrum in order, explain which ones get through Earth’s air, and design a telescope by choosing its aperture and focal lengths.

The electromagnetic spectrum: one kind of light, many sizes

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same thing: electromagnetic waves traveling at about 300,000 km every second. The only difference is the wavelength. Drag the slider from long radio waves all the way to tiny gamma rays.

Build a telescope

A telescope does two jobs. It COLLECTS light with a big lens or mirror (the bigger the aperture, the more light), and it MAGNIFIES the image with an eyepiece. Change the parts, watch the light rays, then look through the eyepiece.

💡 Memory hintMagnification = objective focal length ÷ eyepiece focal length. But aperture is what really matters: it decides how faint and how fine you can see.
⚠️ Common mix-up:A small telescope with “600×!” on the box is not a good telescope. The highest useful magnification is about 2 × the aperture in millimeters; beyond that you just get a bigger, blurrier image (“empty magnification”).

Same object, different light: the Crab Nebula

In the year 1054, astronomers in Song-dynasty China recorded a “guest star” bright enough to see in daylight. It was a star exploding as a supernova, and its wreckage is the Crab Nebula, about 6,500 light-years away. Drag the slider to look at it with six different kinds of “eyes”.

💡 Memory hintDifferent light shows different temperatures and processes: radio and infrared show cool gas and dust, visible light shows glowing gas threads, and X-rays and gamma rays show the hottest, most violent places.

These six images come from NASA’s matched “M1 comparison” set (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0): they were lined up to the same scale and orientation, so they can be laid on top of each other. Gamma-ray telescopes are much less sharp, so that picture is a blurry spot.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1: obtaining, evaluating and communicating current scientific views of the universe and how they changed with evidence. Telescopes are the tools that drove those changes (Galileo saw Jupiter’s moons in 1610; Edwin Hubble used the Hooker telescope to find other galaxies). The optics connect to Grade 8 S8P4 (electromagnetic waves, lenses and mirrors). This goes beyond grade 6: quantitative spectrum relations (frequency = speed of light ÷ wavelength; photon energy = 1240 ÷ wavelength in nm, in eV), resolution ≈ 138 ÷ aperture in mm arcseconds (Rayleigh criterion at 550 nm), and light-gathering power growing with the square of the aperture.

Suggested flow: ask students to predict which kinds of light reach the ground, then check with the spectrum slider and the atmospheric-window figure. In the telescope builder, have students change only the aperture (keeping the magnification fixed) and record the resolution and faintest-star readouts. Finish with the matching challenge to explain “space vs mountaintop”. Notes: the eyepiece view assumes a 50° eyepiece and 1.5 arcsecond atmospheric seeing; the eyepiece views are simulations, not photos.
