Rocket Science

In space there is no air to push against, so what is a rocket pushing on? And why is a rocket about 90% fuel, yet still throws away whole pieces of itself on the way up?

What you’ll be able to do: explain thrust with Newton’s 3rd law and the rocket equation, then design a multi-stage rocket and fly it to orbit, and maybe send a payload toward the Moon.

Action and reaction: where thrust comes from

A rocket throws gas out of its back very fast. It pushes the gas backward, so the gas pushes the rocket forward just as hard. Thrust = mass thrown out each second × exhaust speed (F = ṁ × vₑ). Move the two sliders and watch the thrust.

💸 Where did your Δv go?

The engines’ total Δv = the speed you end with + gravity loss + drag loss + steering and other losses − the speed Earth’s spin gave you for free. For real rockets going to low orbit, gravity, drag and steering losses add up to about 1.5–2 km/s; this simplified model loses somewhat less.
🧮 The rocket equation: why does staging help?

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky wrote it down in 1903: Δv = vₑ × ln(m₀ ÷ m_f). Δv is the change in speed the rocket can make, m₀ is the total mass when the engines start, m_f is the mass when the fuel is gone, and ln is the natural logarithm. Doubling the mass ratio m₀ ÷ m_f only adds vₑ × 0.69, so just adding more fuel pays off less and less. The trick of staging: when one stage’s fuel is gone, you throw away its empty tanks and engines, so the rocket that is left starts again with a big mass ratio. Under the design bench you can compare the Δv of your staged rocket with the same fuel put into a single stage.

Why do rockets curve? (the gravity turn)

Getting to orbit is not about going high; it is about going sideways fast: about 7.8 km/s horizontally at 200 km up. A rocket rises straight up through the thickest air, tips over a little toward the east, then lets gravity gently bend its path. Drag the pitch-over angle: the same rocket gets very different results.

🎯 Same rocket, different turns

Reference rocket: two stages, about the size of a Falcon 9, carrying 8 tonnes.

💡 Memory hintStraight up = fighting gravity for nothing. Turning too hard = ploughing sideways through thick air. A gentle nudge, then let gravity do the turning.
⚠️ Common mix-up:The space station is only about 400 km up, not much farther than New York is from Washington, D.C. (about 330 km in a straight line). The hard part is not the height; it is the sideways speed of nearly 8 km every second.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1 (especially element d: gravity and inertia govern motion in the solar system; reaching orbit means going sideways fast enough that your “fall” never reaches the ground); the Moon mission connects to S6E2 (the Earth–Moon system), and the thrust bench connects to Grade 8 S8P3 (force, mass and motion). This lesson goes beyond grade 6: it uses F = ṁvₑ, the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation (with a natural logarithm), air density and gravity that change with height, and the ideas of gravity loss and drag loss. Suggested flow: on the thrust bench, compare a balloon with an F-1 engine (about 30 million times more thrust); then do the three missions in order (a one-stage rocket reaches space but not orbit, so students discover why staging is needed); finally find the best pitch-over angle in the gravity-turn sim and explain why both extremes fail.

Simplifications: 2D flight in the equatorial plane; a fixed drag coefficient; engine thrust changes smoothly between sea-level and vacuum values; the engines cut off automatically once the lowest point of the orbit is above 150 km; the Moon mission is judged by whether the Δv left over is at least about 3.1 km/s (enough for a trans-lunar injection), without simulating the trip itself. Engine figures are based on real engines but rounded.
