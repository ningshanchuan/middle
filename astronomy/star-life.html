How Stars Live & Die: the H-R Diagram

Why do some stars live for trillions of years while others blow up after just a few million? And where did the calcium, iron and gold in and around you come from? The answers are hidden in one picture: the Hertzsprung–Russell (H-R) diagram.

🎯 You will be able to: read an H-R diagram, predict a star’s life and death from its mass, estimate a star’s temperature from its color, and say where the elements in your body were made.

The life of a star: mass decides everything

Choose a mass (0.1 to 40 times the Sun’s), then press ▶ or drag the time bar. Watch the star be born, shine, swell and die. On the left is its path across the H-R diagram; on the right, what it looks like.

💡 Memory hintBigger stars live fast and die young. A star 10 times the Sun’s mass has 10 times the fuel but burns it thousands of times faster.
⚠️ Common mix-up:Stars do not “burn” like a campfire; there is no fire. They shine by nuclear fusion: atomic nuclei slam together at tens of millions of degrees and join into heavier nuclei. And a star moving on the H-R diagram means it is changing over time, not moving through space.

Simplified model: the tracks follow the rough shape of stellar-evolution models, and the boundaries between fates (about 8 and about 20 solar masses) are approximate. Lifetimes use the rule of thumb “main-sequence life ≈ 10 billion years × mass^−2.5”, which is too short for the heaviest stars (a 40-solar-mass star really lasts about 5 million years).

🚀 Advanced: the mass–luminosity law, and a star’s lifetime from E = mc²
The mass slider above controls this too. A main-sequence star’s luminosity grows roughly as its mass to the power a (L ∝ Mᵃ). Its fuel is the hydrogen in its core, about 10% of the star; when hydrogen fuses to helium, about 0.7% of the mass turns into energy.

A star’s color is its thermometer

Every hot object gives off a whole smooth rainbow of light called blackbody radiation. The hotter it is, the more the peak shifts toward short, blue wavelengths, and the more light it gives off. Drag the temperature slider.

💡 Memory hintWien’s law: peak wavelength (nm) ≈ 2,898,000 ÷ temperature (K). The Sun, about 5,800 K, peaks near 500 nm (green), which is why sunlight looks white, not green: it contains all colors.
⚠️ Common mix-up:There are no green stars and no purple stars: when the peak is at green, all the colors mix into white. The Sun is really white; it only looks yellow or orange low in the sky because our air scatters away some of its blue light. The coolest stars are red; the hottest are blue-white.

You are made of star stuff

The Big Bang made only hydrogen, helium and a tiny bit of lithium. Almost every other element was cooked by stars: by fusion inside them, in the winds of dying stars, in supernova explosions and in colliding neutron stars. Click an element to see where it came from.

Simplified: each element is colored by its main source only; many elements have several sources. After the “origin of the elements” periodic table by Jennifer Johnson (Ohio State University, 2019). A small dot marks elements your body has plenty of.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1: obtaining, evaluating and communicating current scientific views of the universe, including the make-up and scale of stars and galaxies, using real data. This lesson goes well beyond grade 6, toward high-school Earth & Space Science / Astronomy: reading the H-R diagram, the mass–luminosity–lifetime relation, the Stefan–Boltzmann law (L ∝ R²T⁴), Wien’s displacement law and nucleosynthesis. It connects to “the Sun is a star” in S6E1 and to the periodic table in physical science.

Suggested flow: have students find the Sun, Betelgeuse and Sirius B on the H-R diagram and explain why “cool but bright” must mean “huge”. Then compare 1, 5 and 25 solar-mass stars in the life simulator and record lifetimes and fates. Use the color simulator to estimate the temperatures of Betelgeuse and Rigel. Finish with the periodic table and the idea that we are made of star stuff. Note: evolution tracks and regions are schematic, and values are approximate.
