💡 Memory hintA light-year is the distance light travels in one year: about 9.46 trillion km. In one second light could circle Earth 7.5 times!
⚠️ Common mix-up:A light-year sounds like a time, but it measures distance, just like “a 10-minute walk away” describes how far something is.
⚡

Speed of light

About 300,000 km every second, the fastest speed in the universe. Sunlight reaches Earth in 8 minutes and Neptune in about 4 hours.

🔭

Looking back in time

Light from the Andromeda Galaxy travelled 2.5 million years to reach us, so we see Andromeda as it was 2.5 million years ago.

🏠

Cosmic address

Earth → Solar System → Orion Spur → Milky Way → Local Group → Virgo Supercluster → observable universe.

🔢

Countless galaxies

The observable universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies or more, each with millions to trillions of stars.

The expanding universe and the Big Bang

In 1929 Edwin Hubble found that almost every galaxy is moving away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away. Blow up the balloon to see why.

🎈 Balloon model

The rubber is space and the dots are galaxies. Click any galaxy to “stand” on it, then inflate the balloon.

⚠️ Common mix-up:The Big Bang was not an explosion at one spot in space. Space itself began expanding everywhere from a very hot, dense state. Just as the balloon’s surface has no center, the universe has no center: from every galaxy, the others seem to move away.
🔴

Evidence 1: redshift

Light from galaxies moving away is stretched toward red. More distant galaxies are more redshifted, so space is expanding.

📡

Evidence 2: cosmic background glow

In 1965 scientists detected faint microwaves coming from every direction in the sky: leftover heat from the Big Bang.

⚗️

Evidence 3: hydrogen & helium

The universe is about 3/4 hydrogen and 1/4 helium, matching what the Big Bang theory predicts.

⏳

13.8 billion years

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. The first stars and galaxies formed a few hundred million years later; the Solar System formed 4.6 billion years ago.

Galaxy classification challenge: Hubble’s tuning fork

In 1926 Edwin Hubble sorted galaxies by shape, and in 1936 he drew the scheme as a tuning fork: ellipticals on the handle, then two prongs, ordinary spirals on top and barred spirals below, with irregulars off to the side. Every picture below is a real telescope photo. Can you classify them?

How to tell: three clues

1) Arms or no arms? A smooth glow with no arms → elliptical (E). 2) If it has arms, is there a straight bar of stars across the middle with arms starting at its ends? Yes → barred spiral (SB); no → spiral (S). 3) Neither round nor armed, just a messy patch → irregular (Irr). Spirals seen edge-on look like a thin line, often with a dark dust lane.

Is the fork an evolution sequence?

No! Hubble wondered whether galaxies might change along the fork, but we now know they don’t. Big ellipticals are often built by galaxies colliding and merging, and are full of old red stars; spirals still have lots of gas in their arms, where young blue stars are being born. The Milky Way is a barred spiral.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1: asking questions about how models of the origin of the universe (the Big Bang) developed, and modeling the position of the solar system in the Milky Way and in the known universe. Suggested flow: run “Find our address”, then have students drag the View slider to edge-on and explain why the Milky Way looks like a band across the night sky. Compare the four galaxy types (shape, star ages, gas). Use the scale slider for a “cosmic address” exit ticket. Finish with the balloon: students stand on different galaxies and discover that “farther = faster” holds for every galaxy, so there is no center.

Notes: arm positions are schematic but follow the real layout (the Sun lies in the Orion Spur between the Sagittarius and Perseus arms). The model spins as one piece; in a real galaxy, inner and outer stars orbit at different rates. Balloon model limits: the real universe is 3D and there is no “inside” of the balloon.
