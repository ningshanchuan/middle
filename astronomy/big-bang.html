The Big Bang & the Expanding Universe

If almost every galaxy is moving away from us, what happens if you run the movie backward? Everything gets closer, more crowded and hotter… all the way back 13.8 billion years. You will measure how fast the universe is expanding yourself, and use it to work out the age of the universe.

🎯 By the end you can: get Hubble–Lemaître’s law and the age of the universe from your own measurements, tell the 13.8-billion-year history of the universe in order, and explain why looking farther away means looking further back in time.

🕰️ Two discoverers

⛪

Georges Lemaître, 1927

A Belgian physicist and Catholic priest. Using Einstein’s general relativity he showed the universe should be expanding and estimated the rate from existing data. In 1931 he proposed that the universe began from a “primeval atom”: the first form of the Big Bang idea.

🔭

Edwin Hubble, 1929

An American astronomer at Mount Wilson Observatory in California. He used Cepheids for distances and redshifts measured mostly by Vesto Slipher for speeds, and plotted “farther = faster”. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended calling it the Hubble–Lemaître law.

💡 Memory hintv = H₀ × d: speed = Hubble constant × distance. H₀ ≈ 70 means: for every extra megaparsec (3.26 million light-years) of distance, a galaxy moves away about 70 km/s faster.

📡 The oldest light: the cosmic microwave background

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled to about 3,000 K (about 2,700 °C). Electrons joined nuclei to make atoms, and light could travel freely for the first time. That light is still arriving, stretched about 1,100 times by expansion into microwaves at −270 °C (2.7 K above absolute zero). The colors show tiny temperature differences, only about 1 part in 100,000. The slightly denser spots later grew into galaxies.

In 1965 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs in New Jersey stumbled on this “noise” from every direction with a horn-shaped antenna, and won the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. COBE (1989), WMAP (2001) and Planck (2009) then mapped it in ever sharper detail.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S6E1.a: asking questions about how models of the origin of the universe (the Big Bang) developed and changed with new evidence; also S6E1.b (the solar system’s place in the known universe). Suggested flow: in the raisin bread, have students switch homes and discover there is no center; then measure 6–10 galaxies, draw the line, and compute H₀ and 1/H₀ (the Mystery universe makes a good team race). Compare with Hubble’s 1929 data and discuss why the first slope was wrong. Tell the story with the timeline, then play the ordering game. Finish with look-back time: “we see Andromeda as it was 2.5 million years ago”.

Beyond grade 6: Hubble–Lemaître’s law, log time scales, the CMB, dark matter and dark energy, comoving distance. Simplifications: the raisin-bread sim uses a constant expansion rate and adds a few hundred km/s of random “peculiar” motion and 5% distance errors, so points scatter. 1/H₀ ≈ 14 billion years is an estimate; the 13.8-billion-year age comes from the full model (ΛCDM with H₀ = 67.7, 31% matter, 69% dark energy), which this page integrates live for temperatures, scale and look-back times. The exact value of H₀ is still debated (about 67–73, the “Hubble tension”). Timeline pictures are illustrations.
