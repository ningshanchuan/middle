World Religions & Ethnic Groups: beliefs, peoples and places

One city, Jerusalem, is holy to three different religions at the same time. Arabs, Persians, Kurds and Turks are mostly Muslim, yet they speak four different languages. How can people who speak different languages share a faith? How can people in the same ethnic group follow different faiths? This lesson explores what people share when they belong to a religion or an ethnic group.

🎯Mission: watch 4,000 years of religions spreading on the map, build the Abrahamic Venn diagram, place key events on a BCE/CE timeline, and become a clue detective!

Key word 1🙏 Religion

A tradition of shared beliefs and practices. These can include beliefs about the sacred, religious texts, places of worship, prayer, festivals and guidance on how to live. People from different ethnic groups can share a religion.

Key word 2👥 Ethnic group

People who share a sense of identity, often through ancestry and history, language or customs (such as food, clothing, music and holidays). People in one ethnic group may follow different religions.

Key word 3☝️ Monotheism · many gods · philosophy

Monotheism = belief in one God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam). Hindu traditions vary. Many Hindus honor several gods, and some see them as forms of one ultimate reality. Buddhism does not center on a creator god. Confucianism is mostly a philosophy about how to behave and govern.

    👥 Ethnic groups: language, ancestry and customs

    Explore five groups with ties to Southwest Asia: Arabs, Persians, Kurds, Turks and Jews. In South and East Asia, look for more examples of ethnic groups whose members follow different religions. Tap a group on the map or a row in the table.

    Same faith, different peoples

    Four languages, one main religion

    Arabs speak Arabic, Persians speak Persian (Farsi), Kurds speak Kurdish, Turks speak Turkish. Islam is the most common religion in each group. Each group has its own language, history and customs. Iranian is a nationality that includes Persians, Arabs and other ethnic groups.

    Same people, different faiths

    One language, several religions

    Bengalis commonly speak Bengali: in Bangladesh most are Muslim, in India’s West Bengal most are Hindu. Punjabis include Muslims, Sikhs and Hindus. Some Arabs are Christian. Ethnicity is tied to a shared cultural identity. A religion is tied to shared beliefs and practices.

    🚀 Why did Sunni and Shia Muslims split?

    After the Prophet Muhammad died in 632, Muslims disagreed about who should lead. Some accepted Abu Bakr as leader. Others believed Muhammad’s cousin and son-in-law Ali should lead. Sunni and Shia traditions developed over time from this dispute. Both share belief in one God and the Quran and observe practices such as prayer and fasting. They differ on religious leadership and some practices.

    Shares are approximate: about 85–90% of Muslims are Sunni and 10–15% Shia. Iran, Iraq, Bahrain and Azerbaijan have Shia majorities; Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, Egypt and most others are mainly Sunni.

    🚀 Language families: why aren’t Persian and Arabic “relatives”?

    Persian is written in the Arabic alphabet, but its grammar and basic words are Indo-European, distant cousins of English and Hindi, just as English uses the Latin alphabet but is not Latin.

    👩‍🏫 Teacher / parent notes

    This lesson supports Georgia Grade 7 Social Studies standards SS7G8 and SS7G12. Students distinguish ethnic and religious groups, explore religious diversity in Southwest Asia, compare Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and compare Buddhism, Hinduism, Shinto and Confucianism. The Sunni–Shia split connects to SS7H2.c. The timeline provides practice with BCE and CE dates.

    Mainland Chinese curriculum: Grade 7 Chinese History, Vol. 1 covers Confucius and the Confucian school, Emperor Wu’s support for Confucian learning, and Buddhism’s arrival around the transition between the two Han dynasties. Vol. 2 covers Xuanzang’s journey west, Jianzhen’s journey to Japan, and the emergence of the Hui under the Yuan. Grade 9 World History, Vol. 1 covers ancient India and the founding of Buddhism, the rise of Christianity, and the founding of Islam and the Arab Empire. Lesson numbers vary by edition. Civics covers the constitutional right to freedom of religious belief.

    The timeline and maps use traditional or approximate dates for some events. Abraham’s journey to Canaan is traditionally dated to about 1800 BCE, the Vedic period began around 1500 BCE, and dates for the Buddha’s life vary. Shinto has no exact start date, so the map marks the Kojiki’s completion in 712 CE. Routes are approximate. The “Today” map highlights selected places using estimates from around 2020. The bars use Pew’s 2020 estimates, and the population figures for some ethnic groups in China are rounded from the country’s 2020 census.

    Start with the animation and TED-Ed video, then use the map to trace where traditions began and spread. The Venn diagram invites discussion of how traditions view Jesus and what their food laws say. Follow with BCE and CE practice, the cover-cells review, and the quiz and sorting game.
