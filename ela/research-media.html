Don’t Get Fooled

In 1835, a New York newspaper reported that bat-winged people lived on the Moon, and sold more copies than ever. Today a fake can circle the globe before lunch: a 2018 MIT study found false news stories were about 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones.

Your mission: rate sources like a detective, catch fake headlines, find the edits in a doctored photo and learn the “lateral reading” trick professional fact-checkers use.

Mission 2: Fake-headline spotter

A practice news feed (all outlet names and web addresses are made up). Tap the red flags in the headline and source line (they turn yellow), then decide: probably reliable, or suspicious? For a reliable one, tap no flags at all.

🚩 Common red flags

• ALL CAPS, piles of !!!, “SHOCKING”, “You won’t believe…”

• A strange web address, or one that copies a real outlet (an extra “.co” at the end)

• No author, no date, or old news shared as new

• Too good to be true: “one weird trick to grow 3 inches”

• Satire: labeled “humor” or “satire”, it was never meant as news

Remember: a red flag means “check it”, not “definitely fake”. The real test is lateral reading, below.

    What the research says

    Two widely cited studies: one tested whether students can tell ads from news; the other tracked how true and false stories spread on Twitter.

    Figure 1. Top: Stanford History Education Group, 2016: more than 80% of middle schoolers took an ad labeled “sponsored content” for a real news story. Bottom: Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, Science, 2018 (about 126,000 story cascades on Twitter, 2006–2017): false stories were about 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones, and true stories took about six times as long to reach 1,500 people.
