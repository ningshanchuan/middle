Narrative techniques: flashback, foreshadowing & more

The same events, told a different way, become a different story. An author can start at the end, jump into the past at the tensest moment, or hide a tiny clue on page one that explodes on the last page. Those choices are narrative techniques. This lesson teaches you to spot them and, more importantly, to explain what they DO to the reader.

Your mission: rebuild a story’s timeline, hunt every foreshadowing clue in an original story, change the mood by swapping words, then write a RACE constructed response and a story continuation.

Foreshadowing, payoff, set-up, suspense: what’s the difference?

Memory hintA clue may be easy to miss on the first read. Once you know the ending, you can often see why it was there.
⚠️ Don’t mix up:Gloomy weather or a creepy sound can simply build mood (setting description). Look for a detail that points to something that happens or is revealed later.
📚 Foreshadowing in famous stories (public domain)

Jack London, “To Build a Fire” (1908): early on we learn that an old-timer warned the man never to travel alone when it is colder than 50 degrees below zero Fahrenheit. He ignores it, and that warning is foreshadowing.

O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905): the story tells us early that the couple’s two proudest possessions are Jim’s gold watch and Della’s hair — exactly what each of them later sells.

Mood the reader feels:

Mood vs. tone

Mood is the atmosphere the reader senses: cozy, tense, eerie, joyful. Tone is the author’s or narrator’s attitude toward the subject: sarcastic, admiring, humorous, detached. The same blizzard can be told in a frightened tone or a thrilled one.

语文: what setting description does (环境描写的作用)

    Memory hintConnotation includes the feelings and ideas a word suggests. In 语文, 感情色彩 (gǎnqíng sècǎi) describes whether a word carries a positive, negative or neutral feeling.
    ⚠️ Don’t mix up:Mood is about the reader; tone is about the author. Write “the author uses … to create a … mood”, not “the author is eerie”.

    👩‍🏫 Teacher / parent notes

    This lesson supports Georgia Grade 7 ELA standard 7.T.T.1.b on analyzing plot structure, conflict and devices such as flashback and foreshadowing. It also supports 6-8.T.SS.2.a on how figurative language, connotative language and literary devices shape meaning, mood or tone.

    Not repeated here: the plot mountain and the four conflicts are in “Plot, Conflict & Theme”; direct/indirect characterization (STEAL) and point of view in “Characters & Point of View”; hooks, dialogue punctuation, pacing and show-don’t-tell in “Write a Story”. This page focuses on how techniques create effects.

    Also in China’s curriculum: the unified-edition (统编版) junior-secondary Chinese course teaches 记叙的顺序 (chronological 顺叙, later-event opening 倒叙, inserted flashback 插叙, later addition of omitted information 补叙), foreshadowing and payoff (伏笔与照应), set-up (铺垫), suspense (设置悬念), methods of portraying characters (appearance, speech, action, thoughts, expression) and the functions of setting description. A classic example: in Lu Xun’s “My Old Home” (《故乡》, Grade 9 Vol. 1), the memory of young Runtu is an inserted flashback (插叙). Note: English “flashback” often works as 插叙. 语文 倒叙 means opening with the ending or an important later event, then going back to tell earlier events in order.

    Texts: “The Island Race”, “The Sound in the Attic” and all practice sentences are original to this page. “To Build a Fire” (1908), “The Gift of the Magi” (1905), “The Tell-Tale Heart” (1843) and the Odyssey are public domain; this page uses only our own short summaries.

    Suggested use: have students scrub through the flashback version and say where the story jumps back and why it jumps exactly there; then rewrite the opening ending-first and compare the question each version makes the reader ask (“what happens?” vs. “why?”). The constructed-response checklist works as a ready-made rubric.
