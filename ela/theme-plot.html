Plot, conflict & theme

Would you keep reading a story where nothing goes wrong? Every good story is shaped like a mountain: tension climbs, hits a peak (the climax), then comes back down. And buried under the mountain is a message about life — the theme.

The four kinds of conflict

🧍↔🧍 Character vs. character

Two characters want opposite things: a race, a fight, a rivalry. The Tortoise and the Hare.

🧠 Character vs. self

The fight happens inside: fear, guilt, a hard choice. Nobody else can see the battle.

🌨️ Character vs. nature

The enemy is weather, animals, illness or the wild. In Jack London’s “To Build a Fire” it is 75 degrees below zero °F (about −59 °C).

🏛️ Character vs. society

The character pushes against laws, rules, traditions or what a whole group believes.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:A story can have more than one conflict. Ask: who or what is the main opponent?

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia K-12 English Language Arts Standards (approved 2023, full implementation 2025–26), 6-8.T.T.1 Narrative Techniques: 6.T.T.1.b (how setting, events, conflict and characterization influence plot pacing), 6.T.T.1.c (identify multiple themes), 7.T.T.1.b (plot structures and conflict), 7.T.T.1.c (how themes develop through characters, events and plot structure), 8.T.T.1.b / 8.T.T.1.c.

Also in China’s curriculum: the unified-edition (统编版) junior-secondary Chinese textbooks teach the “three elements of a novel” (character, plot, setting) and a four-part plot — beginning, development, climax, ending (开端—发展—高潮—结局), e.g. Grade 9 Vol. 1 Unit 4. Grade 7 Vol. 1 includes Aesop fables in “Four Fables” (寓言四则; check your printing). Note: Chinese classes usually use four parts; US classes usually use five (adding falling action).

Texts: the Aesop fables are our own short retellings; “The Gift of the Magi” (O. Henry, 1905; collected in The Four Million, 1906) is public domain and quotations follow the 1906 text. The science-fair story is original. Tension values are teaching ratings, not measurements.

Suggested use: after building a mountain, scrub through it with the slider and ask why tension peaks where it does; then have students write a theme statement (topic + the author’s message) for a book they have read.
