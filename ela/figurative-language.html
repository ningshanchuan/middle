Figurative language & poetry

How can fog have “little cat feet”? How can hope have feathers? Poets don’t mean their words literally — they put two things side by side so you suddenly see a feeling. Crack the code and poems open up.

Your mission: identify six devices in real lines of poetry, mark rhyme schemes and tap out rhythm, then build your own figures of speech in the poet’s workshop.

Six devices at a glance

🔗 Simile

Compares two things using like or as: “I wandered lonely as a cloud”.

🪞 Metaphor

Says one thing IS another — no like or as: “All the world’s a stage”.

🧍 Personification

Gives human actions or feelings to something non-human: Death “kindly stopped for me”.

📢 Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration for emphasis: “the shot heard round the world”.

🔤 Alliteration

Repeated starting consonant sounds: “Doubting, dreaming dreams…”.

🔔 Onomatopoeia

A word that sounds like what it means: tinkle, murmuring, buzz.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:Not every “like” is a simile: “I like pizza” compares nothing. A simile compares two different things. (In Chinese, 比喻 covers simile, metaphor and more.)

Mission 2: rhyme & rhythm lab

Rhyme scheme: give lines that rhyme the same letter (the first rhyme sound is A, the next new one B…). Tap a button to change its letter, then check. Rhythm: tap the stressed syllables, then hear the beat.

Tap each syllable you say more strongly — its bar grows. English rhythm is a pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia K-12 ELA Standards (2023): 6-8.T.SS.2.a (how figurative and connotative language and literary devices contribute to meaning, mood or tone); 6-8.T.T.4.a (analyze poetic techniques — stanzas, rhyme scheme, imagery, figurative language, sound devices); 6-8.T.T.4.b (apply poetic techniques to produce poetry).

Also in China’s curriculum: Chinese (语文) teaches rhetorical devices — metaphor/simile (比喻), personification (拟人), hyperbole (夸张), parallelism (排比) — from primary school on; e.g. Grade 7 Vol. 1 Unit 1 (Zhu Ziqing’s “Spring”, Lao She’s “Winter in Jinan”). Poetry appreciation covers imagery (意象), rhyme and rhythm. Key difference: English rhythm comes from stressed syllables; Chinese verse uses tones (平仄) and character counts.

Sources: Wordsworth (1807), Burns (1794), Shakespeare (As You Like It), Dickinson (published from 1891), Frost “The Road Not Taken” (1916) and “Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening” (1923), Sandburg “Fog” (1916), Hughes “The Negro Speaks of Rivers” (1921) and “Mother to Son” (1922), Emerson (1837), Poe (1845, 1849), Tennyson (1847), Coleridge (1798) — all public domain in the US. Chinese renderings are our own. “I’m so hungry I could eat a horse” is a common saying. Stress marks follow a common reading; some syllables can be read differently.
