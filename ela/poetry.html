Prose usually comes in paragraphs and wraps to fit the screen. In verse, poets choose where lines end and can group lines into stanzas with blank space. A sentence can cross several lines. A couplet has two lines, a tercet three, and a quatrain four. Some poems, called prose poems, use paragraphs.

Look for punctuation at sentence endings, line breaks at verse line endings, and blank lines between stanzas.

Hear the rhyme and sound

Build a rhyme scheme

Tap the first ending to mark A. Then listen down the lines. Reuse a letter for a rhyme you’ve heard and use the next letter for a new sound. Tap an ending again to change its letter.

Listen to the stressed vowel and the sounds after it. Night and light match from /aɪ/ through the final /t/. If four lines end in night, light, day, and play, give the first two A. Day starts a new rhyme, B, and play matches it. The scheme is AABB.

Listen for the clue

Common mix-up. Assonance repeats vowel sounds. Consonance repeats consonant sounds. Judge rhymes by ear, too.

Clap the strong beats

Choose a line, say it naturally, and clap the syllables that get more emphasis. Tap those syllables, then compare with one possible reading.

Rhythm is the movement you hear, from strong and soft beats to pauses and changes of pace. In English verse, meter often comes from a regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. A weak–strong pair like a-WAY is an iamb. Free verse has rhythm and can use occasional rhyme, with no fixed meter or rhyme scheme.

Four metrical feet

A foot is a group of syllables. ● marks stress and ○ marks a lighter syllable. A reading can bring out the underlying pattern while letting meaning guide the emphasis.

  • ○● Iamb, weak–strong, as in a-WAY.
  • ●○ Trochee, strong–weak, as in GAR-den.
  • ○○● Anapest, weak–weak–strong, as in in the DARK.
  • ●○○ Dactyl, strong–weak–weak, as in WON-der-ful.

Use the poem to explain its meaning

Build an analysis in three moves. Name the choice, cite the words, and explain how the choice develops a theme. A theme is an idea the poem develops, expressible in a complete sentence.

Imagery can evoke sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch. Tone is the speaker’s attitude conveyed through the words. Mood is the atmosphere the poem creates. Support either label with specific language.

Common mix-up. The poem’s “I” is its speaker. It may be the poet or a voice the poet creates. Start with evidence in the poem.

Look closer at pauses, turns, and symbols

Enjambment carries a phrase across a break, as in Frost’s “sweep / Of easy wind.” An end-stopped line reaches a natural stopping point at its end, as line 13 does. A caesura is a pause within a line, such as the small pause after “lovely.” Punctuation and phrasing both give clues.

The “But” in Sonnet 18 marks a turn, or volta. The first eight lines describe a summer that passes. Starting in line nine, the speaker imagines the person lasting in verse. The final couplet names the power of the poem itself.

Dickinson develops an extended metaphor by giving hope a bird’s song, a storm to weather, and no need for a crumb. A symbol is a concrete detail that can carry a wider meaning. The storm can suggest hardship. Check whether the rest of the poem supports that reading.

Compare poems on what lasts

Read “Nothing Gold Can Stay” with Sonnet 18. Find one fleeting natural image in each, then compare the endings. Frost’s rhymed pairs build a series of losses. Shakespeare turns from loss to what a poem can preserve.

A Tang quatrain and an English quatrain

Both passages sketch a scene in a few words and rhyme on lines two and four. Each turns in the second half. The Tang poem moves from the view to climbing for a wider view. Rossetti starts with a question and then shows the unseen wind through the bending trees.

This Tang quatrain has five characters per line. Regulated Chinese verse commonly has five or seven per line, with rhyme and patterns of traditional level and oblique tones (平仄, píngzè). It can also use parallel phrasing (对仗, duìzhàng), as when the sun and the Yellow River fill matching roles in the first two lines.

Listen to the originals. The Tang poem rhymes 流 with 楼. The English stanza rhymes I with by and gets its rhythm from stressed and unstressed syllables. Its four lines have 5, 5, 8, and 6 syllables.
