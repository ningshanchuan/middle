Text structures

The same facts can be built into five different shapes: description, sequence, compare & contrast, cause & effect, problem & solution. Authors leave clues — “first”, “however”, “as a result”, “to solve this” — called signal words. Find them and you can see the skeleton of any article.

Your mission: work as a signal-word detective to crack five articles, rebuild the Brooklyn Bridge story in five shapes, then use signal words to put scrambled paragraphs back together.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia K-12 ELA Standards (2023): 6-8.T.SS.1.a (how authors modify organizational structures or features to convey meaning and achieve purposes); 7.T.SS.1.c (varied transition words to connect ideas, sentences, paragraphs); 6-8.T.T.2.a (information from text features); 6-8.T.SS.1.b (design texts with a variety of text structures).

Also in China’s curriculum: Chinese (语文) expository-text units teach the order of explanation (时间/空间/逻辑顺序 — time, space, logical order) and methods such as examples, comparison and numbers (Grade 8 Vol. 1 Unit 5, e.g. Mao Yisheng’s “Chinese Stone Arch Bridges”; Grade 8 Vol. 2 Unit 2). Sequence ≈ time order; cause/effect and problem/solution ≈ logical order; compare/contrast ≈ comparison. Fittingly, both that lesson and this one feature bridges.

All articles are original and based on standard facts: the Wrights tested gliders at Kitty Hawk 1900–1902 and the 1905 Flyer III flew for over half an hour; South Florida is the only place alligators and crocodiles live together; “Black Sunday” was 14 April 1935; the US began the Panama Canal in 1904 and it opened in 1914, after Gorgas’s campaign ended yellow fever there; the Okefenokee covers about 438,000 acres; the Brooklyn Bridge towers rise about 276 ft, the main span is 1,595 ft, each main cable has 5,434 wires, and it opened 24 May 1883.
