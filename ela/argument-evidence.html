Claims, evidence & reasoning

Should school start later? Everyone has an opinion — but an opinion isn’t an argument. A strong argument needs a clear claim, evidence that is strong and relevant, and reasoning that connects them. Weigh every piece of evidence like a judge.

Your mission: weigh the evidence on the argument scale for a later school start, X-ray an opinion article, then catch six common reasoning traps.

Mission 1: the argument scale

One piece of “evidence” appears at a time. Decide: does it go on the side FOR the claim, on the AGAINST side (a counterclaim), or should it be thrown out (irrelevant, just an opinion, not evidence at all)? Then the scale tips by its real weight: expert advice and research data are heavy; hearsay is light.

Three tests for good evidence

🎯 Is it relevant?

Does it actually connect to the claim? Friday pizza has nothing to do with start times.

💪 Is it strong?

Data, studies and expert advice outweigh one person’s story. One cousin’s experience doesn’t represent every student.

🏛️ Is it credible?

Who is the source? The American Academy of Pediatrics is an organization of doctors; “someone online said” is not.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:A counterclaim isn’t “bad evidence” — it’s the other side’s fair point. Strong arguments admit it, then answer it with a rebuttal.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia K-12 ELA Standards (2023): 6-8.T.T.3.a (argumentative techniques — the author’s claim, relevant evidence, an identified counterclaim, in grade 8 a rebuttal, and a logical conclusion); 6.T.C.2.b / 7.T.C.2.b (how choice of evidence reveals perspective and affects credibility); 6-8.T.RA.2 (locate and evaluate evidence and source credibility).

Also in China’s curriculum: Chinese (语文) argumentative essays have three elements — 论点 (claim), 论据 (evidence: factual or reasoned) and 论证 (the reasoning, by example, principle, contrast or analogy) — taught in Grade 9 Vol. 1 Units 2 and 5. CER’s “reasoning” roughly equals 论证.
