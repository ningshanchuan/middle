Characters & point of view

“Why will you say that I am mad?” Edgar Allan Poe’s narrator asks you this in his very first sentence. But can you trust him? Authors rarely tell you what a character is like — they make you collect the evidence from what the character says, thinks and does. And who tells the story decides how much you get to know.

Your mission: collect evidence like a detective in “The Tell-Tale Heart”, switch the point of view and watch a story change, then track Scrooge from miser to good man.

Verdict: an unreliable narrator

He insists he is sane, yet claims to hear heaven and hell and plans harm because of an eye. A first-person narrator can only tell us what he believes — the reader has to judge what is true.

🕵️ STEAL: five ways authors reveal character

S Speech · T Thoughts · E Effect on others · A Actions · L Looks. These are all indirect characterization: the author shows, you infer.

📢 Direct characterization: the author just tells you

Jack London, “To Build a Fire” (1908): “He was a newcomer in the land, a chechaquo, and this was his first winter. The trouble with him was that he was without imagination.” The narrator states the trait outright.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia K-12 ELA Standards (2023): 6-8.T.T.1.a (how narrative techniques develop plot, characters and setting); 6-8.T.C.2 Authors & Speakers (6.T.C.2.a two or more perspectives in one text; 7.T.C.2.a the prevailing perspective); K-12.P.EICC.3.f (make and support inferences).

Also in China’s curriculum: Chinese (语文) classes teach character-description methods — speech, action, expression, thoughts and appearance (语言、动作、神态、心理、外貌描写), almost a one-to-one match with STEAL — and narrative person (第一人称/第三人称) and its effect, e.g. Maupassant’s “My Uncle Jules” told by the boy Joseph (Grade 9 Vol. 1, Unit 4); Grade 7 Vol. 2 Unit 3 portrays “ordinary people”.

Texts: “The Tell-Tale Heart” follows the original January 1843 Pioneer printing (Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore); Chinese versions are our own translations. “To Build a Fire” is the 1908 version; A Christmas Carol (1843), Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865), Moby-Dick (1851) and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) are public domain. The basketball tryout scene is original.

Suggested use: the excerpt stops before any violence; CommonLit offers the full story at grade 8. Have students rewrite a paragraph of their own in another point of view and explain what the reader can now know — or no longer know.
