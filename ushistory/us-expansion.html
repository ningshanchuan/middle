Westward Expansion: from sea to shining sea

In 1800 the United States stopped at the Mississippi River. By 1853 it reached the Pacific Ocean. How did a country more than triple in size in about 50 years — and who paid the price?
Your mission: drag the years to see how each piece of land was gained, then lead a wagon train 2,000 miles (3,200 km) to Oregon before the snow closes the mountains.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence (GSE) for Social Studies, Grade 4, SS4H3 — explain the causes and motivations of westward expansion and its impact on American Indians (Louisiana Purchase, Lewis & Clark, War of 1812, Trail of Tears, Texas, Oregon Trail, California Gold Rush); geography SS4G1–SS4G2 (Erie Canal; how geography shaped expansion); economics SS4E1 (opportunity cost and price incentives — the trade-offs in the wagon mission). Grade 8 Georgia Studies SS8H4 covers Cherokee removal and Worcester v. Georgia. (Standard codes checked against Cobb County’s 2026 Grade 4 framework, which follows the 2016 GSE.)

China link: the national textbook World History has no lesson on westward expansion; it connects to Grade 9 Vol. 1 “American Independence” and Grade 9 Vol. 2 “The American Civil War” (whether new western states would allow slavery was a main cause of the war). The “Meanwhile in China” box matches Chinese History Grade 8 Vol. 1, Unit 1 (Opium War, Treaty of Nanjing, Treaty of Wangxia, Taiping movement).

Notes: the map is schematic — each acquisition is drawn approximately over modern state borders. Areas follow the U.S. Census Bureau acquisitions table (land + water, rounded). Prices, mileages and odds in the wagon mission are approximate, based on emigrant guidebooks and historians’ estimates, for simulation only. Death tolls (about 4,000 on the Trail of Tears; up to 1 in 10 emigrants) are common estimates and vary between sources. Discussion idea: what did the same piece of land mean to an American settler, a Native nation and a Mexican family?
