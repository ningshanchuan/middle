From the Cold War to today: rockets, walls and the internet

In 1957 a beeping metal ball the size of a beach ball circled Earth every 96 minutes — and Americans panicked. Twelve years later, two Americans walked on the Moon. The same rivalry gave birth to the internet’s ancestor. The US and the Soviet Union never fought each other directly, yet this Cold War (冷战) changed everything. How?

Your mission: start as a Mission Control trainee. Find where the space race and the internet were born, rebuild the space-race and internet timeline, then steer the world through the 13 days of the Cuban Missile Crisis.

Simulation: the 13 days of the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962)

In October 1962, a US U-2 spy plane photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites being built in Cuba — only about 90 miles (145 km) from Florida. First work out what the missiles could reach, then take President Kennedy’s seat and decide. Push the tension too high and the world slides toward nuclear war.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence for Social Studies (2016), Grade 5 — SS5H5: discuss the origins and consequences of the Cold War (e.g. the Iron Curtain, the Berlin airlift, the Korean War, NATO, the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Vietnam War); SS5H6: key developments 1950–1975 (including the space race); SS5H7: trace important developments in America 1975–2001 (collapse of the Soviet Union, the Persian Gulf War, the attacks of 11 September 2001, personal computers and the internet). Mapping checked against Cobb County’s May 2026 Grade 5 framework (2016 GSE); check element wording against the Georgia DOE document.

China’s curriculum: national unified-edition World History, Grade 9 Volume 2, Unit 5 “The Cold War and the US–Soviet standoff” and Unit 6 “Towards a world of peace and development” (the post-Cold War world, information technology); lesson numbers vary by printing. Chinese students meet these events from another angle — the Korean War, for example, is called the “War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea” (抗美援朝战争) in China.

Notes: the map is schematic (modern state borders; Cuba’s outline is hand-drawn). Missile ranges are approximate (R-12 about 2,000 km, R-14 about 4,000 km). 9/11 is covered briefly and factually — be mindful of students’ feelings. Internet data from the World Bank; other facts follow standard US sources (NASA, National Archives) and were written from the author’s knowledge — teachers may wish to check exact dates.

Suggested use: students use the slider to build a three-column US–USSR–China timeline; use the range calculator to discuss why “90 miles” was so frightening; then discuss what was “cold” about the Cold War — and where it was actually hot.
