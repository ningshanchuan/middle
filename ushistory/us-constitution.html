The Constitution & Bill of Rights: an instruction manual for power

In the summer of 1787, 55 men shut the windows of a hot room in Philadelphia and argued for almost four months. Their puzzle: how do you make a government strong enough to get things done — but not so strong that it turns into a king?
Your mission: diagnose what was broken in the old Articles of Confederation, strike the deals at the Convention, then build the Constitution yourself — split power among three branches, defend it in a checks-and-balances game, and use the Bill of Rights on real-life cases.

Mission 1: what was broken in the Articles of Confederation? (1781–1789)

After the Revolution, the 13 states joined in a loose “league of friendship” under the Articles: one Congress, no President, no national courts. Pick a problem, see what happened under the old rules, then fix it with the Constitution.

Why did Americans make the national government so weak on purpose?

They had just fought a war against a king and a Parliament far away that taxed them and sent soldiers. The thing they feared most was a strong, distant government — so almost all power stayed with the states.

Shays’ Rebellion: the last straw

In 1786–87, debt-ridden farmers in western Massachusetts, led by war veteran Daniel Shays, shut down courts and in January 1787 marched on the arsenal at Springfield. Congress had no army to send; the state paid for a militia with money from merchants. Leaders were alarmed: the nation needed a stronger government.

🇨🇳 Meanwhile in China1781–1787 were years 46–52 of the Qianlong Emperor (乾隆) of the Qing dynasty. In 1782 the first set of the giant book collection Siku Quanshu (《四库全书》) was finished. In Qing China one person — the emperor — made the laws, ran the government and was the highest judge. Nobody could “veto” the emperor.

What exactly was the Three-Fifths Compromise?

About 700,000 people were enslaved, most of them in the South. Southern delegates wanted them counted fully so their states would get more House seats (the enslaved people themselves, of course, could not vote). Northern delegates objected. The deal: for House seats and direct taxes, every 5 enslaved people counted as 3. This gave slaveholding states extra seats in Congress. The Constitution also said Congress could not ban the import of enslaved people before 1808. Only after the Civil War did the 13th Amendment (1865) end slavery and the 14th Amendment (1868) end the three-fifths count.

Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists: what was the fight?

Federalists (Hamilton, Madison) said the nation needed a stronger central government, and that separation of powers and checks and balances would stop tyranny. Anti-Federalists (Patrick Henry, George Mason) feared it would crush the states and people’s freedoms — there wasn’t even a list of rights! The solution: ratify first, then add a Bill of Rights.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:The Great (Connecticut) Compromise settled big states vs. small states: House by population, Senate 2 per state. The Three-Fifths Compromise was about how enslaved people were counted — it did not give them three-fifths of a vote; they could not vote at all.
