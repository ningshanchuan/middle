The Civil Rights Movement: how ordinary people changed the law

In 1954, the laws of 17 states said Black and white children must go to separate schools. By 1965, Congress had outlawed segregation (种族隔离) in public places and protected Black citizens’ right to vote. No army, no weapons — so who made that happen, and how?

Your mission: become a civil-rights history detective. Track the movement across the map, crack 8 case files, then run a bus boycott yourself.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:Brown v. Board (1954) was a Supreme Court decision that school segregation is unconstitutional. The Civil Rights Act (1964) and the Voting Rights Act (1965) were laws passed by Congress and signed by the President. A ruling doesn’t change everything overnight — many schools stayed segregated for years after 1954.

📊 Data: Black voter registration after the Voting Rights Act

Before federal protection, many Southern states used “literacy tests”, poll taxes and threats to keep Black citizens from registering to vote. See what changed after the 1965 law.

Data (rounded): Mississippi 6.7% (1964) → 66.5% (1969); Alabama 19.3% (1964) → 61.3% (1969); all areas covered by the Act 29.3% (1965) → 52.1% (1967). Source: U.S. Commission on Civil Rights and Justice Department figures, as summarized in Wikipedia’s “Voting Rights Act of 1965”.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence for Social Studies (2016), Grade 5, SS5H6 — the importance of key people, events and developments between 1950 and 1975 (Brown v. Board of Education, the Montgomery Bus Boycott, the March on Washington, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965; Thurgood Marshall, Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King Jr.; locate Montgomery, AL). Related: SS5CG1–SS5CG3 (how citizens’ rights are protected under the Constitution, how amendments are made, how amendments such as the 24th — ending poll taxes in federal elections — maintained a representative democracy). Georgia Studies (Grade 8) SS8H11: civil rights in Georgia (Atlanta, the Albany Movement, Dr. King’s Atlanta roots). Mapping checked against Cobb County’s May 2026 Grade 5 framework (2016 GSE).

China’s curriculum: there is no dedicated lesson on the US civil rights movement in the national textbooks; it can be connected to the World History Grade 9 Volume 2 lesson on post-war changes in capitalist countries. For Chinese learners, key terms carry Chinese in English mode and English in Chinese mode.

Notes: the 1954 map is simplified by state (17 states plus D.C. required segregated schools; Arizona, Kansas, New Mexico and Wyoming allowed local option) and is schematic. Rider numbers in the boycott calculator are approximate and only illustrate economic pressure. Violence (the Birmingham church bombing, Dr. King’s assassination) is stated factually without detail; prepare students before discussing it.

Suggested use: students drag the slider and fill in a “place – strategy – result” table, then consolidate with the case files. Discussion: why did nonviolence (非暴力) win support across the nation?
