How the Internet works: a packet’s trip around the world

You tap a video stored on a server about 11,000 km (7,000 miles) away — and it starts playing in under a second. Nobody “drives” it. The video is chopped into thousands of small pieces, and each piece finds its own way through routers, undersea cables and your home Wi-Fi, then gets glued back together on your screen. How do they not get lost?

Your mission: start as a network intern. Send a message from Atlanta to Beijing and cut cables on purpose to watch packets reroute; look up the IP address behind a name; race copper, fiber and Wi-Fi; then fix five broken networks to become Chief Network Engineer.

🔢 An IP address is really binary

An IPv4 address is four numbers from 0–255. Each one is 8 bits, so 32 bits in all.

4.3 billionIPv4 addresses (2³²)
8.2 billionpeople on Earth (≈2025)
So what happens when addresses run out?

One person often has a phone, a laptop and a tablet… IPv4 ran short years ago. The answer is IPv6: 128 bits, about 3.4×10³⁸ addresses — enough to give every grain of sand on Earth many of its own. China is rolling out IPv6 fast.

Why is the second lookup so fast?

The resolver remembers the answer in its cache for a while. Next time anyone asks for that name, it answers at once instead of starting again from the root servers.

Sim 3: how bits travel — electricity, light and radio

All data ends up as 0s and 1s. Copper wire sends them as high or low voltage, fiber as flashes of light, Wi-Fi as changes in a radio wave. Type a letter, pick a “road” and a distance.

🚚 Speed ≠ bandwidth: how long to download a 5 GB game?

Signals race through copper and fiber at about the same speed (roughly two-thirds the speed of light). The real difference is bandwidth: how many bits per second fit — like the number of lanes on a highway.

Bandwidths are typical round numbers; real speeds vary. 5 GB = 40 billion bits (1 byte = 8 bits).
