Algorithms: sorting & searching

A search engine finds answers among billions of pages in under a second. That takes more than a fast computer — it takes a smart algorithm: a step-by-step method for solving a problem. How big is the gap between a smart and a slow algorithm? Finding one name among a million: the slow way can take a million looks; the smart way never needs more than 20.

Your mission: start as a Shuffler. Race three sorting algorithms, crack a secret number with binary search, design the rules for a maze robot — then BE the algorithm: put mystery boxes in order from light to heavy using a balance scale and as few weighings as possible. Top rank: Big-O Master.

Hint: the right-hand rule

Keep your right hand on the wall: turn right if you can, else go straight, else turn left, else turn around. In a maze without loops this always reaches the exit — but rarely by the shortest route.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

US standards: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017) 2-AP-10 (use flowcharts and/or pseudocode to address complex problems as algorithms), 2-AP-13 (decompose problems into parts); also 2-AP-17 (test with a range of cases — new data, new mazes). Georgia K-12 Computer Science Standards (grades 6–8): CSS.KC.6-8.18 (a problem can have multiple solutions), 19 (solve problems iteratively), 20.5 (implement a simple algorithm); CSS.CT.6-8.32.1 (decomposition, pattern recognition, algorithmic thinking, abstraction), 35.2 (Boolean operators to improve searches), 36.1–36.3 (algorithmic problem-solving of rising complexity). Codes as listed in GaDOE’s 6–8 CS framework (via CodeHS); check wording against the GaDOE document.

China’s curriculum: Compulsory Education Information Technology Curriculum Standard (2022), core strand “algorithms” (算法). Stage 3, grade 5 module “Algorithms around us” (身边的算法: describe algorithms in words and flowcharts, feel the difference in efficiency, e.g. searching and sorting); Stage 4 (grades 7–9) applies algorithms in programming projects. Textbook editions and lesson numbers vary.

Notes: in the race, “steps” = comparisons + moves (swaps or writes); bubble sort stops early when a pass makes no swaps; merge sort is the bottom-up version. The chart shows worst-case counts from formulas, not measured times; “1 billion steps per second” is only an assumption for comparison. The minimum worst-case comparisons for sorting 4, 5, 6 and 8 items (5, 7, 10, 16) are known results, written from the author’s knowledge.

Suggested use: start unplugged — students act out bubble and selection sort with playing cards and count steps. Use the guessing game to discuss why doubling the list adds only one guess. In the maze, have students find a rule order that always works and discuss “always works” vs “fastest”. After the main mission, ask students to explain their strategy and compare it with merge sort.
