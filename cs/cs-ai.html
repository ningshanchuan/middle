Artificial intelligence: teach a machine to learn

Your phone can tell a cat from a dog in a photo — yet nobody ever wrote it a rule for “cat”. So how does it know? It looked at thousands of examples and found the pattern itself. That is machine learning (机器学习). Today, you are the trainer.

Your mission: train a fruit-sorting AI until it scores well on test fruit it has never seen; catch bias and bad labels in its training data; then build a tiny next-word chatbot and find out why it can sound confident and still be wrong.

Simulation: train a fruit classifier

The AI can’t see fruit. It only gets two numbers — features (特征): size and colour. Each dot is one fruit. You label examples; when a new fruit arrives, the AI finds the k closest examples and lets them vote. That is the k-nearest-neighbour algorithm (k-NN). The background colours are its “map” of what it would guess anywhere.

Training set vs test set — what’s the difference?

Training data (训练数据) are the labelled examples you give the AI to learn from. The test set is 64 other fruit it has never seen. Only a good score on the test set shows it really learned — and didn’t just memorise.

What is k? Is a bigger k always better?

With k = 1 the AI trusts only its single closest example — one mislabelled dot can fool it. With k = 5, five neighbours vote, so a few mistakes get outvoted. But if k is too big, borders blur and small groups get swamped by big ones.

Do real AIs work like this?

Same idea: features + labelled examples → model → prediction. Real image AIs use neural networks (神经网络) that find thousands of features in each photo by themselves and train on millions of pictures. But they, too, only learn what is in their data.

What is bias (偏差)?

If a group of people or things is missing or rare in the training data, the AI makes more mistakes on them — not on purpose, but because the data was incomplete. Some face-recognition systems were less accurate for darker-skinned faces because their training photos had too few of them. The fix: more complete, diverse data — and check accuracy group by group.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:Scoring 100% on its own training examples ≠ learned. An AI can “memorise” the examples and still fail on new data (overfitting, 过拟合). Always test it on data it hasn’t seen.

Is AI intelligent the way you are?

No. You saw two or three cats and can recognise every cat — and you know cats get hungry and scared. An AI needs thousands of examples and only knows statistical patterns, not understanding. It can beat people at narrow tasks like chess or labelling photos, but it can’t switch to a new task the way you can.

Who is responsible for an AI’s decisions?

People are. Those who design, train and use an AI must check that it is fair, safe and respects privacy. That’s why many countries, including China and the US, are writing rules for AI.

Why does it sound sure and still talk nonsense?

It only knows how words link, not what sentences mean or whether they’re true. “The moon is a planet” is a smooth chain of likely words — and false. Real chatbots learned from trillions of words, so they sound far more human, but they can still invent confident, wrong answers — called hallucination (幻觉). Check important facts in reliable sources.

How far is this from a real large language model?

Ours looks back one word and stores a few dozen pairs. A large language model uses a neural network that looks back over thousands of tokens (词元) with billions to trillions of parameters. Same core job: predict the next token.
