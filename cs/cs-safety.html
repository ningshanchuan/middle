Cyber defender: lock it, spot it, stop it

A password-cracking computer can try 10 billion guesses a second. How long would it need for yours? Scammers often don’t bother cracking — they just trick you into handing your password over. That’s phishing (网络钓鱼). The first line of cyber defence isn’t software. It’s you.

Your mission: build a password even a supercomputer can’t guess; catch the phishing traps in 7 messages and rise from Rookie to Cyber Defender; then make smart, kind choices in 8 real online situations.

    Advanced: the entropy formula, the attacker’s speed and time to crack
    Lines = fully random passwords: entropy = length × log₂(number of symbols). Dashed line = bits needed for an average crack time of 100 years at the current speed. Big dot = your practice password (the meter’s estimate).
    🎯 Challenge The attacker has a huge cracking cluster: 1 trillion (10¹²) guesses a second. In the meter above, build a practice password of at most 16 characters that takes at least 1,000 years on average.
    Why does length beat weird symbols?

    Each character multiplies the number of possibilities. “Fz9!q” has only 5 characters — about 30 bits however weird it looks. A five-word passphrase has 25+ characters: far harder to guess and easier to remember.

    Two locks: what is two-factor authentication?

    Two-factor authentication (2FA, 双重验证) asks for something you know (your password) plus something you have (a code on your phone or a security key). Even if your password is stolen, the thief can’t get in without the second key.

    What’s wrong with one password everywhere?

    If one small site gets hacked and leaks your password, attackers try it on your email, games and other accounts. Use a different password for each important account — a password manager (set up with a parent) can remember them.

    How accurate is this meter?

    It’s an estimate: it takes points off for common passwords, dictionary words, runs like abc/123/qwerty and years. 10 billion guesses a second is the worst case — an attacker who stole a site’s password file guessing offline. Websites usually limit login attempts, so online guessing is far slower.

    ⚠️ Don’t mix up:Swapping letters for look-alike symbols (a→@, o→0) barely helps — cracking programs try those swaps first. What really works: length + randomness + never reusing.
