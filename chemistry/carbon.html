Diamond, graphite and C₆₀ are allotropes (同素异形体, tóngsù yìxíngtǐ), different pure forms of carbon. Their atoms are arranged differently, which gives them different properties. A pencil core usually contains a mixture of graphite and clay.

Carbon takes oxygen from copper oxide

Elemental carbon is generally stable at room temperature. At high temperatures it can react with oxygen or take oxygen from some metal oxides.

0%

Follow the oxygen atoms

2 CuO(s) + C(s) —Δ→ 2 Cu(s) + CO₂(g)

Black copper(II) oxide loses oxygen and forms reddish copper. That is reduction. Carbon gains oxygen and forms carbon dioxide, so carbon is oxidized. Carbon is the reducing agent.

Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide

Both gases have one carbon atom per molecule, but they have different numbers of oxygen atoms and very different properties. Choose a gas and see what happens when it passes through clear limewater.

CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)

Like elemental carbon in the model above, CO can take oxygen from heated copper(II) oxide. The oxide is reduced to copper, and CO gains oxygen to form CO₂.

CO poisoning happens when CO binds to hemoglobin, the protein in blood that carries oxygen. This interferes with oxygen delivery and can cause headaches, dizziness and even death. If a CO alarm sounds or you suspect poisoning, get to fresh air outside immediately and call emergency services from there (911 in the US).

A high concentration of CO₂ can be dangerous too. Smell cannot identify either gas.

Reagents, collection and testing

CaCO₃(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑

Limestone or marble reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid without heating. At the same temperature and pressure, CO₂ is denser than air. Feed it near the bottom of an upright jar so it displaces air upward. Some CO₂ dissolves in water, so collection over water loses some gas.

The first gas out contains air from inside the apparatus. To identify CO₂, bubble the gas through clear limewater and look for cloudiness. When collecting by air displacement, hold a lit splint at the jar’s mouth to check whether the jar is full. In this preparation, the splint going out indicates that CO₂ has reached the opening.

The carbon cycle and greenhouse effect

Carbon moves among the atmosphere, living things, the ocean and rocks. Energy also moves between the surface and atmosphere. Follow each in its own diagram.

Trace where carbon goes

Some ocean carbon becomes part of sediments and rocks and can stay there for a long time.

Trace infrared radiation

The surface absorbs sunlight and emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases such as CO₂ absorb and re-emit some infrared radiation. The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm. Added CO₂ strengthens it. Arrows show direction and relative change.

Combustion, extinguishing and fuels

Turn off each condition in the fire triangle and see when the flame goes out. Turn conditions back on to test another combination.

Fire triangle

A fire goes out when fuel is removed or cut off, oxygen is cut off, or the burning material cools below its ignition temperature. Cooling changes the fuel’s current temperature. Each toggle sets up a new situation in the model.

A lid limits the oxygen supply to a small grease fire. Water can splash burning oil and spread the fire. If there is a fire, get to safety and call the fire department.

Carbon and energy in fuels

Combustion releases energy as heat, which can warm buildings, cook food or help generate electricity. Coal, petroleum and natural gas are fossil fuels. They form too slowly to be replaced on human timescales. Methane is the main component of natural gas.

Fuel choices also depend on usable energy, air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. Wind and solar generate electricity without burning fuel. Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water. Producing hydrogen requires energy.

Practice with worked solutions

Reduction: how much copper can 8.0 g of CuO yield?

Assume excess carbon and complete conversion. Use rounded molar masses of 80 g/mol for CuO and 64 g/mol for Cu. The equation gives a 1:1 mole ratio of CuO to Cu.

8.0 g ÷ (80 g/mol) = 0.10 mol
0.10 mol × 64 g/mol = 6.4 g

The theoretical yield is about 6.4 g of copper.

Extension — limiting reactant: how much CO₂ can 0.20 mol CaCO₃ and 0.30 mol HCl produce?

To react all 0.20 mol of CaCO₃ would take 0.40 mol of HCl, so HCl runs out first. Each 2 mol of HCl can produce 1 mol of CO₂.

0.30 mol HCl × (1 mol CO₂ / 2 mol HCl) = 0.15 mol CO₂
0.15 mol × 44 g/mol = 6.6 g

The maximum is 0.15 mol of CO₂, or about 6.6 g.

Evidence: if an unknown gas puts out a lit splint, does that identify it as CO₂?

No. Nitrogen also fails to sustain a burning splint. For the two gases compared here, CO₂ and CO, the first appearance of cloudiness in limewater distinguishes them.
