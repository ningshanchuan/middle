Every layer has the same volume, but not the same mass. More mass in the same volume = higher density = lower in the column.

⚠️ Common mix-up:“Heavy things sink and light things float” is wrong! What matters is whether the object’s density is greater or less than the liquid’s. A big log is heavy but floats; a small steel nut is light but sinks.

Why does ice float?

The same substance has different densities in different states. For most substances the solid is denser than the liquid — but water is a famous exception.

🧊

Ice: 0.92 g/cm³

When water freezes, its molecules lock into an open, hexagon-shaped pattern with lots of space, so it expands by about 9%. Ice is less dense than water and floats — about 90% of an iceberg is underwater.

💧

Liquid water: 1.00 g/cm³

In liquid water the molecules are close together and slide past each other. Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down, and fish survive the winter in the water below.

♨️

Steam: about 0.0006 g/cm³

Gas particles are very far apart, so gases have tiny densities. The same mass of water takes up more than 1,600 times more space as steam!

💡 Memory hintThe more tightly packed the particles, the higher the density: usually solid > liquid > gas (ice and water are the exception).

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports GSE S8P1b: plan and carry out investigations to compare physical properties of matter such as density, melting point and boiling point. Liquid densities in the 3D column are typical values: honey 1.42, corn syrup 1.33, dish soap 1.06, water 1.00, vegetable oil 0.92, rubbing alcohol 0.79 g/cm³; an object between two layers sits partly in each, in proportion to the densities. Suggested activities: (1) have students predict where each object will settle before dropping it; (2) use the custom-object slider to find the density range that hovers between oil and water; (3) in the displacement lab, stress reading the bottom of the meniscus and discuss why the floating wax must be pushed under with a pin. Common misconceptions: “heavy things sink”, “cutting an object changes its density”, “thicker liquids are always denser”.
