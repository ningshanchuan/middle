Probability: putting a number on chance

Why is the chance of heads ½? Why is 7 the most common total with two dice? And if 10 flips give 7 heads, is ½ still right? Let’s count, draw, and then really flip thousands of times.

⚠️ Common mix-up:A coin has no memory: after 5 heads in a row, the next flip is still ½ heads. And two coins have 4 equally likely outcomes (HH, HT, TH, TT), not 3, so “one head and one tail” has probability ½, not ⅓.

Advanced: expected counts, how big the error is, the law of large numbers

Pink funnel on the graph = theoretical p ± 2√(p(1−p)/n): about 95% of the time the experimental probability stays inside it. 100× more trials makes the funnel only 10× narrower: the error shrinks like 1/√n.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports Georgia 7.PR (probability as a number from 0 to 1; sample spaces with lists, tree diagrams and tables; theoretical vs experimental probability; using probability to make predictions). Step 3 targets the common belief that two coins have only 3 outcomes. Before steps 5–6, have every student flip a coin 10 times and pool the class results: individual results vary a lot, but the class total lands near ½. The simulators use real random numbers, so every run differs; discuss why 100 flips rarely give exactly 50 heads.
