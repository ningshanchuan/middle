Cylinders, Cones & Spheres: where do ⅓ and ⁴⁄₃ come from?

Would you rather have an ice-cream cone filled to the top, or a cup with the same width and height? How much more is in the cup? Stack some coins, pour water from a cone, and drop a ball into a can. Three simple experiments reveal three volume formulas, and a surprising ratio: 1 : 2 : 3.

⚠️ Common mix-up:The formulas use the radius r, not the diameter (a 10 cm diameter means r = 5 cm). The height h of a cone is the straight-up height from the tip to the base, not the slanted length along its side.

Challenge: why is the sphere exactly ⅔ of the cylinder?

Slice both solids at the same height z. Left: the sphere’s slice is a disk. Right: a cylinder with two cones scooped out leaves a ring. Drag the slider and compare the two slice areas.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports Georgia 8.GSR (apply the volume formulas for cylinders, cones and spheres to solve real-world problems). After step 4, have students guess what fraction of the cylinder the cone holds before playing the pour. If possible, repeat the pour with paper cups and rice. Steps 7–9 tie the sphere to a cylinder of height 2r, which helps students remember the ⁴⁄₃. The “Why 4/3?” section uses the Pythagorean theorem from the previous lesson and works well as an extension. Remind students to tell radius from diameter and height from slant height, and that volume is measured in cubic units.
