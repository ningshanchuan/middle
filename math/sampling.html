Sampling: see the whole jar from one handful

A new game has millions of players. How can asking only 1,000 of them tell you what they all think? What fraction of the beads in a jar are red? You don’t have to count them all: a random handful gives an estimate. But every handful is different, bigger handfuls are steadier, and if the way you grab is biased, no amount of grabbing will save you.

⚠️ Common mix-up:A big sample is not the same as a good sample. If the method is biased (asking only your friends, scooping only from the top of the jar), even a huge sample will be off in the same direction. The key to a random sample is that every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.

Compare two groups: is the difference real?

Twelve plants are chosen at random for each fertilizer and their heights (cm) are measured. Use the slider to set the true difference between the fertilizers, then sample again and again. Compare the difference of the means with the spread (MAD): a gap of about 2 MADs or more usually means the groups really differ.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports Georgia 7.DSR (using random samples to make inferences about a population; recognising that samples of the same size vary; comparing two populations using measures of center and variability). The dot plots in steps 3–5 are the heart: ask students to predict whether samples of 80 will give a wider or narrower pile. In the bead jar the red beads are deliberately concentrated near the top, so students see that random sampling still works on an unmixed population, while a convenience “scoop from the top” overestimates every time. The first two handfuls in the lesson are fixed so the narration can quote them; the many-sample dot plots and everything in Explore use real randomness, so they differ every time the page is opened.
