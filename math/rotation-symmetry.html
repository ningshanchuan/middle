A rotation turns a plane figure about a fixed point. To describe it, give the center, direction, and angle. The center stays in place. Every other point turns the same way by the same angle and stays the same distance from the center. The original figure and its image are congruent. Corresponding sides and angles stay equal.

Does a half turn line up?

Predict first, then turn. Check whether the outlines and colors match.

Two figures are centrally symmetric about O if a half turn about O takes one onto the other. A centrally symmetric figure matches itself after a half turn. O is the center of symmetry. If a point and its image are distinct, O is the midpoint of the segment joining them.

Common mix-upThe three-blade pinwheel in the model matches at 120°. Central symmetry requires a match at 180°. For letters, test the actual letter shape.

Why each shape belongs

A segment’s endpoints swap. In rectangles and rhombuses, the diagonals bisect each other, so the half turn swaps both pairs of opposite corners. Each vertex of a regular octagon moves four steps to its opposite vertex.

An equilateral triangle and a regular pentagon have odd numbers of vertices, so the vertices can’t all be paired with an opposite one. A semicircle’s arc moves to the other side of its diameter. The modeled T’s top bar moves to the bottom.

Turn a point around the origin

Tap a grid intersection to place P, or set its coordinates with the sliders. Predict where P′ will land before you choose a turn.

For a 90° counterclockwise turn, right becomes up and up becomes left. For a 90° clockwise turn, right becomes down and up becomes right. For a half turn, take the opposite of both coordinates.

Worked example: two turns from the same point

Start with P(3, −2). A 90° counterclockwise turn about the origin sends 3 units right to 3 units up and 2 units down to 2 units right.

Given P(3, −2), find the point P′ symmetric to P about the origin O.

Use the half-turn rule and take the opposite of each coordinate.

The image is P′(−3, 2). The midpoint of PP′ is O.

Worked example: three distances inside an equilateral triangle

Rotate triangle ABP counterclockwise 60° about A. B lands on C and P lands on Q. This carries the length BP to CQ.

Given equilateral triangle ABC with AP = 3 cm, BP = 4 cm, and CP = 5 cm. Q is the image of P under the 60° counterclockwise turn about A.

Find the measure of ∠PQC.

  1. The rotation preserves lengths, so
  2. ∠PAQ is 60° and AP = AQ, so APQ is equilateral.
  3. In triangle PQC, the square of the longest side PC equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

By the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, the right angle in triangle PQC is opposite the longest side, PC. So ∠PQC = 90°.

Design a rosette

6

Divide a full turn into n equal steps and place a copy of the motif at each step. Each motif has one outer tip. The pattern matches itself at exactly n positions in a full turn, counting the starting position once. This is n-fold rotational symmetry. When n is even, a half turn is a whole number of steps, so the rosette also has central symmetry.

Compose two reflections

35°

Positive angles turn counterclockwise. Negative angles turn clockwise.

Challenge: Make the result a 90° counterclockwise turn about O. Which angle and order work?

Check your result

Set β to 45° and reflect in ℓ₁ first. Reversing the order gives a 90° clockwise turn.

Reflecting across two intersecting lines produces a rotation about their intersection. Turn from the first line to the second through the smaller angle, then double that turn. Perpendicular lines give a half turn in either direction.

Why twice the angle?

Measure directed angles from horizontal ℓ₁. A ray at angle α goes to −α after the first reflection. Reflecting that ray across ℓ₂ at angle β gives the direction below. Both reflections also preserve distance to O.

Distinct parallel mirrors give a translation. Reflecting twice across the same line returns every point to its starting position.
