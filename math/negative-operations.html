Operations with Negatives: why is negative × negative positive?

Your game score is 20 and the referee takes away a 5-point penalty. Does your score go up or down? “Subtracting a negative is adding.” “A negative times a negative is positive.” These rules are not arbitrary. With walks on a number line, plus and minus chips, and one simple pattern, we will see why they have to be true.

⚠️ Common mix-up:“Two negatives make a positive” is a rule for multiplying and dividing only! Adding is different: (−3) + (−4) = −7, two negatives added get MORE negative. And −5 − 3 is −8, not −2: subtracting 3 walks 3 more steps left.

Explore: the chip calculator

Pick two numbers and an operation. Yellow chips are +1, red chips are −1. When adding, zero pairs cancel; when subtracting, if there are not enough chips to take away, the calculator first adds zero pairs (the dashed ones).

Explore: the number-line walker

Adding: start at a and walk b steps (right if positive, left if negative). Subtracting: turn it into adding the opposite, then walk. Multiplying: from 0, make |a| jumps of size b; if a is negative, flip to the other side of 0 at the end.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Supports Georgia 7.NR (add, subtract, multiply and divide rational numbers; subtraction as adding the additive inverse; number-line and zero-pair models; why a negative times a negative is positive). Suggested order: make addition and subtraction solid with the number line and zero pairs, use equal groups for positive × negative, then let the pattern in step 6 force negative × negative. Have students write their own pattern tables, e.g. (−3) × 2, (−3) × 1, (−3) × 0…, to check the rule. The chip calculator is ideal for problems like 2 − 5 and 3 − (−2) that need zero pairs first.
