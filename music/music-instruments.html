Instruments of the World

A violin and an erhu play the same note — A4, 440 vibrations a second — yet you can tell them apart instantly. Why? The secret is in the harmonics: every instrument has its own sound fingerprint, called timbre.

Your mission: tune a virtual string to target notes and find its harmonics; walk through a Western orchestra and a Chinese orchestra and ring the 2,400-year-old bells of Marquis Yi; then become a timbre detective who names instruments by ear alone.

Mission 1: the string lab (physics link)

A string’s frequency is f = (1 ÷ 2L) × √(T ÷ μ): L = length, T = tension, μ = mass per metre. Move the sliders, pluck, and tune to the target note (within ±10 cents). Then lightly touch the string at ½ or ⅓ — those are harmonics.

What if you halve the length?

The frequency doubles — one octave up. Pressing a finger or a fret just shortens the part of the string that vibrates.

What are harmonics?

A string vibrates as a whole AND in 2, 3, 4… parts at once, at 2×, 3×, 4× the basic frequency. The mix of these harmonics is what makes each instrument’s timbre.

Mission 2: walk through two orchestras

A Western orchestra has four families: strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion. A Chinese orchestra has four sections too: bowed strings, plucked strings, winds and percussion. Click an instrument to hear it and read its story. Explore 8 or more for stars.

National treasure: the bells of Marquis Yi of Zeng (about 433 BCE)

Dug up in 1978 in Suizhou, Hubei: 65 bells weighing over 2,500 kg in all, with all 12 half steps. The magic trick: one bell, two notes. Strike the middle of the front or the side and you get two different pitches, about a third apart. Click the middle (正鼓 zhènggǔ) or the side (侧鼓 cègǔ) of each bell and play the opening of 《茉莉花》 “Jasmine Flower”!

Note: bell pitches here are illustrative (each bell’s two notes a major or minor third apart), not measurements of the real bells.

Detective tipsListen to the attack first: plucked strings go “ping” and fade; bowed strings and winds can hold on. Then listen for vibrato: the erhu’s is wide. Brass has a bright buzz; the clarinet sounds hollow.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

US standards: Georgia Standards of Excellence, Middle School General Music — MSGM6–8.RE.1 (aurally distinguish ensembles, voices and instruments; recognize characteristics of diverse genres and cultures), MSGM6–8.CN.1 (principles shared by music and other disciplines — here acoustics/physics), MSGM6–8.CN.2 (compare music of world cultures, its functions, musicians and performance conditions). National Core Arts Standards: MU:Re7.2, MU:Cn11.0. Also connects to Georgia Grade 8 Physical Science S8P4 (waves).

China: Compulsory Education Arts Curriculum Standards (2022), Music, Stage 4 (Grades 7–9) — in appreciation, recognizing common Chinese and foreign instruments and their timbres, and the make-up of the Chinese national orchestra (the “cultural understanding” core competency). Also links to Grade 8 physics “sound phenomena” (pitch, loudness, timbre; frequency and vibration).
