Classical Chinese: one character, one world

“学而时习之，不亦说乎？” Here 说 doesn’t mean “speak” — it means “glad”. Classical Chinese (文言文 wényánwén) uses the same characters as today, but often with different meanings, and usually one character per word. How do you crack it?

Your mission: tap words to decode 《论语》 (the Analects of Confucius) and two stories from 《世说新语》, master the five translation moves, and learn to punctuate a text with no punctuation at all.

Mission 1: tap a word, understand the text

Every underlined word can be tapped: the panel shows its meaning in this sentence, its pronunciation and what kind of word it is (borrowed character, old-vs-new meaning, a noun used as a verb…). “Translation” shows each sentence in modern Chinese and English.

Four kinds of “trap” words

  • Borrowed characters (通假字): a similar-sounding character stands in for another. 说 = 悦 (glad).
  • Old vs new meaning (古今异义): 去 meant “leave”, not “go to”.
  • One word, many meanings (一词多义): 乐 = happy, or “to take joy in”.
  • Word-class shifts (词类活用): nouns acting as verbs etc. 饭 (rice) → “to eat”.
⚠️ Don’t mix up:In 吾日三省吾身, 三 means “many times”, not exactly three, and 省 is xǐng (“examine”), not shěng (“province”).

Mission 2: the five translation moves

Turning classical Chinese into modern Chinese takes just five moves. Meet them, then play: look at the highlighted part and pick the move it needs.

留

Keep

Names, places, titles and reign years stay as they are.

删

Delete

Drop filler words that have no meaning today.

补

Add

Add the missing subject or object.

换

Swap

Swap old words for modern ones (often one character → two).

调

Reorder

Put inverted sentences back into modern word order.

Tip 1: look for 曰 and 云 ▸

After 曰 (“said”) comes a pause, then the speech.

Tip 2: look for sentence-final particles ▸

也, 矣, 乎, 哉, 焉, 夫 usually end a sentence — break after them.

Tip 3: look for matching halves ▸

Classical writers loved balance: two halves with the same shape split in the middle.
