How Chinese characters grew up — and calligraphy

Over 3,300 years ago, a scribe in the Shang dynasty carved 马 (mǎ, “horse”) as a little horse with a mane and four legs. Today 马 takes just three strokes. How did the picture turn into a character?

Your mission: be a character detective — trace one character through 3,300 years, crack the four ways characters were built, write with the correct stroke order and meet the great calligraphers. Complete missions to climb from 书童 (page) to 状元 (top scholar)!

Mission 2: crack the building code (the “six ways”, 六书 liùshū)

Around 100 CE the scholar Xu Shen (许慎) listed six ways characters work. Four of them actually build characters — click each card to see how.

日 山
Pictographs (象形 xiàngxíng): draw the thing

日 (sun) began as a circle with a dot; 山 (mountain) as three peaks. These were the first characters — but there are only a few hundred.

上 本
Indicatives (指事 zhǐshì): point with a mark

Add a mark at the root of 木 (tree) and you get 本 (root); at the top, 末 (tip). 刃 (blade) is 刀 (knife) with a dot on its edge.

休 明
Compound ideas (会意 huìyì): put meanings together

A person (亻) leaning on a tree (木) = 休 (rest). Sun + moon = 明 (bright). One person following another = 从 (follow).

河 妈
Sound–meaning (形声 xíngshēng): half meaning, half sound

河 (river) = 氵 (water: the meaning part) + 可 (kě: the sound hint). Over 80% of modern characters are built this way!

Five scripts and the great calligraphers

Calligraphy (书法 shūfǎ) is the art of writing with a brush. There are five main scripts, each with its own personality. (The big characters below just name each script; real masterpieces are in the gallery.)

篆

Seal (篆书)

Round, even lines, like bent wire.

隶

Clerical (隶书)

Wide and flat; strokes start like a silkworm’s head and end like a swallow’s tail.

楷

Regular (楷书)

Every stroke clear — the “model” script we learn first.

行

Running (行书)

Strokes flow into each other — fast but readable.

草

Cursive (草书)

The wildest: whole characters in one sweep. Hard to read!

Wang Xizhi (王羲之) — the “Sage of Calligraphy” ▸

Eastern Jin dynasty. In 353 CE he wrote the Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Poems (《兰亭集序》), called “the best running script under heaven”. We only have Tang-dynasty copies — the original is said to have been buried with Emperor Taizong.

Yan Zhenqing & Liu Gongquan — “Yan’s sinew, Liu’s bone” ▸

Tang masters of regular script. Yan’s style (Duobao Pagoda Stele) is full and powerful; Liu’s (Xuanmi Pagoda Stele) is lean and upright. Yan’s grief-stricken Draft of a Requiem to My Nephew is “the second-best running script”.

Ouyang Xun, Zhang Xu, Huaisu ▸

Ouyang Xun’s regular script is strict and sharp (Jiuchenggong Liquan Ming). Zhang Xu and the monk Huaisu wrote “wild cursive” — nicknamed “Crazy Zhang and Drunk Su”.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:The main line is oracle bone → bronze → small seal → clerical → regular. Cursive and running scripts grew up alongside as “fast-writing” styles — they are not the last step after regular script.

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes

Standard: 《义务教育语文课程标准（2022年版）》, stage 4 (grades 7–9), “Literacy and writing”: recognise about 3,500 common characters and write about 3,000; write neat regular script and learn running-regular script; copy model calligraphy and appreciate its beauty. Also the task group “Accumulating and organising language”.

Textbook links: the primary-school unit 遨游汉字王国 (Grade 5, vol. 2) lays the groundwork; writing and calligraphy appreciation run through grades 7–9. Script history, the six ways and the calligraphers are background knowledge beyond the set texts.
