Tang poems: pictures that carry feelings

“A lone column of smoke stands straight over the great desert; the sun sets, round, over the long river.” Just ten characters — and you can see a whole desert sunset. How did Tang poets fit a painting and a feeling into a few words?

The secret is the 意象 (yìxiàng, “image”): a thing in the scene — the moon, a wild goose, a willow — that carries a feeling. Your mission: step into seven textbook poems, click the images in each picture, listen and read along, then hunt images in the game.

🌙 Moon = missing home or someone ▸

Everyone sees the same moon, so it links people far apart. “I send my worried heart to the bright moon.”

🪿 Wild goose = homesickness, letters ▸

Geese fly south in autumn and home in spring; legend says they carry letters.

🌿 Willow = parting ▸

柳 (liǔ) sounds like 留 (liú, “stay”), so friends gave willow twigs when parting.

🔥 Beacon fire & smoke = war, the frontier ▸

Beacon fires warned of attack. In frontier poems, smoke and desert show how vast and lonely the border was.

🌅 Setting sun, dusk = desolation, homesickness ▸

Dusk is when people go home — so travellers feel homesick most at sunset.

🐦 Cuckoo (子规) = sorrow ▸

Legend says the cuckoo cries until it bleeds; its call sounds like “better go home”.

Meet the poets

Open a card for the story.

Du Fu 杜甫 (712–770), “Sage of Poetry” ▸

A realist poet whose works recorded the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763), so they are called “history in verse”. Young Du Fu wrote the confident Gazing at Mount Tai; the older Du Fu wrote the sorrowful Spring View.

Li Bai 李白 (701–762), “Immortal of Poetry” ▸

A romantic poet of wild imagination. Legend: at the Yellow Crane Tower he wanted to write a poem, read Cui Hao’s and said, “The view is right here, but I can’t put it into words — Cui Hao’s poem is already up there!”

Wang Wei 王维 (701?–761), “Buddha of Poetry” ▸

Poet and painter. Su Shi said of him: “In his poems there are paintings; in his paintings there are poems.”

Bai Juyi 白居易 (772–846) ▸

Famous for plain, clear language — legend says he read new poems to an old woman to check she understood. As governor of Hangzhou he built a lake dyke and wrote many West Lake poems.

Cui Hao 崔颢 (?–754) ▸

One poem made him immortal. The Song critic Yan Yu said: “Of all Tang seven-character regulated verse, Cui Hao’s Yellow Crane Tower comes first.”

Wang Wan 王湾 (c. 693–751) ▸

His lines about the sun rising out of the night sea were so admired that the chief minister Zhang Yue wrote them up in the government hall as a model.
