Idioms, couplets and the Chinese year

Four characters can hold a 2,000-year-old story: a man carving a mark on a moving boat to find his lost sword, a farmer waiting by a tree stump for another rabbit to crash into it. The red paper couplets on Chinese doors follow strict rules of tone and matching. And the year is cut into 24 “solar terms” — in 2016 UNESCO listed them as world heritage.

Your mission: collect the stories behind famous 成语 (chéngyǔ, idioms), judge whether they are used correctly, match couplets with the tone rules, tour the traditional festivals and spin the 24-solar-term wheel. Complete missions to rise from 书童 (page boy) to 状元 (top scholar)!

Mission 3: the secret of couplets — tones and matching

A couplet (对联 duìlián) is a pair of lines that mirror each other: the same number of characters, matching word types and structure, and opposite tones. Tone rule of thumb: Mandarin 1st and 2nd tones are “level” (平 píng), 3rd and 4th are “oblique” (仄 zè). The first line usually ends on 仄, the second on 平. Characters marked 入 were “entering tones” in old Chinese and count as 仄.

⚠️ Don’t mix up:On a door, the first line (上联) goes on the RIGHT as you face it and the second on the left — old Chinese was read top-to-bottom, right-to-left. The horizontal scroll (横批 héngpī) goes across the top. To tell which line is first, check the last character: the one ending on 仄 is the first line.
